An AI adoption playbook

Six steps for putting an AI tool into an operational business so that people use it, drawn from how service changes actually landed in restaurants.

September 2026 · The playbook I would run. The mechanisms come from the safety reporting, learning platform and kiosk work at KFC South Pacific; none of those was an AI rollout, and that is the point: adoption is a service design problem before it is a technology one

Every adoption failure I have seen in a restaurant network had the same shape. The tool was installed, the tool worked, and the people whose day it was meant to change had no role, no training, no reason and no feedback. Kiosks reached half the network before anyone was rostered to stand next to one. A learning platform showed every course to every new starter. A reporting platform assumed people would log incidents themselves, and they told their manager instead. An AI tool will land the same way unless the human half is designed. This is the playbook for designing it.

1. Discovery: find the workflow, not the use case

Interview the people who do the work, in the place they do it, and map the workflow as it is, including the workarounds. The workaround gallery is the design brief: every paper form transcribed twice a day, every phantom entry made to get a run through, is where a tool can help or will be routed around. Choose the sample to test the business's theory of the problem, not to confirm it.

Output: a current-state blueprint with owners, fail points and the two or three moments where the tool would change what someone does.

2. Configure for the workflow, and name the interim gates

Set up the tool so it asks the person who knows. Move any question the user cannot answer to the person or step that can. Write the prompts, guardrails and review steps as configuration that can be versioned and audited, and label the interim workarounds as interim so nobody mistakes them for the design. Decide the tiers of autonomy up front: what the tool may draft, what it may write only through a validator, what a person always decides.

Output: a configuration document, a guardrails list with an owner each, and a rollback plan.

3. Train on the shift, not in the classroom

Two weeks of coaching on shift beats a module. Build the training around a huddle guide, a card the person carries, and short good-and-bad examples of the tool in use. Make the first use supervised and the first success visible. Make sure the platform can roster the role that supports the tool, because if the time and attendance system cannot see the role, the role does not exist.

Output: a training kit, a card, a first-shift checklist, and a named person on each site who has done it once already.

4. Champions who converge, not a mandate that imposes

Find the groups that have already invented a version of the practice and converge them into one standard rather than issuing a fifth. Give champions the recognition ladder, from daily practice to quarterly awards, and tie recognition to outcomes rather than to usage counts, because usage incentives produce usage, not value.

Output: a champion network with a cadence, a standard the champions co-wrote, and a recognition ladder.

5. Measure adoption honestly, and close the loop

Baseline before launch, with the numbers pulled from the system rather than typed in. Track leading measures that people control (was the review step done, was the gate passed, was the override used and why) beside lagging ones (tickets, corrections, time). Report the share you cannot attribute as a named gap. Tell the people who used the tool what changed because they did, every month, in the place they work.

Output: a KPI tree with baselines, a weekly readout during rollout, a monthly fixes-from-your-reports notice.

6. Decide: continue, expand, pause or stop

Pilot in a small number of sites with a control, for one reporting period, with the decision criteria written before the pilot starts. Confront the null results. Expect the novelty effect and say so. Then take the decision to a forum that can make it, with the evidence they can see.

Output: an after-action paper with the decision requested and the caveats stated.

What makes it an AI playbook

Steps two and five carry the parts that are specific to AI: the tiers of autonomy, the review gate that the tool cannot pass on its own, the audit trail of prompts and outputs, the rollback, and the measurement of how often a person corrected the tool. The other four steps are how any service change lands in a workforce that is young, busy and franchised, and skipping them is how AI rollouts fail.
