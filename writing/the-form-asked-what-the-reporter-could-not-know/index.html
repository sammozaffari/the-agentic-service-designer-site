The form asked what the reporter could not know

Two fields asked a person with their hand under the cold tap to predict the future. Help text would not have fixed that.

September 2026 · From the safety reporting work at KFC South Pacific, 2025 · The prototype

The incident form opened with a question about the outcome. First aid only, medical treatment with no lost time, lost time under a shift, lost time over a shift. A team member reporting a burn a minute after it happened cannot answer that. A burn that looks minor at ten in the morning can need treatment by four. Then came a question about whether the event was notifiable to the regulator, with a link to a compliance matrix. Area safety leads told us they asked the restaurant's safety champion which option to choose. Team members guessed.

The guesses did what guesses do. Reports were classified wrongly and sent back, newer managers struggled with the categories, and the data lost the meaning the safety team needed from it. The form was not hard to fill in. It was impossible to fill in correctly.

The fix everyone proposed first

Help text. A sentence under each field explaining the options, examples of what counts, maybe a tooltip on the regulator question. It is the obvious fix and it is the one we rejected, because explaining a question does not make it answerable. The categories were an industrial taxonomy applied to a kitchen, in an order that asked for the outcome before the event. A better-labelled wrong question is still the wrong question.

Mechanism first

The proposal asks what happened, in the reporter's own words: burn, cut, slip, hit by something. Then how, from a short list that fits the mechanism. Then where on the body, where in the restaurant, when. That is everything the reporter actually knows. Severity is classified by the manager after the welfare check, from the treatment that was actually needed. The regulator question moves to the manager's review, where the person answering it has the context and the training.

Two other things followed. One entry point, because the distinction between an incident and a hazard was itself a place people hesitated. And hazards became conditions only: the floor is wet, the exit is blocked. The live hazard list had contained events like verbal abuse, which belong in an incident report, and that mix was another reason people did not know which form to use.

Each form now asks the person who knows. The team member knows the mechanism. The manager knows the outcome. The safety team gets data that means what it says.

Which field in your form asks the user to predict the future, and who actually knows the answer?
