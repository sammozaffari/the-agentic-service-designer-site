The sampling decision

We chose the restaurants to test an explanation. The explanation failed, and the recommendations changed.

September 2026 · From the safety reporting work at KFC South Pacific, 2025 · The case

When the safety team asked why some restaurants reported far more incidents than others, the business already had an answer. Rougher suburbs, rougher safety culture. It was said often enough that it had become a fact, and a study designed to collect complaints would have confirmed it, because you can find rough stories anywhere you look for them.

So we designed the sample to test the explanation instead of to illustrate it. Eight restaurants: high-reporting and low-reporting, higher-crime and lower-crime suburbs, metropolitan and regional. If location explained reporting, the pattern would show up across that grid. If it did not, something else would have to.

What we found

Across the grid there was no difference that tracked the suburb. There was a difference that tracked the manager. Where a manager talked about safety, thanked people for reports and showed the reporting system on the first shift, reporting was strong regardless of where the restaurant was. Where none of that happened, reporting was thin, in a leafy suburb or otherwise.

That one finding reorganised the recommendations. The platform fixes that had been top of the list moved down. The cheapest lever, what the manager does on the first shift and every day after, moved up, because the research said it was also the strongest one. An onboarding checklist with a mandatory demonstration, a short template for the manager's own explanation of why reporting matters, and a daily check-in card became the quick wins. The system changes went into a later phase.

Why the design mattered more than the method

None of this needed a clever method. Thirteen interviews, a team survey and restaurant visits are ordinary tools. What made the difference was choosing the sites so the study could say no to the business's own theory. A convenience sample of the restaurants nearest the office would have produced a report about phones and forms, and the manager finding would have stayed invisible.

It also changed how the findings landed. A recommendation that contradicts the room is easier to accept when the room can see it was tested rather than asserted. The sample grid went on the first slide.

When did a sample surprise you, and what did it cost the recommendation you were about to make?
