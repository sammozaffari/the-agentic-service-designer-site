01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

Northgate Health, a mutual health insurer with roughly 40,000 members in the Benelux, had launched a telehealth benefit with delivery partner Meridia Telehealth — and almost nobody was getting through the front door. Only 45% of members who started enrollment finished, 23% of booked first consultations ended in no-shows, and the median member waited 26 days from first click to first consult. Over 28 weeks, a single lead service designer, amplified by an AI agent team, ran the complete arc from research to live pilot: 20 stakeholder interviews and a 400-member survey synthesized by a Claude Code research team into an evidence register; a current-state journey map and a fail-point-annotated blueprint; insight-grounded ideation producing a two-part concept (concierge onboarding plus a digital enrollment assistant); three rounds of progressive-fidelity prototyping with an OpenCode-built interactive prototype; and a GitHub issue-driven implementation culminating in a 12-week pilot with 5,000 members. Pilot results (illustrative): completion 45% to 76%, no-shows 23% to 7%, time-to-first-consult 26 to 9 days, NPS −8 to +31.

What you will learn:

  • How to run the full insight-to-implementation arc with a one-designer-plus-agents team without diluting method rigor.
  • How to encode research synthesis as an executable SOP for agents — prompts, grounding rules, and hallucination controls that hold up under GDPR and clinical-safety constraints.

02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

Northgate Health is a mid-sized mutual insurer covering approximately 40,000 members in Belgium and the Netherlands: older-skewing (31% over 65), loyal (median tenure 9 years), and digitally mixed — 68% of members use the portal monthly, but only 22% in the 75+ band. In 2024 Northgate contracted Meridia Telehealth, a 60-clinician platform, to offer video GP and specialist consultations as an included benefit. Eighteen months post-launch, adoption was stuck at 7% of the membership, member services was absorbing a steady stream of "how do I sign up?" calls, and the executive committee was questioning the €410,000 annual platform contract at renewal.

The challenge

Operational data told a story of a leaking funnel and an anxious membership. Of 28,500 members reached by launch campaigns in the trailing six months, 9,800 visited the enrollment page, 6,200 started, and only 2,790 completed — a 45% start-to-complete rate. Of the 1,674 who booked a first consult, 385 never attended (23% no-show). Member Effort Score sat at 4.1 out of 5, onboarding-cohort NPS was −8, and enrollment-related support calls ran at 61 per 100 enrollments — classic failure demand: contacts caused by the organization failing to make the service work the first time (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md). And the contract renewal decision was 30 weeks away.

Success criteria (agreed with the client)

Metric Baseline (illustrative) Pilot target
Start-to-complete enrollment rate 45% ≥ 70%
First-consult no-show rate 23% ≤ 10%
Median days, enrollment start to attended first consult 26 ≤ 12
Member Effort Score (1 = low, 5 = high) 4.1 ≤ 2.5
Onboarding cohort NPS −8 ≥ +25
Enrollment support calls per 100 enrollments 61 ≤ 25

Constraints

Budget €180,000 all-in; 28 weeks to a pilot readout before contract renewal; a 2011-era member master database with batch overnight sync; GDPR plus national health-data rules (special-category data, explicit consent, EU residency); and a non-negotiable rule that any triage or health-content change requires sign-off from Meridia's medical director.

Why service design

This was not a conversion-rate problem on one web page — the leaks spanned a paper letter, a portal, an identity service, a scheduling engine, a video platform, member services, and the member's own GP. That is precisely what service design exists for: an experience produced by a socio-technical system whose failures live below the line of visibility (See: 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md). Polaine, Løvlie, and Reason's arc — research to insight, ideas, prototyping, implementation — provided the governing structure (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md), and the Service Value Equation gave the economic frame: perceived value collapses when access costs (time, paperwork, anxiety) balloon, regardless of clinical quality (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md). For the series' operating model, see article 01.

03 · Approach

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research (weeks 1–3)

Goal. Understand why members leak out of the funnel and why those who get through still miss their first consult — across the whole service ecology (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md).

Method & SOP. Triangulated fieldwork (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md): 20 episodic interviews of 60–90 minutes (8 members, 4 Meridia clinicians, 3 back-office staff, 2 member-service agents, 2 GPs, 1 compliance officer) anchored in concrete past episodes; 4 shadowing days on the member-services floor and Meridia triage desk; a survey of 400 members (1,850 invited, 21.6% response); secondary data (24 months of claims, 6 months of enrollment event logs, ~41,000 events); and an artifact audit of all journey communications.

AI execution. Fieldwork stayed human — consent, rapport, and clinical sensitivity are not delegable. Everything after the recording was agent-amplified. Interviews were transcribed, pseudonymized (codes P-01…P-20), and processed by a Claude Code subagent team using the transcript-to-evidence prompt in Section 7: one agent extracted evidence items, one verified every quote verbatim against the transcript store (the engagement's key hallucination control), and one triaged survey open-text responses through the same schema. Result: 312 evidence items, each traceable to a participant code and line range, stored as research/evidence-register.jsonl (PR #14). OpenCode ran the funnel pipeline and generated the mock dataset (A7) in agent mode:

Human designer judgment. Recruitment quotas (oversampling 65+ and post-discharge members), the consent protocol, every live interview, and the decision to pursue "member anxiety" — not "member apathy" — after two early interviews contradicted the client's working theory.

Artifact produced. Research plan and interview guide (A6), evidence register (312 items), funnel baseline, artifact audit wall (23 artifacts, 9 with contradictory next steps).

4.2 Synthesis (weeks 3–4)

Goal. Convert 312 evidence items into insights that pass the acid test: each must change what Northgate designs (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md).

Method & SOP. Bottom-up affinity synthesis on a shared wall, per the data → themes → insights ladder (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md), executed as a hybrid session: the designer and two client stakeholders worked a physical wall while Claude Code maintained the digital twin.

AI execution. A Claude Code synthesis agent proposed the first-pass clustering (312 items → 47 clusters) under a hard rule that cluster names be active sentences, not nouns — "Members keep paper proof because the service gives them none," not "Communication." The humans merged and renamed freely; the agent re-propagated edits to the digital wall between breaks. Convergence: 47 → 11 themes → 7 insight cards in the standard three-part syntax (observed behavior + root cause + design implication). Codex then ran an adversarial challenge pass — a second opinion asked to attack, not agree:

Illustrative Codex host command — emitted pack; live case execution unverified.

TERMINAL
codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read synthesis/insight-cards.md and
research/evidence-register.jsonl. For each of the 7 insights: (1) check every
cited quote exists verbatim in the register, (2) argue the strongest
alternative explanation for the observed behavior, (3) flag any insight whose
design implication is not actually implied by the evidence. Be adversarial."

Codex killed one candidate insight ("members distrust video medicine" rested on one quote and contradicted survey data showing 71% willingness) and forced evidence upgrades on two others. The surviving 7 insights each cite ≥3 independent evidence items from ≥2 methods.

Human designer judgment. Naming insights is framing, and framing is political — the designer chose "anxious, not apathetic" for the headline insight because it redirected executive blame away from members, and accepted Codex's kill without sentiment.

Artifact produced. Seven insight cards; three load-bearing examples (illustrative quotes from fictional participants):

ID Insight (compressed) Evidence Implication
I-01 Members keep paper proof (photos, filed letters) because enrollment gives no completion evidence — "I took a photo of the letter in case they say I never signed up" (P-03, member) 31 items, 3 methods Status heartbeat at every milestone
I-02 62% of enrollment abandonment clusters at identity verification; capture fails under home lighting and glare — "it kept saying hold still, I held still for ten minutes" (P-11, member, 78) 44 items + event logs Assisted capture, manual review fallback, save-and-resume
I-05 No-shows are driven more by consult anxiety than forgetfulness — "I didn't know what it would be like, so I just didn't open the app" (P-06, member) 18 items + no-show reason coding Practice call + clinician intro card before the real consult

Five design principles followed: D1 One front door. D2 Never ask twice (pre-fill from the member record, with explicit consent). D3 Prove it works before it matters (tech check and practice call). D4 A human is one tap away. D5 Set expectations, then keep them. The insight set feeds the HMW bank (Section 4.4) and the traceability table (A9).

4.3 Mapping (weeks 4–6)

Goal. Build the two alignment artifacts everything else hangs on: the current-state journey map (what it is like to be the member) and the future-state service blueprint (how the organization will produce the new experience).

Method & SOP. Current-state journey mapping from tagged evidence — phases defined by member goals, never internal stages; pain points strictly separated from opportunities (See: 09-mapping-experiences/04-customer-journey-maps.md). Then future-state blueprinting from the validated journey spine, rehearsed column-by-column with operations and IT in a blueprint rehearsal (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/06-prototyping-services.md).

AI execution. Claude Code authored the mermaid diagrams as version-controlled code — journey map and blueprint lived as .md files in Git, diffed in PRs like software. During the blueprint rehearsal, every unanswerable question ("where does the concierge see that a practice call failed?") became a GitHub issue tagged blueprint-gap on the spot — 23 issues, each later resolved into a backlog ticket. Codex reviewed the blueprint PR adversarially and caught 4 genuine defects, including a missing exception path when identity-verification manual review exceeds 24 hours and an unstaffed discharge-to-concierge handoff. Both were fixed before pilot.

Human designer judgment. Choosing where the line of visibility sits for the concierge — backstage casework stays invisible, but the concierge-to-clinician handoff is deliberately visible ("I have briefed Dr. Aerts for you"). Also: refusing IT's request to split the blueprint per system, which would have re-siloed the artifact.

Artifacts produced. A1 journey map and A2 blueprint (Section 5), fail points F-01…F-06 annotated and carried into the traceability table (A9).

4.4 Ideation (weeks 6–8)

Goal. Convert insights into a coherent, commercially viable service concept — not a pile of idea fragments (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/05-developing-ideas.md).

Method & SOP. Structured HMW formulation from insight cards, then a half-day co-design workshop (11 participants: 3 members, 2 member-service agents, 1 Meridia clinician, ops, IT, compliance, designer) with strict divergence/convergence separation, brainwriting, clustering, concept sheets, and Desirability–Feasibility–Viability scoring.

AI execution. Claude Code prepared the HMW bank beforehand (5 primary HMWs, 14 sub-prompts, each traced to an insight ID) and acted as workshop scribe: transcribing idea sheets from photos between rounds, clustering 68 raw ideas into 9 themes live, and drafting the four concept sheets during lunch so the afternoon convergence session scored real artifacts, not memories. Example HMW chain: I-05 → "How might we make the first consult feel familiar before it happens?" → "…as familiar as a test call before a job interview?" A Business Model Canvas overlay pass exposed the key economic assumption: concierge staffing pays for itself if it halves no-shows, because each no-show wastes a €65 clinician slot and typically regenerates a €8.50 support call (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/08-service-design-and-business.md).

Human designer judgment. Facilitation, member safeguarding, and the convergence call: the designer insisted the concierge was the experience, not a fallback, citing I-03 and I-05.

Artifact produced. The selected concept, working name Guided Entry: (1) a digital enrollment assistant — conversational, pre-filled with consent, document check, tech-readiness check, practice-call booking; (2) an onboarding concierge — a named human guide who proactively contacts 65+ and post-discharge members on day 1 and follows up after the first consult. DFV scores 4.6 / 3.8 / 4.2 (illustrative).

4.5 Prototyping (weeks 8–11)

Goal. Test the riskiest assumptions at the lowest fidelity that answers the question: hypothesis first, prototype second (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/06-prototyping-methods.md).

Method & SOP. Progressive fidelity in three rounds, each with a written experiment canvas stating the learning question and pass/fail thresholds before anything was built (A6).

AI execution.

  • R1 — Desktop walkthrough + paper wireflows (2 days, 8 internal participants). Learning question: does the enrollment sequence have orphaned steps? Claude Code generated the wireflow screens as printable paper mockups. Outcome: 3 orphaned steps found (consent revocation, caregiver handoff, practice-call reschedule).
  • R2 — Interactive prototype + concierge roleplay (2 weeks). OpenCode built the clickable enrollment assistant in agent mode — static HTML/JS with stubbed identity and scheduling steps, seeded with the mock dataset so every hallway test used realistic names, conditions, and letter histories, never "Test User":

Illustrative OpenCode host command — emitted pack; live case execution unverified.

TERMINAL
opencode run --agent prototyper "Scaffold a static prototype of the Guided Entry
enrollment assistant in prototype/. Screens: welcome, consent + pre-fill,
document check, tech check, practice-call booking, confirmation with status
timeline. Seed with data/enrolment-funnel-sample.csv. No backend; stub async
steps with 800ms delays. Plain-language copy at CEFR B1 reading level."

Fourteen hallway tests (members from Northgate's advisory panel) followed; the designer observed while a Claude Code agent tagged observation logs nightly against the plan's success signals. Median task completion 12 minutes against a 15-minute threshold; the consent screen failed 4 of 14 tests and was rewritten twice.

  • R3 — Wizard of Oz concierge (3 weeks, 40 members). A live landing page offered concierge onboarding to two bounded cohorts; behind the curtain, two member-service agents manually performed pre-fill, scheduling, and reminder calls — testing the business model, not the software. Outcome: 85% accepted concierge help; concierge effort averaged 14 minutes per member; the five request types consuming 70% of concierge time became the pilot automation spec.

Human designer judgment. Setting pass/fail thresholds before tests (and holding them when a beloved screen failed), choosing safe cohorts for WoO exposure, and every interaction with elderly participants.

Artifacts produced. Prototype plan template (A6), three experiment canvases, observation logs, the working prototype, and a validated automation spec.

4.6 Implementation (weeks 11–26)

Goal. Turn the validated concept into a bounded live pilot — implementation as design continued, not handoff.

Method & SOP. Blueprint-to-backlog translation, frontline enablement via the capability–authority–motivation triad, and a pilot charter with explicit scale/iterate/kill gates (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/07-implementation.md, 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md).

AI execution. The blueprint was walked cell by cell and decomposed into 214 GitHub issues across four workstreams (IT 96, Operations 58, HR/training 27, Legal/compliance 33), each carrying its blueprint step ID for traceability (A9). Pull requests were the review gates: copy changes needed member-services sign-off; anything touching triage or health content required the clinical-safety label, enforced by a GitHub Action blocking merge without the medical director's review. The six concierge staff trained with investigative rehearsals scripted from R2/R3 edge cases — angry member, failed video, confused caregiver, out-of-scope medication question. Their incentive metric moved from average handling time to first-contact resolution plus attended-consult rate, the single change that aligned motivation with the new experience.

Pilot design: 5,000 invited members over 12 weeks, skewed to the hardest cohorts (1,800 members 65+, 300 post-discharge referrals). Rollback guardrails: revert to legacy enrollment within 4 hours if verification failure exceeded 10% for 48 hours; a €3,000 goodwill budget at concierge discretion. Daily standups reviewed a live dashboard; 11 issues were fixed in flight, including reminder SMS timing (24h → 48h + 3h) and a caregiver-consent wording fix.

Human designer judgment. Gate criteria negotiation with the executive sponsor, the clinical escalation protocol, and the weekly steering narrative — translating dashboard noise into decisions.

Artifacts produced. Pilot charter, 214-ticket backlog, SOP-as-code (A6), training scripts, live KPI dashboard.

4.7 Measurement (weeks 26–28)

Goal. Close the loop: triangulated measurement across experience, operations, and finance, feeding the scale decision (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md, 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md).

AI execution. OpenCode maintained the dashboard pipeline (nightly jobs reconciling enrollment events, consult attendance, support-call coding, and survey responses into one KPI table); Claude Code drafted the pilot evaluation report as a steering-group-reviewed PR, every number generated from the same query set — no hand-typed metrics. Gate decision: scale, with two conditions (extend concierge to the chronic-care cohort; re-audit the scorecard at 6 months).

06 · Use cases

Use cases

Use case A — Elderly member onboarding with a family caregiver

Scenario. Maria (persona 1), 74, receives the redesigned invite letter. Her daughter Elena, 52, lives 40 minutes away and handles "the computer things." What was designed. The letter offers two equal doors: QR to the assistant, or a phone number to the concierge. Maria calls; concierge Tom verifies her, and — because she asks for Elena — walks the caregiver-consent flow (form C2, signed by Maria with Elena witnessing by video). Elena completes the assistant on Maria's behalf in 11 minutes, every pre-filled field shown to Maria for confirmation. A practice call is booked for Saturday when Elena visits; the consult happens Tuesday; the GP receives the summary within 24 hours.

AI working session. The delegation flow was the hardest interaction in the prototype. OpenCode generated three consent-flow variants overnight; Claude Code reviewed copy for CEFR B1 readability and explicit-consent wording; Codex reviewed the model against GDPR Article 9 and flagged that the caregiver could see visit summaries by default — over-broad. Scope was narrowed: the caregiver can act on enrollment, not view clinical content, unless separately consented.

Outcome (illustrative). 65+ enrollment completion rose from 29% to 71%; 22% of 65+ pilot enrollments were caregiver-assisted; zero consent complaints; practice-call attendance 94% in this cohort.

Use case B — Referred specialist pathway

Scenario. A GP refers a member to teledermatology for a persistent skin condition. Before the redesign, the member received a paper referral letter and enrolled from scratch; median referral-to-consult was 21 days and 30% of referrals never converted.

What was designed. The GP's referral triggers a pre-filled enrollment: the member receives an SMS ("Dr. Peeters has referred you to a Northgate skin specialist — tap to confirm"), consents to referral data being used for pre-fill, uploads three guided photos of the skin area (the assistant checks focus and lighting inline — the I-02 fix applied to clinical images), and books a slot from a reserved teledermatology block. A Meridia nurse triages within 8 business hours.

AI working session. Claude Code drafted the SMS and photo-guidance copy from the design principles; a subagent then red-teamed it for phishing resemblance, producing a version anchored to the GP's name and practice — 13/14 hallway-test participants judged it legitimate versus 6/14 for v1. OpenCode stubbed the referral API using the mock dataset's gp_referral records.

Outcome (illustrative). Referral-to-consult median 21 → 6 days; referral conversion 70% → 88%; triage review 6 minutes per case.

Use case C — Post-discharge onboarding from the hospital partner

Scenario. Jonas (persona 2) is discharged after orthopaedic surgery. The old pathway: a paper leaflet with a URL; 12% enrolled within 72 hours.

What was designed. A discharge handshake: with Jonas's consent recorded at discharge, the hospital sends a structured referral to Northgate; the concierge calls within 24 hours, enrollment is pre-filled, and a video follow-up is booked inside 72 hours. The clinician receives the discharge summary; Jonas receives a plain-language "what to watch for" card (clinically approved, version-locked).

AI working session. GitHub issues drove the build: 33 of the 214 backlog tickets trace to this pathway. Codex reviewed referral data minimization and caught over-collection of full medication history in the draft schema — fixed before build. The R3 Wizard of Oz round surfaced that outreach calls placed 4–6pm converted best (working members answer after work) — a finding no stakeholder predicted.

Outcome (illustrative). 96% of consented discharge patients enrolled within 72h (vs 12%); median discharge-to-follow-up 2.4 days; 30-day unplanned post-discharge contacts down 18%.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Three SOPs were encoded for agent execution, each following the same pattern: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Transcript-to-evidence SOP (research). Output: JSONL evidence items. Acceptance: every quote verbatim-verifiable against the transcript store; every item carries participant code, role type, journey phase, and line range. Gate: human spot-check of 10% before merge. The prompt template:
NOTE
You are EVIDENCE-EXTRACTOR. Input: one pseudonymized interview transcript.
Extract discrete evidence items. For each item output EXACTLY:
- evidence_id: EV-<transcript>-<seq>
- participant_code, role_type (member|frontline|backstage|partner)
- journey_phase (discover|enroll|onboard|first_consult|ongoing|off_journey)
- type: verbatim_quote | observed_behavior | operational_fact | workaround
- text: the verbatim quote or factual statement (never paraphrase quotes)
- emotion_valence: -2..+2
- candidate_theme: 3-6 word cluster label
RULES: quote text must be copy-paste exact from the transcript; if unsure,
drop the item. Output JSONL. No invented content — every item must trace
to a transcript line range you include as source_lines.
  1. Research synthesis SOP (synthesis). Steps: load register → propose clusters (active-sentence names) → map items → draft insight cards (three-part syntax) → human wall session → absorb edits → regenerate. Acceptance: no bare-noun cluster names; every insight cites ≥3 items from ≥2 methods; orphans reported, never dropped. Gate: Codex adversarial pass plus the human session.
  2. Artifact-change SOP (implementation). Every change to a member-facing artifact is a PR containing: diff, linked blueprint step ID, readability check, GDPR/consent checklist, clinical label where relevant. Acceptance: CI green (mermaid, markdown, CSV schema) plus required reviewers. Gate: CODEOWNERS routing — member services for copy, medical director for clinical content.

The meta-SOP. The engagement itself ran research → synthesize → map → ideate → prototype → measure → deliver, with a hard gate between stages: no ideation before insights passed the acid test; no prototype before a written hypothesis; no pilot before a rehearsed blueprint with zero open blueprint-gap issues; no scale before pilot thresholds were met. Each gate is a GitHub milestone with required reviews — process enforced by tooling, not memory.

Guardrails.

  • Patient data privacy / GDPR. No real member data in any prompt, ever: agents worked on locally pseudonymized transcripts and synthetic fixed-seed mock data. Consent flows apply data minimization under DPIA review; all processing EU-resident.
  • Clinical safety. Anything touching triage, symptoms, or health content carries a clinical label enforced in CI; merge is blocked without the medical director's approval. Agents may not draft medical advice except as marked placeholder for clinical rewrite.
  • Hallucination control. Quote verification runs on every synthesis PR: a quoted string not found verbatim in the register fails the build. Insights without evidence IDs fail schema validation.
  • Human sign-off points. Interview conduct; insight naming; concept selection; consent-model changes; clinical content; pilot go/no-go; scale decision.

Failure modes observed. (1) Over-clustering: the first pass proposed 61 clusters with six near-duplicates; fixed by active-sentence names plus a similarity merge pass. (2) A confident but wrong survey read ("members prefer phone over video," drawn from an enrollment question, not a consult question) — caught by requiring every claim to carry the survey question ID. (3) Mermaid drift: early diagrams broke rendering; CI linting killed the class of error permanently.

Causal logic (Service Profit Chain). The chain ran as Heskett, Sasser, and Schlesinger describe (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md): internal service quality (concierge tools, aligned incentives, single case view) raised frontline capability; external service value rose chiefly through the denominator of the value equation — access costs collapsed (34 minutes median enrollment effort to 12, 26 days waiting to 9, anxiety addressed by the practice call); satisfaction and NPS followed; retention and reduced failure demand produced the economics. Estimated annualized effect at full scale (illustrative): failure-demand reduction ≈ €168k, recovered clinician slots ≈ €46k, retention uplift ≈ €90k contribution — roughly €300k/year against €180k implementation cost, payback inside 9 months, before the strategic effect on the €410k platform contract renewal (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/08-service-design-and-business.md).

KPI tree (abridged). North-star: members with an attended first consult within 14 days of interest. Drivers: enrollment completion (verify success, pre-fill acceptance), attendance (practice-call completion, reminder delivery), effort (screens, handoffs). Lagging: NPS, 90-day retention, failure-demand volume, cost-to-serve. Reviewed weekly in pilot; monthly in run-state.

09 · AI tool playbook

AI tool playbook

Claude Code — orchestration, research team, artifact authoring. The engagement's operating system: ran the parallel subagent research team (extraction, quote verification, survey triage), the synthesis digital wall, all mermaid authoring, HMW and concept-sheet drafting, observation-log tagging, and the pilot report. Excels at: structured extraction against a schema; consistency across dozens of files; 80%-there first drafts. Failed at: judging which insight would land politically; facilitation. Human took over: live research, naming, gates.

OpenCode — agent-mode pipelines and prototype build. Built the interactive prototype (R2) from a one-paragraph brief in about 40 minutes including three consent-screen iterations; ran the event-log funnel pipeline; generated the seeded mock dataset (A7); scaffolded the KPI dashboard jobs. Excels at: self-directed multi-file builds, realistic seed data, fast iteration. Failed at: copy tone (first-pass button labels read like a tax form — rewritten to CEFR B1) and method intent (it will happily build a screen the experiment does not need; the written hypothesis was the leash). Human took over: test design, thresholds, copy.

Codex — deep research and adversarial review. Second-opinion engine, run via codex exec --skip-git-repo-check with the artifact plus a specific concern, never the whole repo. Delivered the insight challenge pass (one insight killed), the blueprint review (4 real defects), the GDPR consent-model review (1 scope fix), and the referral data-minimization review (1 over-collection fix). Excels at: disagreeing productively — "be adversarial" is a load-bearing instruction. Failed at: occasionally flagged stated client constraints as defects (e.g., demanded real-time sync the 2011 member master cannot do); every finding was verified before action — reconcile by verifying, never by averaging.

GitHub — the system of record. Issues as work items (214 tickets with blueprint step IDs), PRs as review gates (CODEOWNERS; clinical label blocking), the board as phase tracker, milestones as stage gates. 10 · Lessons learned

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • The headline insight was an empathy correction, not a feature idea. "Anxious, not apathetic" reframed the engagement; the client's working theory (members do not want telehealth) would have produced a marketing campaign, and the survey alone would have confirmed it. Episodic interviewing broke the theory (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md).
  • Verbatim verification is the highest-ROI automation in AI-assisted research. One grep-style check caught 7 fabricated or misattributed quotes — every one plausible, every one wrong.
  • Agents cluster fast and name badly. Left alone, the synthesis agent produced noun-bucket clusters that would have quietly destroyed insight quality. The active-sentence naming rule, enforced in CI, fixed it in one line.
  • The Wizard of Oz round earned its budget three times. The 4–6pm calling window, the five-request automation spec, and the 14-minute concierge cost basis all came from WoO; none were discoverable in interviews or hallway tests.
  • Incentive alignment beat tooling. The concierge worked because the metric changed (handling time → attended-consult rate). Two earlier internal attempts at "better onboarding comms" failed on this rock (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/07-implementation.md).
  • Codex is a reviewer, not an oracle. Two of its six blueprint findings were contextually wrong; treat it as adversarial counsel whose claims require verification, not as a verdict.
  • Done differently next time: baseline the Good Services scorecard in week 1 (scored in week 5, losing comparative precision), and involve the medical director at ideation, not prototyping — one clinical rework cycle was avoidable.
