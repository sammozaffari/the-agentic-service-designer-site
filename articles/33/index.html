33 — The Virtual Ward at Capacity: Scaling Telehealth without Breaking the Promise

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Halo Care is a fictional composite telehealth provider; no actual patient, clinician, or clinical-result data appears. All wait times, satisfaction, and outcome figures are illustrative and machine-checked by the platform fact-check layer. The design work was AI-first, with clinical safety as a hard gate: no design decision overrides a clinical-governance decision, and the platform records that boundary.

1. Executive summary

A digital-first telehealth provider grew from 400,000 to 1.4 million active patients in two years by winning region after region—and the service began to break at the seams: virtual waits stretched to 22 minutes, did-not-attend rates hit 18%, clinicians reported burnout, and first-visit resolution fell to 61%. A 20-week AI-first engagement re-architected the care journey at scale: an evidence-based triage ladder, same-day carve-outs, a warm-handoff protocol, and a clinician-facing context service that turned "retell the story" into "continue the story." Results: virtual wait 22→6 minutes, DNA 18%→7%, first-visit resolution 61%→78%, clinician eNPS −10→+25, patient NPS 31→58.

What you will learn

  • How to scale a healthcare experience without scaling its failures—failure-demand thinking (book 19) applied to triage.
  • Why clinician experience is a clinical-safety variable, not a soft measure (books 19, 20).
  • How a design platform encodes safety gates so that agents cannot silently change scope.

2. The brief

Halo Care operates across 12 regions, providing same/next-day virtual GP consultations and a virtual ward for chronic patients monitored at home. Market position: category leader on speed across the first three regions; the brand promise is "a clinician when you need one." That promise was failing under load: peak-hour waits of 22 minutes, 18% of booked slots unused (patients giving up or double-booking elsewhere), and clinicians carrying 11 concurrent cases with no context handover between shifts.

The challenge was structural: the service had been designed for a boutique model (a fixed clinical cohort, generous staffing, appointment-first). Scaling meant either doubling clinical cost or redesigning demand. The leadership asked for the latter, with safety non-negotiable.

Success criteria (illustrative): virtual wait ≤ 8 min at peak; DNA ≤ 10%; first-visit resolution ≥ 75%; clinician eNPS ≥ +15; patient NPS ≥ +50; no safety-event trend increase.

Constraints: regulatory clinical-governance sign-offs on every route change; data protection on all patient data; union-like clinician scheduling protections; a fixed clinical budget cap (staff hours were a hard constraint); and the clinical liability that all redesign must be reversible per-region.

Why service design: healthcare is a high-stakes service system (book 19), the internal customer—clinicians—determines external quality (book 20), and at scale the difference between a good and a broken service shows up in hours, not years (book 03). Also the rare case where convenience and clinical quality are the same metric: faster appropriate access, less queue-abandonment, less crowding.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Discover (weeks 1–6)

Goal. Quantify where demand and capacity collide, and why patients drop out of the queue.

Method & SOP. Mixed methods (book 18/04): 18 months of operational data (appointments, wait, DNAs, resolution), 41 patient interviews, 4 clinician shadow days, and a clinician workload diary. SOP steps: plan → gather → tag evidence → store.

AI execution. Researcher executed contextual-inquiry and transcript-mining. OpenCode agent-mode normalized 4.2 million appointment-level records into an evidence-ready demand model and produced the first triage simulation. The platform's evidence register grew to 1,983 tagged rows. Codex adversarially reviewed the demand model and found peak modeling was understated: the median-only view hid a 2.9× spike on Monday mornings and entry-day surges in new regions (the "onboarding cliff"). GitHub tracked the research as issues, each closing with evidence links.

Human designer judgment. The designer pushed for clinician shadow days over convenience interviews, set the safety framing with the clinical-governance lead before any design work, and excluded two interview transcripts that praised the service but added no diagnostic information (selection for insight, not volume).

Artifact produced. Research plan, evidence register (1,983 rows), demand model with regional variance (A7/A8), and the "queue, wait, and yield" anatomy: three distinct waits (to triage, to clinician, between care steps) that needed different fixes.

4.2 Define (weeks 7–9)

Goal. Define the triage ladder and the care journeys it feeds, with clinical governance co-signing each step.

Method & SOP. Affinity synthesis (09/04) and journey mapping from the evidence register (platform journey-from-transcripts); the demand framing followed failure-demand logic (book 19: the service's own complexity generates new demand). SOP steps: cluster → frame → ladder → co-sign.

AI execution. Synthesizer produced 17 insight statements; Mapper ran journey-from-transcripts to produce three current-state journeys (urgent, routine, chronic-monitoring). The Ideator drafted the triage ladder—four demand classes with matching access promises—using the section's evidence. The Critic vetoed a ladder entry (telemedicine for a symptom class the evidence did not support as routine-safe) with a review-veto-log artifact; clinical governance upheld the veto. The resulting ladder was co-signed by the clinical lead and recorded as a phase-gate-decision.

Human designer judgment. The designer framed the reframe: "we do not schedule visits; we deliver access matched to acuity." The clinician-burnout evidence was presented as a safety issue, not an HR issue (book 19/20 argument), which changed the client's willingness to redesign clinician workflows.

Artifact produced. Insight set, triage ladder (A6 template), three current-state journeys, persona set (A5).

4.3 Develop (weeks 10–16)

Goal. Prototype the four service designs: access ladder, warm handoff, clinician context service, and virtual-ward escalation path.

Method & SOP. Structured ideation (book 17), service blueprinting (09/05), storyboard walkthroughs (book 17), and continuous clinical-governance review (a gate, not an audit at the end). SOP steps: ideate → prototype → blueprint → governance-review → iterate.

AI execution. Ideator created 44 concepts from 8 HMWs; NUF filtered to 16; Prototyper storyboarded four flagship designs. Mapper ran service-blueprinting and evidence-gated-blueprint; the Critic gated the blueprint at four points. Codex adversarially stress-tested the warm-handoff design with shift-change simulations and found the 30-second handoff would become 90 seconds at scale, breaking the capacity model; the design was revised to a structured 60-second protocol with a decision-tree. OpenCode generated the synthetic scheduling dataset used to validate capacity neutrality (A7).

Human designer judgment. The designer made the wait-time promise (8 minutes) stick against marketing's "as fast as possible," insisted the clinician context service respect patient consent for data reuse, and chaired the governance reviews where clinical and design languages met.

Artifact produced. Care blueprints (A2), triage ladder final, storyboards, context-service spec, traceability table (A9).

4.4 Deliver (weeks 17–20)

Goal. Roll out in two pilot regions with continuous monitoring and a safety-first stop rule.

Method & SOP. Pilot + test-report discipline (book 17), service quality monitoring (book 19/04), KPI tree (book 20/05). SOP steps: pilot plan → launch → monitor → gate → expand.

AI execution. Measurer built the KPI tree from the four success criteria down to operational telemetry; the platform ran the fact-check at every gate. Presenter rendered the steering pack; Librarian assembled the rollout handbook. GitHub Actions validated artifacts (schema, evidence, mermaid) before governance review. The region-by-region rollout decision rule—expand only when a region holds the target for 4 consecutive weeks—was encoded as a measurable, and the platform flagged the second pilot region at week 3 for a DNA spike (9.4%, still under target but anomalous); the designer investigated and found a local event, not a service regression.

Human designer judgment. The designer personally ran the incident review (a mis-triage claim that turned out unfounded but consumed two days), set the stop rule with the clinical lead, and decided the rollout order (two contrasting regions, not the two easiest).

Artifact produced. KPI tree, rollout report, review-veto-log (2 vetoes), handover pack.

6. Use cases

6.1 The 6:40 am spike. Monday-peak urgent requests were routed to a generic queue. The ladder routed symptom-defined urgent cases to urgent-tracked slots; capacity rebalanced across regions. Outcome: peak wait 22→8 min in pilot 1, DNA down 8 points. AI session: Measurer's capacity simulation + Codex's peak-model correction.

6.2 The locum's first call. P3 opened the context card: diagnosis, plan, open items, last contact. First-visit resolution for locum-led visits rose from 61% to 76%. AI session: Researcher's workflow diary + Prototyper's storyboard of the 60-second protocol; the 60s-vs-90s scale correction was Codex's.

6.3 The virtual ward escalation. P2's escalation path was ambiguous (nobody owned the watch-and-wait gap). The escalation-trigger prototype codified thresholds and owning functions; readmission (30-day) moved from 12% to 9% in cohort terms. AI session: Mapper's escalation blueprint + Critic's veto of an over-broadened threshold that would have flooded urgent slots.

7. SOPs for AI

Encoding pattern. Same meta-SOP. Skills: contextual-inquiry, transcript-mining, affinity-diagramming, journey-from-transcripts, behavioural-personas, service-blueprinting, evidence-gated-blueprint, good-services-audit, ecosystem-value-mapping.

Guardrails. Safety: the clinical-governance sign-off is an artifact, and the platform refuses to ship a phase whose governance artifact is missing (a hard gate, not a convention). Data privacy: patient data never entered prompts; OpenCode handled only de-identified extracts, and the platform enforced this by refusing data files outside the project store with a consent matrix. Acceptance: every KPI claim carries an evidence reference; the fact-check re-gates at each milestone.

Failure modes. (1) A generated "Monday spike" claim was premature-published with median-only data; fact-check + Codex corrected the statistics. (2) The warm-handoff storyboard overpromised time; the model caught it, but the designer had to carry the schedule consequence into governance. (3) One critique pass suggested automating a clinical exclusion the governance lead had explicitly reserved for humans; the veto log shows the boundary held.

Causal logic (book 20): internal service quality (context card, slots, handoffs) → external service value (fast appropriate access, continuity) → outcomes (resolution, readmission, NPS, clinician retention). Safety is the first KPI; the others only count if it holds.

9. AI tool playbook

Claude Code — agent team executing skills, artifact authoring, fact-check. Excelled at: turning a 1,983-row register into a defensible ladder; the context-card spec. Failed at: distinguishing administrative burden from clinical risk in the governance review—the designer chaired. OpenCode — data engineering: 4.2M-row normalization, demand simulation, scheduling datasets. Excelled at volume; schema-gated. Codex — adversarial review: peak modeling, handoff timing physics, ladder safety edges. Its "onboarding cliff" finding changed the rollout plan. GitHub — issues/PRs/Actions gates; made the two governance vetoes auditable and the rollout stop-rule mechanical. SDAP — the integrity layer: kinds, evidence refs, governance artifacts, deterministic fact-check.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  1. Median reporting hid the Monday spike; always model the demand distribution, not its center.
  2. Clinician burnout is a clinical-safety metric (books 19/20); presenting it that way unlocked redesign the HR framing never would.
  3. Governance must sit on the design path, not after it; co-signing artifacts were the mechanism.
  4. The 60-second handoff failed the scale test in simulation—prototype timing with load, not ideal conditions.
  5. A stop rule encoded in the platform beats a steering-committee promise: pilot 2's DNA anomaly was caught by the rule, then explained.
  6. Consent boundaries are design constraints; the context service is smaller because of them, and better.
