01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

Lumen EdTech, a B2B school platform serving 240 schools across the Netherlands, Belgium, and Germany, had a support problem that was really a mental-model problem. Of 12,000 tickets per quarter, 68% were comprehension failures: parents could not find grades, teachers could not find grading drafts, students missed deadlines buried in a reverse-chronological feed. The platform was engineered around courses and classes, while users thought by child, by grading sitting, and by due date. Over 24 weeks, one lead service designer and an AI agent team extracted those models, made the misalignment explicit, and realigned the product as a unified Communication Hub. Pilot result in 12 schools: support volume down 46%, comprehension tickets down 69%, and flagship task success up 36–46 points.

02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

Lumen EdTech is a mid-sized European edtech vendor headquartered in Utrecht: 310 staff, €38M ARR, and a platform spanning gradebook, assignments, attendance, timetabling, and parent messaging. Its customers are schools; its users are 64,000 teachers, 1.4M students, and just over a million parents. Engineering maturity is high; research maturity is not. Support is a 22-agent team handling ~12,000 tickets a quarter at €7.20 loaded cost per ticket.

The challenge

The support queue was the symptom; the disease was conceptual. One quarter of tickets showed 68% of volume traced to three comprehension failures rather than defects: parents hunting grades across class pages (3,720 tickets), teachers losing grading drafts they believed were saved (2,640), and students missing deadlines buried in a reverse-chronological feed (1,800). Parent NPS sat at −4; teacher weekly active gradebook use at 61%; two municipal districts (9 schools) had signalled non-renewal. The executive theory was "the UI is dated; reskin it." Research showed the UI was faithfully rendering the wrong model of the world (See: 09-mapping-experiences/06-other-alignment-diagrams.md).

Success criteria (agreed with the client)

Metric Baseline (illustrative) Pilot target
Support tickets per quarter 12,000 ≤ 7,800
Comprehension tickets (three categories) 8,160/quarter ≤ 3,300/quarter
Parent task success: "what did my child do today" 42% ≥ 80%
Teacher task success: "resume yesterday's grading" 46% ≥ 85%
Student task success: "everything due this week, in order" 58% ≥ 90%
Parent "I know what my child did today" CSAT (1–5) 2.6 ≥ 3.8
Teacher weekly active gradebook use 61% ≥ 75%

Constraints

Budget €220,000 all-in; 24 weeks to a pilot readout before two district renewal decisions; the immutable school calendar; GDPR plus minors' data constraints (parental consent for student sessions, no student PII in prompts, EU residency); and a legacy content model in which grade history is append-only, constraining how drafts can be restructured.

Why service design

Lumen's problem was structural, not cosmetic. Kalbach's diagram-selection logic points straight at the mental model diagram for "durable human purposes vs. feature fit" (See: 09-mapping-experiences/06-other-alignment-diagrams.md). The engagement therefore anchored on Indi Young's method, supported by representational synthesis (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/05-representational-and-synthesis-methods.md) and Kumar's observation → finding → insight ladder (See: 17-101-design-methods/05-mode-4-frame-insights.md). Journey and story methods then translated the realigned model into time (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/04-understanding-the-journey.md, 10-the-users-journey/01-why-story.md).

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research: capturing actions, thoughts, and feelings (weeks 1–6)

Goal. Gather empirical evidence of how each actor group actually thinks the service works — not how stakeholders assume they do (See: 09-mapping-experiences/03-understanding-the-user.md).

Method & SOP. Triangulated fieldwork captured actions, thoughts, and feelings: 100 critical-incident interviews (60 teachers, 40 parents), nine days of classroom and staffroom shadowing across six schools, 24 moderated baseline usability sessions on the flagship tasks, and the full quarter's 12,000 support tickets as the quantitative backbone. A week-1 assumption-mapping workshop surfaced internal folklore into an assumptions register, every item tagged [ASSUMP] until verified or killed (See: 09-mapping-experiences/03-understanding-the-user.md).

AI execution. Interviews stayed human — consent, rapport, and the ethics of researching around minors are not delegable. Everything after the recording was agent-amplified. Transcripts were pseudonymized (T-01…T-60, P-01…P-40) and processed by a Claude Code subagent team under the extraction SOP in Section 7: one agent extracted evidence items, one classified each under the strict belief/question/task taxonomy, one verified every quote verbatim against the transcript store. Result: 1,180 evidence items in research/evidence-register.jsonl (PR #31). OpenCode ran the ticket pipeline in agent mode:

Illustrative OpenCode host command — emitted pack; live case execution unverified.

TERMINAL
opencode run --agent synthesizer "Classify analysis/tickets_q1.csv (12000 rows) into
the taxonomy in analysis/ticket-taxonomy.md. Output ticket_id, topic,
actor_guess, confidence. Rows under 0.7 confidence go to review_queue.csv.
Do not invent topics. Print category counts."

The support lead human-coded a 400-ticket sample; the pipeline's precision against it was 94%, and 1,900 low-confidence rows went through human review before the category counts were accepted (A8 pie).

Human designer judgment. Recruitment quotas (oversampling secondary-school parents and 15+-year-tenure teachers, the groups the folklore least described), the consent protocol, every live session, and the decision to research students through usability sessions and shadowing rather than interviews — parental-consent logistics for 40 student interviews would have consumed three weeks; observed behavior plus card sorts answered the mental-model questions at lower risk.

Artifact produced. Research plan and interview guide (A6), evidence register (1,180 items), assumption register (23 assumptions; 17 killed by data), ticket category baseline (A8).

4.2 Mental-model extraction: beliefs, questions, tasks per actor (weeks 5–9)

Goal. Convert 1,180 evidence items into three durable actor mental models — the demand side of the alignment diagram (See: 09-mapping-experiences/06-other-alignment-diagrams.md).

Method & SOP. The Indi Young method: extract granular user-voice statements from task-focused interviews, separating beliefs (claims users treat as true about how the system or school works), questions (recurring information needs), and tasks (intended actions); affinity-cluster tasks into towers; group towers into mental spaces. The taxonomy discipline is the method: a belief is not a task, and conflating them produces a collage, not a model. Here, "beliefs, questions, tasks" is a deliberate AI-extraction adaptation of Young's canonical "tasks, thoughts, feelings" language so agents keep cognitive claims, information needs, and intended actions cleanly separated.

AI execution. The Claude Code team structured 639 model elements from the register: 96 beliefs, 212 questions, 331 tasks, each carrying evidence IDs and transcript line ranges — no element without evidence. Tower clustering used the active-sentence naming rule ("Parents assemble one picture per child by hand," not "Grades"). Codex then ran the adversarial taxonomy audit:

Illustrative Codex host command — emitted pack; live case execution unverified.

TERMINAL
codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read research/evidence-register.jsonl and
models/mental-model-items.jsonl. (1) Flag every item whose type violates the
taxonomy: a belief must be a falsifiable claim about how things work, a
question an information need, a task an intended action. (2) Flag any belief
supported by fewer than 2 independent participants. (3) Flag any statement
phrased in system language rather than user voice. Be adversarial."

The audit caught 41 misclassified items (3.5% — mostly observed behaviors filed as beliefs, e.g., "teacher opens the gradebook at night" is a behavior, not a belief) and forced evidence upgrades on six beliefs. Every surviving belief cites ≥2 independent participants; headline beliefs cite ≥5.

Human designer judgment. Naming the mental spaces (framing is political — "Act when needed" frames parents as partners, which the parent council noticed), the segment call below, and accepting Codex's demotions without sentiment.

Guardrail in action — segment, never average. The first pass produced a single parent model. Codex's challenge pass flagged that the by-child belief was weakest among German secondary (Gymnasium) parents, who think partly in Fächer (subjects) because report structures are subject-based. The model was split: primary-school parents (pure by-child), NL/BE secondary parents (by-child with subject drill-down), DE secondary parents (by-child summary, subject-primary detail). One averaged model would have been wrong in two directions — the engagement's most-cited methodological lesson.

Artifacts produced. Three actor mental models (Section 5, M1–M3), segment variants, and the belief register feeding the gap inventory.

4.3 Alignment diagnosis: card sorts and the gap inventory (weeks 8–11)

Goal. Quantify the divergence between how users group the world and how the platform structures it, and enumerate the gaps as an evidence-linked inventory.

Method & SOP. Open card sort, 40 participants (16 parents, 14 teachers, 10 students), 42 content cards, instruction: "group these the way you would expect to find them when you open the app." Analysis: pairwise normalized distance (1 − share of participants co-grouping each pair) → average-linkage hierarchical clustering → cut at 0.40 → clusters compared against the platform's information architecture. Then the gap inventory: overlay the three actor models on the documented system content model and enumerate every divergence as a pair — user belief vs. system truth — classified by severity using ticket volume and task-failure rates (See: 17-101-design-methods/05-mode-4-frame-insights.md).

AI execution. OpenCode ran the full card-sort SOP-as-code (Section 7), producing the distance matrix (A7) and cluster report deterministically under a fixed seed; Claude Code rendered the dendrogram (A8) as version-controlled mermaid and drafted the gap inventory with per-gap evidence citations. Codex adversarially attacked the headline gap:

Illustrative Codex host command — emitted pack; live case execution unverified.

TERMINAL
codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read models/parent-mental-model.md and
models/system-content-model.md. Argue against the claim that parents organize
by child rather than by course, using only research/evidence-register.jsonl
and analysis/ticket-topics.csv. If the claim survives, name the weakest
segment where it holds."

The claim survived — strengthened, in fact: ticket text showed parents repeatedly writing "my son/daughter" while navigating screens named after courses. But Codex's segmentation finding (above) and one genuine catch — the draft-loss gap was partly a concurrent-tab engineering defect, not purely a model gap — both reshaped the final inventory.

Human designer judgment. Severity classification (ticket volume is demand; task failure is harm; harm weighted higher for students, because a missed deadline is a real-world consequence), and the decision to publish the misalignment diagram company-wide — an uncomfortable artifact, framed as "the platform is well-built for a world users don't live in."

Artifact produced. The misalignment diagram (Section 5, M4) with headline gaps X, Y, Z, plus minor gaps G4 and G5. The alignment-metrics baseline (A6) put model-match at 38% (parents), 52% (teachers), 57% (students).

4.4 Designing the aligned architecture: the Communication Hub (weeks 11–14)

Goal. Realign the product structure to a coherent mental model — not three models, but one hub that presents itself by-child to parents, by-sitting to teachers, and by-due-date to students over a shared event backbone.

Method & SOP. Content-model realignment expressed as engineering artifacts, then blueprinting from the validated actor spines (See: 09-mapping-experiences/02-the-anatomy-of-experience-maps.md), and per-actor story spines written before any screen was discussed (See: 10-the-users-journey/04-storymapping-in-practice.md). The parent spine: Every day, Sabine wonders what her children actually did at school. One day, the portal starts answering that question per child. Because of that, she stops hunting across eleven class pages. Until finally, "how was school" becomes a conversation instead of an interrogation.

AI execution. Each content-model change was drafted by Claude Code as a PR against platform/content-model/ using the CMC template (A6). Seven PRs (CMC-01…07) merged after architecture review, including CMC-02 (child aggregation), CMC-04 (GradingSession with explicit states), and CMC-05 (due-date-primary feed ordering). GitHub Actions enforced the artifact contract: mermaid lint, markdown lint, CSV schema, quote verification, and the belief-evidence check. Codex reviewed migration risk on every CMC and caught one load-bearing issue: CMC-04's first draft allowed silent draft merging across concurrent tabs and was rewritten with optimistic locking plus a visible conflict warning before build.

Human designer judgment. The architecture decision that the hub is one service with three presentations, not three portals — splitting would have re-siloed the data and recreated G4; and the sequencing call that CMC-04 (teacher drafts) ships first, because teacher trust was the engagement's most fragile asset.

Artifacts produced. CMC-01…07, the future-state blueprint (A2), per-actor story spines, and the traceability table (A9).

4.5 Pilot & measurement (weeks 15–24)

Goal. Prove the realignment moves alignment metrics and failure demand in the real environment — a bounded pilot with explicit gates.

Method & SOP. Twelve schools (NL 7, BE 3, DE 2 — deliberately including Gymnasium-segment parents), 1,050 teachers, 21,000 students, 16,400 parent accounts, eight weeks. Measurement triangulated: telemetry (model-match recomputed weekly from first-click paths), 24 moderated pilot usability sessions mirroring baseline, the weekly ticket pipeline, and CSAT/NPS instruments unchanged for comparability (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/04-understanding-the-journey.md, 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md).

AI execution. OpenCode maintained the nightly metrics pipeline (events → model-match, task proxies, ticket joins) with every dashboard number generated from the same query set; Claude Code drafted the pilot report as a steering-reviewed PR; GitHub tracked the 87 implementation issues, each carrying its blueprint cell ID.

Human designer judgment. Gate negotiation with the two at-risk districts (they demanded parent-facing evidence, not engineering metrics — model-match was translated into "parents find the answer in under two minutes"), the consent posture for student telemetry (aggregated, k-anonymized, DPO-signed), and the go/iterate/kill call: go, with one condition — notification defaults revisited after G4's digest initially raised notification-configuration questions (1,080 → 1,260 tickets), a known and accepted transition cost.

Artifact produced. Alignment metrics dashboard, pilot evaluation report, and the before/after table in Section 8.

Taxonomy rules (enforced by Codex audit + CI): a belief is a falsifiable claim about how the system or school works that the user treats as true; a question is a recurring information need; a task is an intended action. Behaviors observed but not verbalized are evidence items, never beliefs. Acceptance: every belief ≥2 independent participants; headline beliefs ≥5; every quote verbatim-verifiable.

Template 2 — Card-sort protocol + analysis SOP

NOTE
CARD SORT PROTOCOL — LUM-NL-01 — v2 — 60 min per participant
1. Participants : 40 (16 parents, 14 teachers, 10 students), recruited per segment quotas
2. Cards        : 42 content cards; one concept per card; no system jargon on card faces
3. Instruction  : "Group these the way you would expect to find them when you open the app.
                   Name your groups in your own words."
4. Rules        : open sort; unlimited groups; think-aloud; no interviewer suggestions
5. Capture      : group membership matrix per participant + group names + think-aloud notes
ANALYSIS SOP (deterministic, seeded):
  S1 build co-grouping matrix: share of participants co-grouping each card pair
  S2 normalized distance = 1 - share; write CSV per data contract (A7)
  S3 average-linkage hierarchical clustering; cut height 0.40
  S4 label clusters with active-sentence names; compare against platform IA
  S5 acceptance: every cluster >= 3 cards or flagged as singleton; merge order printed;
     report lists cards whose placement conflicts with the platform model

Template 3 — Mental-model mapping guide

NOTE
MENTAL-MODEL MAPPING GUIDE — actor: [parent|teacher|student] — segment: [ ]
1. Load the evidence register filtered to this actor and segment
2. Extract beliefs / questions / tasks under the v1 schema (template 1)
3. Cluster tasks into towers; name towers as active sentences
4. Group towers into 3-5 mental spaces; name spaces from user goals, never features
5. Draw the line of alignment; place platform offerings beneath the towers they serve
6. Mark: towers with no offering (unmet need), offerings under no tower (bloat),
   and beliefs the platform contradicts (misalignment candidates)
7. Cross-check every misalignment candidate against ticket volume and task failure
8. Publish with evidence IDs; version in Git; review gate = taxonomy audit + human read

06 · Use cases

Use cases

Use case A — The parent portal, realigned by child

Scenario. Sabine (persona 1), 6:40 pm, between train and dinner. Old world: she opens the portal, picks her daughter, faces 11 class pages, opens four, finds marks posted last Thursday with no test date, cannot tell whether "nothing new" means "fine" or "not entered," and emails the mentor — one of 3,720 such escalations that quarter.

What was designed. The child card: one today view per child aggregating across courses — attendance mark, work set and returned, marks with formative/summative labels, and an explicit "all events reported" closure line (the G4 fix: silence is rendered, not implied). A daily digest per child at 17:30, quiet hours respected. The parent mental model became the information architecture; courses still exist one tap down for the DE subject-primary segment.

AI working session. Claude Code drafted the digest copy from the design principles and the parent belief register; a subagent red-teamed it for false reassurance (a digest must never imply "all is well" — only "all events are reported"), producing the closure-line wording. Codex reviewed the aggregation model against G5 and forced the formative/summative label into the digest, citing B-P-07. OpenCode stubbed the child-aggregate endpoint against the mock dataset for hallway tests.

Outcome (illustrative). Parent flagship task success 42% → 88%; median time-to-answer 6.5 → 1.8 minutes; missing-grade tickets −71%; mentor reassurance contacts −44% in pilot schools; Sabine-segment model-match 38% → 89%.

Use case B — The teacher's lost draft: "where did my grading go"

Scenario. Jeroen (persona 2) grades 3B's algebra test on Sunday evening, enters 22 of 30 marks, closes the laptop. Monday he enters two oral marks from a second tab; Tuesday the single autosave draft has been silently overwritten — Sunday's sitting is gone. The spinner spun; it saved; it is not there. 2,640 tickets a quarter were this story.

What was designed. GradingSessions (CMC-04): every grading episode is a named, first-class object — "3B algebra test, started Sun 21:14" — with explicit draft/published states, a resume banner ("Continue 3B algebra — 22 of 30 entered"), optimistic locking, and a visible conflict warning when a second tab touches the same sitting. Publishing is one deliberate act with a visibility preview (labels shown at publish time — the G5 fix inherited).

AI working session. OpenCode built the interactive prototype of the sitting flow in agent mode; Claude Code generated the edge-case script for hallway tests (expired session, two tabs, mid-sitting class merge); Codex's migration review caught the concurrent-tab overwrite path in the first CMC-04 draft — the exact defect being fixed, reproduced in the fix — and the proposal was rewritten before build.

Outcome (illustrative). Resume-task success 46% → 92%; draft tickets −77%; zero lost-grade incidents across 1,050 pilot teachers in eight weeks; teacher weekly active gradebook use 61% → 79%; median re-entry overhead 11 → 3 minutes per teacher per week.

Use case C — The student feed: posted order vs. due order

Scenario. A student participant (S-03, moderated session) scrolls Monday's feed: top item is a teacher announcement posted 9:04 am; below it, a maths worksheet posted Friday but due Tuesday 8:30. She works the announcement's reading — newest first — and discovers the worksheet at 10 pm. 1,800 tickets a quarter and a steady trickle of late submissions were this story.

What was designed. Due-date-primary feed (CMC-05): work is ordered by deadline with explicit posted-vs-due labels ("posted Fri · due Tue 8:30"); announcements live in a separate rail so they cannot crowd out deadlines; "due this week" is a one-tap filter matching the student mental space "What is due next."

AI working session. Claude Code generated two feed variants (pure due-date, and due-date with urgency weighting) and the label copy; the ten student card-sort participants' groupings chose between them — pure due-date won 8 of 10. Codex challenged the urgency-weighted variant's opacity (a ranking students cannot explain recreates Gap Z in miniature); it was dropped.

Outcome (illustrative). Due-this-week task success 58% → 94%; median time 3.8 → 1.1 minutes; assignment-order tickets −54%; late submissions in pilot schools −19%.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Three SOPs were encoded for agent execution, each following the same pattern: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Mental-model extraction SOP (evidence per belief). Output: JSONL model items under the v1 schema (A6 template 1). Acceptance: every belief cites ≥2 independent evidence items from ≥2 participants; every quote verbatim-verifiable; zero taxonomy violations on audit. Gate: Codex taxonomy audit plus human read. The prompt template:
NOTE
You are MENTAL-MODEL-EXTRACTOR. Input: one pseudonymized interview transcript
plus the evidence register items already extracted from it.
Produce model items. For each item output EXACTLY the v1 schema fields.
TYPE RULES (violations are rejected):
- belief: a falsifiable claim about how the system or school works that the
  speaker treats as true ("saved means my sitting is saved"). Never a behavior.
- question: a recurring information need in user voice ("where is the round
  I started Sunday").
- task: an intended action, active verb, user voice.
- observed behavior without a verbalized claim is NOT a belief; leave it in
  the evidence register.
EVIDENCE RULES: statement text must trace to source_lines you cite; beliefs
require evidence from at least 2 independent participants (return
status: candidate if only 1). Never paraphrase quotes. If unsure, drop the
item and log the drop reason.
  1. Card-sort pipeline SOP-as-code. OpenCode executed it end to end; the same command regenerates analysis on new sort waves:

Illustrative OpenCode host command — emitted pack; live case execution unverified.

TERMINAL
opencode run --agent mapper "Read research/cardsort_raw.csv (40 participants x
42 cards). Compute pairwise normalized distance as 1 minus co-grouping share.
Run average-linkage hierarchical clustering, cut height 0.40, fixed seed 42.
Write data/cardsort_distance_sample.csv and reports/clusters.md. Print the
merge order and flag every card whose placement conflicts with the platform IA
in models/system-content-model.md. Do not rename clusters with nouns alone."

Acceptance: deterministic output under the seed; every cluster ≥3 cards or flagged singleton; cluster names active sentences; a human verifies the conflict list is non-empty (an empty list means the sort measured nothing). Gate: designer reviews clusters against interview explanations — the dendrogram says what groups, interviews say why.

  1. Alignment-gap enumeration prompt. Run once per actor model against the system model:
NOTE
You are ALIGNMENT-AUDITOR. Inputs: one actor mental model (beliefs, questions,
tasks with evidence IDs) and the documented system content model.
Enumerate every divergence as a gap record:
- gap_id: GAP-<actor>-<seq>
- user_belief: the belief ID and statement, verbatim from the model
- system_truth: the actual mechanism, cited to the content-model file and line
- cost_signature: ticket category and volume, task-failure rate, or "unpriced"
- severity: critical | major | minor (critical = real-world harm, e.g. missed
  deadline, lost work; major = support demand; minor = friction)
RULES: no gap without both sides cited. If the divergence is an engineering
defect rather than a model gap (e.g. concurrent-write loss), classify it
defect-not-gap and say so. Output JSONL.

The meta-SOP. Research → model → diagnose → align → measure, with hard gates: no modeling before the evidence register passes quote verification; no gap inventory before the taxonomy audit is clean; no CMC PR before its gap carries ticket volume and task-failure data; no pilot before all merged CMCs have rollback flags; no scale before model-match targets hold for four consecutive weeks.

Guardrails.

  • Never average mental models. Per-actor models with explicit segment variants (primary vs. secondary-NLBE vs. secondary-DE parents; batch vs. continuous graders). A single averaged model is wrong for everyone with great confidence — the most dangerous artifact in this method.
  • Evidence per belief. CI fails any PR or model file whose belief claims lack evidence IDs; the quote verifier greps every quoted string against the transcript store.
  • Minors' data. No student PII in any prompt, transcript, or agent context; student research via consented sessions and aggregated telemetry (k-anonymized, DPO-signed); GDPR with EU residency throughout.
  • Human sign-off points. Interview conduct; segment definitions; gap severity classification; CMC merges (product owner + architecture + DPO); pilot go/iterate/kill.

Failure modes observed. (1) Belief/task conflation — 41 observed behaviors were filed as beliefs; the taxonomy audit caught them. (2) Segment averaging — the single parent model hid the DE Gymnasium variant; fixed by segment variants. (3) Invented belief — "parents distrust teachers" rested on one vivid quote and contradicted ticket sentiment; the ≥2-participant rule killed it. (4) Card-sort over-clustering — an early run proposed nine clusters from noise; the fixed cut height (0.40) produced the stable four. (5) Mermaid drift — CI linting killed the class of error permanently.

10 · Lessons learned

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • The biggest defect was conceptual, not visual. Lumen's UI faithfully rendered its content model; no reskin could fix a by-course architecture serving by-child users. The executive "reskin" theory died on the misalignment diagram, not in an argument (See: 09-mapping-experiences/06-other-alignment-diagrams.md).
  • Taxonomy discipline is the difference between a model and a collage. Beliefs, questions, and tasks look identical at transcript speed. The strict v1 schema plus a Codex taxonomy audit caught 41 conflations; without it, the "model" would have been a well-organized list of observations.
  • Segment or lie. The averaged parent model was wrong in two directions; the DE Gymnasium variant changed the drill-down design. Any mental-model SOP should fail CI on a single-undifferentiated-actor model by default.
  • Support tickets are a mental-model goldmine. 68% of ticket volume mapped onto three gaps. Ticket text — parents writing "my daughter" at screens named after courses — was evidence as strong as any interview, and agent classification with a human-reviewed confidence queue made 12,000 tickets tractable.
  • Card sorts measure groupings, not reasons. The dendrogram showed what users group; only interviews explained why. Running the sort without the belief register would have produced an IA tweak, not a realignment (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/05-representational-and-synthesis-methods.md).
  • Codex is counsel, not verdict. Two headline findings (DE segmentation, the CMC-04 concurrent-tab catch) were engagement-shaping; one was unsupported. Verify every finding against the evidence register; never average adversarial output with your own read.
  • Content-model PRs turned alignment into an engineering conversation. Architects reviewed a diff with a KPI contract and a rollback flag, not a poster. The CMC template is the artifact most worth stealing from this engagement.
  • Done differently next time: baseline the model-match score in week 1 (instrumented in week 9, costing comparative precision), and run the student card sort as its own wave from day one — ten student participants was thin for a three-way segmentation.
