Mixed Methods: Quant + Qual under One Roof at Vesper Pharmacy

00 · Provenance note

Confidentiality & provenance note

Vesper Pharmacy is a fictional composite client assembled from patterns observed across several European retail pharmacy chains; no real company, shopper, pharmacist, or store is depicted. All names, figures, datasets, and quotes are illustrative and anonymized, and the mock dataset in data/ is synthetic. This article shares knowledge and process — research designs, SOPs, prompts, review gates — never client specifics. The engagement ran AI-first: OpenCode built the quantitative pipeline, Claude Code ran qualitative synthesis, a dedicated integration agent reconciled the two streams, and GitHub versioned every artifact, with the human designer owning framing, ethics, and gate decisions.

01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

Vesper Pharmacy, a fictional chain of 310 stores, had a puzzle its dashboards could not solve. Click-and-collect was supposed to be the convenient option, yet 18% of prepared orders were never collected, click-and-collect shoppers reported higher effort than walk-ins, and the fastest-served cohort — elderly repeat patients — gave the worst satisfaction scores. Separate teams had separate explanations, each backed by its own data, none reconciled. Over 24 weeks, one lead service designer with an AI agent stack ran a mixed-methods program: quantitative evidence (1.8M pickup events a year, a 12,038-response survey, CSAT/effort telemetry, store operations data), qualitative evidence (35 shopper interviews, 20 pharmacist shadow days, 15 mystery shops), and operations logs — fused through a triangulation matrix in which every theme carries quant-support, qual-support, or conflict tags. The redesign that followed — one status truth, a ready-means-ready notification rule, a dedicated pickup point, and a transparent queue — cut no-shows from 18% to 7% in the pilot, halved pickup effort, and freed roughly 2.6 pharmacist-hours per store per week from status-chasing.

02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

Vesper Pharmacy Group operates 310 community pharmacies across the Netherlands and Flanders: roughly 1.8M pickups a year, 2,900 staff of whom 640 are pharmacists, and a digital channel launched in 2023 — app and web repeat-prescription ordering, click-and-collect, and a small home-delivery offer. Digital adoption is respectable (38% of pickup volume starts as a digital order) but loyalty is lopsided: digital-native parents order weekly while the 65+ repeat-prescription population — the revenue backbone — barely touches the app and reports the lowest satisfaction.

The challenge

Three anomalies, three competing explanations, zero shared evidence. First, 18% of click-and-collect orders are never collected — 123,000 abandoned orders a year, each already picked, packed, and later restocked. Digital blamed store execution; store ops blamed "unreliable app users"; marketing blamed reminder timing. Second, click-and-collect effort (CES 5.0 on a 1–7 scale) exceeds walk-in effort (4.5) — the convenience channel is less convenient than the queue it was meant to replace. Third: elderly repeat patients are served fastest (median 4.3 minutes) yet rate the experience 2.8 out of 5, the worst CSAT of any segment, while wait time barely correlates with their satisfaction (r = 0.06).

Standard retail prescription abandonment tends to sit around 9-10% in published benchmarks [unverified], which makes Vesper's 18% click-and-collect abandonment rate a glaring channel failure rather than ordinary pharmacy leakage.

Meanwhile 41% of app users reported contradictory prescription status messages (SMS saying one thing, the app another), and stores fielded a constant stream of "is my order ready?" calls — failure demand caused by the organization failing to make the service legible the first time (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md).

Constraints

Budget €240,000 for research and design plus a separately costed pilot; 24 weeks to a pilot readout ahead of the annual store-ops planning cycle; a dispensing system that is read-only for anything patient-related; GDPR plus health-adjacent data handling — prescription contents never leave the client's EU data zone and agents only ever see tokenized event streams; and a works council agreement requiring consultation on any change to counter staffing or scripts.

Why service design

Every internal explanation was single-lens: digital had funnel analytics, ops had queue data, CX had survey verbatims, and nobody could arbitrate because the streams were never joined. That is precisely what methodological triangulation exists for: direct observation (what people do), interviews (how they explain it), and quantitative analytics (how often and for whom) combined into one account (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md). Grönroos's perceived-quality model gave the frame — satisfaction is the gap between expected and experienced service, split into technical quality (was the order ready?) and functional quality (how did the wait feel?) — which correctly predicted that "make it faster" was the wrong fix for at least one cohort (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md). Outside In supplied the operating discipline: a standing customer-understanding capability with closed loops, not a one-off research project (See: 14-outside-in/04-customer-understanding.md).

03 · Approach

Approach: the AI-first service design process

The engagement ran the Double Diamond with research and synthesis deliberately overweighted — the design itself was comparatively simple once the evidence was reconciled. Three evidence streams ran in parallel, each with a named agent owner, plus one integration agent whose only job was reconciliation.

We deviated from the template's default Good Services 15-principles audit in A10 because Grönroos's six criteria more directly test the technical-vs-functional quality gap at the center of this pickup failure.

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research design and the pre-registered analysis plan (weeks 1–2)

Goal. Decide what evidence would settle the argument before collecting any of it — and force the organization's three competing explanations into one testable frame.

Method & SOP. Assumption-mapping workshop first, per Kalbach: nine stakeholders (digital, ops, CX, two store managers, IT, marketing, DPO, works council) built a hypothesis map of the pickup journey, and every confident claim was extracted into an "Assumptions to Test" register — the deliberate separation of what the organization thinks it knows from what it has verified (See: 09-mapping-experiences/03-understanding-the-user.md). Then a pre-registered quantitative analysis plan: hypotheses, cuts, and significance rules fixed before the survey closed, as the primary p-hacking guardrail (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/04-evaluative-methods.md).

AI execution. Claude Code transcribed the workshop wall from photos and structured 41 assumption cards into research/assumption-register.csv, each tagged with its owner and the stream that could falsify it. Codex then adversarially reviewed the draft analysis plan:

TERMINAL
codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read research/analysis-plan.md. Attack it as a
reviewer: (1) list every place where a null result could be re-cut into a
publishable positive (post-hoc segmentation, scale re-scoring, window choice);
(2) check each hypothesis has a falsification condition; (3) flag any metric
whose operational definition is ambiguous. Output numbered defects only."

Codex returned 11 defects — including an undefined "active app user" denominator and a plan to test 23 cohort cuts without multiplicity control. The final plan fixes 8 primary hypotheses, a Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction across secondary cuts, and a rule that any exploratory finding is labelled exploratory in every downstream artifact.

Human designer judgment. Choosing which anomalies were worth a hypothesis at all (the elderly fast-but-unhappy puzzle nearly didn't make the plan — the ops director considered it settled), and negotiating the data-zone rules with the DPO before any pipeline was built.

Artifact produced. Assumption register (41 items; 27 later falsified), pre-registered analysis plan (A6), data-zone protocol.

4.2 The quantitative build (weeks 2–6)

Goal. Turn 1.8M pickup events, the survey, and telemetry into a reproducible baseline: the funnel from order to pickup, per channel, per cohort, per store.

Method & SOP. Funnel and cohort analysis over the event stream; survey fielded per the survey design brief (A6) with cleaning rules fixed in advance; store operations data (queue timestamps, staffing rosters, ready-shelf dwell) joined on store-week (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/02-foundational-research-methods.md on secondary data; 14-outside-in/04-customer-understanding.md on VOC architecture).

AI execution. OpenCode owned the pipeline in agent mode, every step a versioned script with a GitHub runbook:

TERMINAL
opencode run --agent build "Read warehouse exports pickups_events.csv (1.8M rows,
tokenized). Build the funnel order_placed -> ready_notified -> collected_7d per
channel (app/web/phone) and per cohort (first-time vs repeat, age band, store
format). Then: join notification_sent_at to shelf_scan_at and flag orders where
notification precedes shelf scan ('premature ready'). Write results/funnel.md
with per-step rates, and data/pickup_funnel_sample.csv as a 113-row synthetic
sample whose cohort means match the computed baseline and pilot to one decimal."
TERMINAL
opencode run --agent build "Clean survey_raw.csv per sop/survey-cleaning.md:
drop speeders (<40% median completion time), straight-liners (zero variance on
grid items), failed attention checks (2 embedded). Report exclusions per rule.
Never impute. Write results/survey-clean.csv and a cleaning log."

The pipeline found the three load-bearing quantitative facts: (1) no-shows concentrate exactly where the "premature ready" flag fires — orders notified before shelfing no-show at 2.3× the rate of properly notified orders — and first-time click-and-collect users no-show at 31% versus 9% for repeat users; (2) wait time explains satisfaction variance in every cohort except 65+ repeat patients (r = 0.06) — the client's analyst was right, and the conclusion drawn from it was wrong; (3) 38% of store phone calls and 24% of counter conversations are status queries, peaking 11:30–13:00 when 31% of daily pickup volume arrives. A GitHub Action re-runs the funnel nightly so the baseline can never silently drift.

Human designer judgment. Defining the cohorts that mattered, and refusing the request to exclude "atypical" stores from the baseline.

Artifact produced. Baseline funnel and cohort pack, clean survey frame (12,038 completes from 41,000 invited, 29.4%), data/pickup_funnel_sample.csv (A7), reproducible runbooks.

4.3 Qualitative fieldwork (weeks 3–8)

Goal. Explain the numbers: why shoppers abandon ready orders, why the fastest cohort is the least satisfied, and what status messages actually do to behavior.

Method & SOP. Three complementary lenses per the foundational-methods taxonomy (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/02-foundational-research-methods.md): 35 semi-structured shopper interviews (12 elderly repeat patients, 9 digital-native parents, 6 chronic multi-script, 5 caregivers, 3 delivery users) anchored in critical incidents — "walk me through the last pickup that went wrong" (See: 09-mapping-experiences/03-understanding-the-user.md); 20 pharmacist shadow days across 12 stores, master-apprentice protocol; 15 mystery shops on a fixed checklist (A6).

AI execution. Fieldwork stayed human. Everything after the recording was agent-amplified: Claude Code ran the transcript-to-evidence SOP — one agent extracting evidence items into research/evidence-register.jsonl (participant code, verbatim quote ≤ 25 words, theme, emotion 1–5), a second verifying every quote verbatim against the transcript store, a third tagging each item with the quantitative cut it speaks to. 412 evidence items resulted. The qual stream answered the regression's riddle within days: elderly shoppers were not unhappy despite being fast — speed was never their currency. Twelve of 14 elderly interviews and 4 mystery shops independently surfaced the same functional-quality failure: no visible queue position, fear of losing their place, no acknowledgement on entry ("You stand there wondering if anyone has even seen you" — P-19, 74). Technical quality (the wait outcome) was fine; functional quality (how the process felt) was failing (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md).

Human designer judgment. Recruitment quotas (oversampling elderly and caregivers), consent in a health context, every live interview and shadow day, and the decision to run mystery shops unannounced with store-manager sign-off but without staff foreknowledge.

Artifact produced. 412-item evidence register, shadowing field notes (20 days), mystery-shop scores (15 visits), interview highlight reel for the G1 gate.

4.4 Integration: one evidence story (weeks 8–10)

Goal. Fuse the streams into a single triangulated account where every theme carries its evidence weight and every conflict has a resolution — not two decks that disagree.

Method & SOP. Methodological triangulation executed as a matrix, not a meeting (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md): every candidate theme registers its quant evidence (metric, n, effect), qual evidence (quotes, coverage), and ops evidence (logs), and receives a verdict — triangulated, quant-supported, qual-supported, or conflict — plus an evidence-strength tag. Conflicts are never averaged; each spawns competing hypotheses and a discriminating test.

AI execution. A Claude Code integration agent ran the reconciliation SOP (Section 7) as a standing loop: pulling funnel cuts, evidence items, and ops summaries, then proposing matrix rows with citations. Representative pairing prompt:

NOTE
You are the Integration agent. Input: theme claim T, the quant cut Q (metric,
n, effect size, p or CI), and qual items E1..En (verbatim quotes, coverage).
1. State whether Q and E agree, disagree, or address different questions.
2. If conflict: propose the two strongest competing hypotheses and ONE
   discriminating test (a data slice or 3-5 follow-up interviews).
3. Assign evidence strength: strong = quant adequate-n AND >=3 independent
   qual sources AND ops corroboration; moderate = two streams; weak = one.
4. Never let a vivid quote raise a weak-evidence row. Quotes illustrate;
   they do not elevate.
Output: matrix row JSON, cited ids only.

Three conflicts surfaced and were resolved. The queue conflict: qual said "queues feel endless," quant said median walk-in wait is a modest 8.4 minutes — resolved by slicing: perceived wait inflates under queue opacity and the 11:30–13:00 peak; the fix is transparency and peak smoothing, not staffing. The notification conflict: ops insisted ready-notifications were accurate (send logs agreed); the join the quant pipeline built showed 17% of notifications preceded the physical shelf scan — both were right about different clocks. The anecdote trap: one viral story ("a customer drove 40 minutes to a locked store") initially dominated stakeholder memory; the integration agent tagged it weak and it correctly died at G1. Codex's final challenge pass caught one theme ("shoppers want locker pickup") resting on a single leading survey question — demoted to exploratory.

Human designer judgment. The verdict column is a judgment, and the designer owned it — including the politically delicate call that the "unreliable app users" theory was falsified by the first-time/repeat split: a design failure hitting newcomers, not a character flaw of users.

Artifact produced. Triangulation matrix (14 themes; 9 triangulated, 2 quant-supported, 1 qual-supported, 2 resolved conflicts), 7 insights, 6 design principles: P1 One status truth. P2 Ready means ready (notify only on shelf scan). P3 Separate the fast from the complex. P4 Make the queue visible. P5 First-timers get a guide, not a shrug. P6 Every pickup teaches the system (feed outcomes back into predictions).

4.5 Mapping and design (weeks 10–14)

Goal. Convert the triangulated themes into the two alignment artifacts — the current-state journey map with its emotion line, and the future-state blueprint for a unified pickup flow — then rehearse them with the people who will run them.

Method & SOP. Journey mapping with evidence tags on every assertion (See: 09-mapping-experiences/03-understanding-the-user.md), then future-state blueprinting with moment-of-truth quality specs per Grönroos: each touchpoint carries a technical standard (order on shelf, code valid) and a functional standard (acknowledged within 30 seconds, position visible) (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md).

AI execution. Claude Code authored both maps as mermaid in version control, regenerating lane content from the triangulation matrix so the maps cannot drift from the evidence; every assertion carries a source tag ([FUN-02], [INT-19], [MYS-07], [OPS-LOG]). During the store-manager blueprint rehearsal, each unanswerable question ("who owns the ready shelf at shift change?") became a GitHub issue tagged blueprint-gap — 17 issues, each resolved into the pilot runbooks.

Human designer judgment. Where the line of visibility sits (backstage prep stays invisible; the shelf scan is deliberately visible as the truth event), and insisting on one blueprint for all channels rather than per-system diagrams.

Artifacts produced. A1, A2, A3, A4, A5 below, with the traceability table (A9) linking every blueprint change to the theme that demanded it.

4.6 Pilot and triangulated measurement (weeks 16–24)

Goal. Prove the unified pickup flow in 12 stores and measure it with the same mixed-methods discipline that produced it — quant, qual, and ops read together.

AI execution. OpenCode maintained the pilot dashboard (nightly funnel, effort/CSAT micro-survey, status-call volume from telephony logs, ready-shelf scan compliance); the same GitHub Action that guarded the baseline now guarded the readout — every number is generated from the committed query set. Claude Code ran 12 follow-up interviews and 6 mystery shops mid-pilot (the inner loop of the closed-loop VOC), catching one failure quant alone would have missed: the new position display reassured elderly shoppers but a Dutch wording choice ("in de rij") read as scolding to some; copy fixed in week 4. The outer loop routed three structural issues (one store's shelf layout, one wholesaler cutoff, one SMS-template bug) to owners with dates.

Human designer judgment. Gate criteria, the works-council conversation about the pickup-point role, and the scale recommendation.

SOP / PROMPT
# Analysis Plan v1.0 (locked 2026-03-13, PR #9)
H1: No-show rate is higher for orders notified before shelf scan.
    Test: rate ratio, chi-square, alpha 0.05. Falsifier: ratio CI includes 1.
H2: First-time C&C users no-show at >=1.5x repeat users.
H3: In the 65+ repeat cohort, wait time does NOT predict CSAT (r < 0.15),

secondary tests. Exploratory outputs must carry the label EXPLORATORY.

06 · Use cases

Use cases

UC1 — The click-and-collect no-show: quant found it, qual explained it

Scenario. 123,000 prepared orders a year never collected. Quant isolated the pattern first: no-shows concentrate where the notification preceded the shelf scan (2.3× rate), among first-time users (31%), and after Friday-evening notices. But the funnel could not say why an arrived shopper leaves empty-handed. Qual did: interviewees described arriving to "not ready, come back tomorrow," learning that "ready" is a lie, and stopping trusting all Vesper notifications, including the true ones. "I ignore the messages now. I just go when I'm passing" (INT-31). Mystery shops confirmed 4 of 15 premature-ready events live.

What was designed. The ready-means-ready rule (notification bound to shelf scan, not batch completion), a day-2 human reminder call replacing the day-6 automated nudge, and a first-timer walkthrough in the order confirmation.

AI working session. OpenCode built the premature-ready flag and the cohort splits; the integration agent paired the funnel cuts with 9 quote-grounded evidence items and assigned strength strong; Claude Code drafted the reminder-call script at B1 reading level. Sample instruction: "Pair theme T1 against FUN-02 and INT-23/31/04. State agree/disagree, then propose one discriminating test for the residual question: do re-notified shoppers return?" (Answer: they do — 71% collect after one accurate re-notification, which sized the reminder-call benefit.)

Outcome (illustrative, pilot). No-show 18% → 7%; first-time completion 69% → 91%; restock labor −5,000 store-hours/yr at scale.

UC2 — The elderly paradox: fast service, worst scores

Scenario. The 65+ repeat cohort: median wait 4.3 minutes — fastest in the business — and CSAT 2.8, the lowest. Operations read the regression (r = 0.06) as "satisfied enough, unfixable." The qual stream read it as a category error: this cohort was never buying speed.

What was designed. Queue transparency, not queue speed: a position display visible from entry, a greet-on-entry standard ("We see you, Mr. Peeters — two ahead of you"), a seated waiting zone with sightline to the counter, and verified caregiver proxy pickup. Wait barely moved (4.3 → 3.7 min); uncertainty collapsed.

AI working session. The integration agent's conflict protocol was the star: quant said "no relationship," qual said "the relationship is with visibility, not minutes." The discriminating test — three follow-up interviews plus a mystery-shop repeat with a paper position ticket in one store — resolved the conflict in nine days. Codex challenged the resolution ("perceived-position correlation could be halo bias"), forcing the mid-pilot wording fix as confirmation. Outcome (illustrative, pilot). Elderly CSAT 2.8 → 4.2; seated-waiting uptake 64% of 65+ visitors; zero wait-time investment.

UC3 — Prescription-status confusion: one truth, every channel

Scenario. SMS fired from the dispensing system; the app read the order-management system; the two clocks disagreed on 17–22% of active orders, and 41% of app users had seen a contradiction. Shoppers developed folk theories ("trust the SMS, the app lies"), and 38% of store phone calls became "is it ready?" — each interrupting a pharmacist mid-check, a medication-safety-relevant distraction observed 7 times per shadow day.

What was designed. A channel-orchestration fix, not a copy fix: one status service emitting a single event stream; the notification orchestrator renders it per channel (app primary, SMS digest fallback); the counter screen shows the same status the shopper sees.

AI working session. OpenCode prototyped the event-feed reconciliation against logs and proved both systems were "accurate" about different events (dispensed vs shelved); Claude Code rewrote every status message around the single source with a B1 reading-level pass; a GitHub Action now diffs app status against SMS templates nightly — contradiction is a CI failure, not a customer discovery. Outcome (illustrative, pilot). Contradiction reports 41% → 9%; status calls −58%; pharmacist shift time on logistics/status 11% → 6%.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs on this engagement:

  1. Quant/qual reconciliation SOP (the signature artifact). Encoding pattern: theme claim → quant cut + qual items + ops log pulled by id → agree/disagree verdict → conflict protocol (two competing hypotheses + one discriminating test) → evidence-strength tag → human verdict gate. The matrix, not a narrative report, is the deliverable; the narrative regenerates from the matrix.
  2. Transcript-to-evidence SOP (A6e) — closed taxonomy, verbatim quotes ≤ 25 words, verifier agent, ≥ 2 independent items per theme, human reads every NEW-THEME.
  3. Survey analysis SOP — pre-registered plan locked by PR; FDR correction; EXPLORATORY label propagated into every downstream artifact by CI check.
  4. Funnel pipeline runbook — every query a committed script; a nightly Action regenerates the baseline; a number that cannot be regenerated cannot enter a deck.
  5. Mystery-shop scoring SOP — fixed 15-point checklist so 15 visits by 4 different shoppers produce comparable rows, not essays.

Theme–evidence pairing prompt template (workhorse of the integration agent):

NOTE
Pair THEME with EVIDENCE. Inputs: theme claim T; quant cut Q {metric, n,
effect, CI}; qual items E[] {participant, verbatim quote, coverage}; ops ref O.
Rules: (1) verdict = triangulated only if >=2 independent streams agree;
(2) a vivid quote never raises evidence strength; (3) small qual samples
generalize to the sample frame only — write prevalence as "x of n interviewed",
never as a population claim; (4) conflict -> two hypotheses + one test;
(5) output JSON with cited ids; no ids, no row.

Guardrails:

  • P-hacking prevention. Analysis plan locked before survey close; secondary cuts under FDR; exploratory findings labelled in every artifact (CI-enforced string check). Codex's 11-defect review of the draft plan is why this held.
  • No overgeneralizing small qual. 35 interviews explain mechanisms, never prevalence. The pairing prompt forces "x of n interviewed" phrasing; prevalence claims must come from survey or funnel.
  • Data-zone restrictions. Prescription contents never leave the client's EU zone; agents see tokenized event streams and pseudonymized transcripts only; PII redaction verified by regex re-scan before any file enters the workspace — verified, not trusted.
  • Human sign-off points. G1 evidence gate (CX lead + ops director read the matrix, not a summary), G2 pilot go (works council + DPO), readout (COO). Every gate is a PR with named approvers.

Failure modes observed and mitigations:

  1. Agent overweighted vivid anecdotes. Early matrix drafts elevated the "40-minute drive to a locked store" story above funnel evidence. Mitigation: evidence-strength tags with the "quotes illustrate, they do not elevate" rule; the row was correctly demoted and died at G1.
  2. Survey agent over-cleaned. First cleaning run excluded 22% of responses by stacking speeder and straight-liner rules on elderly respondents (slower, more uniform answers). Mitigation: cleaning rules stratified by age band; exclusions reported per cohort so bias is visible.
  3. Integration agent averaged a conflict. Asked to reconcile "queues feel long" with "waits are short," its first draft wrote "moderate queue issue." Averaging is prohibited by the SOP; the discriminating-test branch produced the transparency insight that became P4.

10 · Lessons learned

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  1. Reconciliation is a process, not a meeting. The matrix worked because it was a standing artifact with an agent owner, regenerated as evidence arrived — the one week it went stale, stakeholders re-anchored to their pet explanations and re-convergence cost a full session.
  2. The most valuable quantitative finding was a join nobody had run. Notification time vs shelf-scan time existed in two systems for two years. Agents are excellent at proposing joins across silos — but only if someone gives them both tables.
  3. "No correlation" is a finding, not a dead end. The r = 0.06 result nearly closed the elderly question. Qual reframed the variable: the currency was certainty, not minutes. Always ask what the metric is a proxy for.
  4. Cleaning rules are ethical decisions. The over-cleaning failure would have systematically excluded the exact cohort the project existed to understand. Report exclusions per cohort, always.
  5. Pre-registration feels bureaucratic until the first inconvenient null. H3's null survived three attempts to re-cut it away — because the plan was locked and CI checked the label.
  6. Averaging a conflict destroys the information in it. Both sides of the queue conflict were right about different things; the discriminating test found the real variable. Conflicts are the highest-value rows in the matrix.
  7. Mermaid in CI from day one. Two xychart title failures and one broken pie label were cheap in PRs; they would have been embarrassing in the COO readout.
