CX Maturity & Governance: Titan Manufacturing

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Titan Manufacturing is a fictional composite of European-headquartered industrial manufacturers with global aftermarket operations. All names, business-unit labels, figures, diagrams, and datasets in this article are illustrative and anonymized; the purpose is to share method and operating practice, not client specifics. The engagement was run AI-first: Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub did the heavy lifting for assessment, synthesis, telemetry design, governance drafting, and review, while the human designer held the framing, politics, ethics, and final decisions.

1. Executive summary

Titan Manufacturing had the classic symptoms of a company that had invested in service touchpoints without building a service operating system. Its five business units each ran their own customer logic across equipment sales, parts ordering, service contracts, field service, dealer support, and portal access. Large accounts buying from more than one unit faced multiple logins, different service promises, inconsistent frontline handling, and no named owner when a case crossed unit boundaries. Internally, Titan had no shared CX scorecard, no journey ownership model, and no forum with the authority to resolve customer-impacting trade-offs across the business. We used Forrester's six disciplines and maturity model to assess the enterprise, selected a federated central-versus-BU operating model, stood up a design governance structure with journey owners and review gates, and built a measurement architecture that linked relationship and transactional CX signals to operational and commercial outcomes. Within nine months of launch, Titan's enterprise maturity index moved from 2.1 to 2.9, cross-BU cases assigned a named owner within one business day rose from 42% to 87%, portal login success in the consolidation pilot rose from 78% to 94%, and relationship NPS for the top 500 accounts improved from +9 to +16.

2. The brief

Client portrait

Titan Manufacturing is a fictional Munich-headquartered global manufacturer with five business units: Packaging Systems, Flow Control, Industrial Drives, Process Heat, and Precision Components. It serves 8,400 B2B customers across food processing, chemicals, automotive supply, pharma, and advanced materials. The installed base is serviced through 36 service hubs, 160 authorized dealers, and a blended direct-plus-channel model. Roughly 2,100 accounts buy from more than one BU; for them, Titan is experienced as one company on the invoice and four companies in practice.

Digital maturity was uneven. Packaging Systems had a credible self-service portal for spares and manuals. Industrial Drives had decent field-service scheduling. Flow Control still relied on distributor email chains in several markets. Process Heat ran renewals from spreadsheets. Precision Components had strong key-account relationships but almost no standardized feedback mechanism. The corporate portal team had already built identity components, but each BU had wrapped them differently, so customers still saw multiple entry points, roles, and case flows.

The challenge

Titan's executive team did not ask for a prettier portal. They asked why a company with strong engineering, decent margins, and a loyal installed base kept hearing the same customer complaint: "We never know who owns the problem once it crosses a product line." The symptoms were measurable:

  • 5 BU-specific service and portal experiences for customers who believed they had one supplier.
  • 11 dashboards and 5 survey tools, but no shared relationship-versus-transactional measurement stack.
  • 31,200 annual avoidable cross-BU contacts, of which 37% were ownership or status chasing.
  • 17 different renewal templates and 9 separate dealer escalation rules.
  • Portal identity success at 78% in cross-BU journeys because roles, entitlements, and site hierarchies were inconsistent.
  • Only 42% of cross-BU cases assigned a named owner within one business day.

The strategic context made the problem urgent. Aftermarket service and parts represented 34% of Titan's revenue and 49% of its gross margin. The COO wanted more cross-sell across the installed base, but every attempt hit fragmented ownership and duplicate tooling. The CFO was willing to fund improvement only if the investment case moved beyond "better experience" into retained revenue, failure-demand reduction, and lower duplication cost.

Constraints

Budget for the first wave was capped at €2.8M over 24 months, with only €640k available in the first six months. An ERP modernization was already in flight, so the programme could not assume a core-platform replacement. Dealer contracts varied by region, with local channel sensitivities in Iberia, DACH, and Central Europe. Field service hubs were measured on utilization and first-time-fix, not customer effort. Identity and entitlement data sat in multiple systems. Any central model that looked like a corporate takeover of BU commercial control would fail politically.

Why service design

This was a service design in organizations problem, not a UX clean-up. Titan needed to move from isolated touchpoint fixes toward repeatable cross-silo capability, which is the organizational shift described in 01-this-is-service-design-doing/09-service-design-in-organizations.md. The right lens was therefore maturity, governance, and operating model: assess the six disciplines, identify the limiting disciplines, build a federated model, and create journey ownership with real decision rights (See: 14-outside-in/02-the-six-disciplines.md; 14-outside-in/03-the-maturity-model.md; 14-outside-in/07-governance-and-culture.md). Because service performance in manufacturing depends on frontline tooling, ownership, and incentives, the investment case also needed the Service Profit Chain leadership logic: fix internal service quality and governance first so customer value and retention can move (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/06-leadership-and-implementation.md).

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

We treated the engagement as a capability-build programme with one lighthouse journey proving the operating model. The process still followed Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver, but each phase explicitly exercised the six disciplines and moved Titan from the Explore/Formalize boundary toward an Industrialize-ready operating system. In Kumar's terms, the programme spanned sense intent, know context, know people, frame insights, explore concepts, frame solutions, and realize offerings; in TISDD terms, it scaled the loop from research through implementation up to the organizational governance level.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

Phase 1 — Discover (weeks 1–5)

Goal. Establish Titan's current maturity by discipline and identify the limiting disciplines blocking progress.

Method & SOP. We used a six-discipline diagnostic with five-stage maturity logic (Ignore, Explore, Formalize, Industrialize, Differentiate) from 14-outside-in/03-the-maturity-model.md. The SOP was strict:

  1. Define observable evidence for each discipline before scoring.
  2. Interview leaders and practitioners from each BU and shared function.
  3. Audit artifacts: dashboards, survey instruments, design standards, escalation rules, org charts, council minutes, incentive scorecards.
  4. Score each BU by discipline first; calculate the enterprise view second.
  5. Force every score to cite evidence. If evidence was weak or contradictory, score lower.
  6. Identify the limiting discipline rather than averaging comfortingly.

We ran 52 interviews: 5 BU GMs, 8 regional service managers, 6 portal and digital leads, 7 key-account directors, 5 dealer managers, 6 field technicians and schedulers, 5 finance and controller stakeholders, 4 IT architects, and 6 corporate sponsors. We audited 340 artifacts and 11 dashboards. The result was clear: Titan looked more mature in customer understanding and service pride than its customers actually experienced, because measurement and governance were weak enough to keep the enterprise stuck.

AI execution. Claude Code handled the interview fan-out and initial scoring. Each subagent read a batch of transcripts, extracted evidence against one discipline, and returned structured findings. Representative prompt:

You are CX-MATURITY-ASSESSOR.
Input: interview transcripts, meeting notes, dashboard screenshots, policy docs.
Score one discipline on a 1 to 5 scale.
Rules:
- no score without quoted or file-based evidence
- score by operating reality not executive aspiration
- if one BU is advanced but enterprise adoption is low, score the enterprise low
- output fields: discipline, score, evidence, contradiction, blocker, next action
- unknown means not evidenced, not assumed

OpenCode built the evidence index and merged BU scorecards into one machine-readable table, then produced the sample CSV used later in this article. Codex did two jobs. First, it benchmarked Titan directionally against sector leaders in industrial service, but the memo was explicitly marked illustrative and non-citable. Second, it reviewed the assessment for score inflation:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Review the maturity evidence pack. Attack every score above 3.
Where is the proof of enterprise adoption, decision rights, and metric linkage?
Flag flattering language, missing evidence, and any benchmark claim that is not explicitly
marked illustrative."

GitHub acted as the audit spine. We opened one issue per discipline, attached evidence snippets, and required sponsor sign-off before a score moved from draft to agreed baseline.

Human designer judgment. The designer decided what counted as maturity rather than mere activity. Titan had many customer artifacts, but many were project leftovers with no operating consequence. The designer also held the line against political pressure from two BU leaders who wanted their unit scores lifted because they had recently funded portal work. The assessment scored operating system quality, not effort spent.

Artifact produced. Enterprise maturity baseline, BU heatmap, evidence register, and the executive baseline pack. The limiting disciplines were Measurement and Governance, both at 1.6 enterprise-wide.

Phase 2 — Define (weeks 6–9)

Goal. Choose the operating model, define decision rights, and write the governance charter.

Method & SOP. This phase used stakeholder impact and incentive mapping plus governance chartering from 04-service-design-for-business/06-organizational-change.md. We compared three options:

  • Centralized corporate CX office. High consistency, low BU trust.
  • Fully decentralized BU ownership. High local fit, no shared standards.
  • Federated model. Small central office with standards, measurement, and review gates; BU-embedded service owners accountable for journeys.

The SOP for the charter was:

  1. Define enterprise journeys and the boundaries of each.
  2. Name executive sponsor and operational journey owner.
  3. Define which decisions sit centrally, which stay in BUs, and which require council arbitration.
  4. Build a review cadence with pre-read inputs and issue-escalation rules.
  5. Tie governance to funding and standards, not advisory discussion alone.

This is the governance-and-culture move described in 14-outside-in/07-governance-and-culture.md: a steering council, named journey owners, and proactive review gates.

AI execution. Claude Code generated three operating-model options, each with pros, risks, staffing implications, and likely resistance points. It also drafted the first governance charter and a service-owner registry schema. Representative prompt:

Draft a federated CX governance charter for a five-BU manufacturer.
Must include: executive council, standards board, journey-owner forum,
review cadence, decision rights, funding triggers, and escalation route.
Rules:
- central office sets standards and measurement, does not absorb BU delivery
- every cross-BU journey has one accountable owner
- dealer-channel changes require channel lead consultation
- output plain-language charter plus a RACI table

Codex reviewed the draft against the maturity model and flagged two weaknesses: the first draft gave the council visibility but not veto power over customer-impacting changes, and it left dealer-direct boundary disputes too ambiguous. Both were fixed. GitHub became the operating-model repository: owners/, journeys/, standards/, and gates/ folders were created as the future system of record.

Human designer judgment. The designer made the most important political choice of the programme: not to position the central team as a corporate inspector. In Titan's culture, that would have triggered BU resistance immediately. The federated model was framed as a service to the BUs: common evidence, common standards, and a faster way to resolve cross-unit conflicts. The designer also negotiated that the COO, not the CIO or a corporate transformation office, would sponsor the council, because the change was operational and commercial, not just digital.

Artifact produced. Governance charter, decision-rights map, service-owner matrix, and forum cadence. Titan now had a design council with teeth rather than a proposal for one.

Phase 3 — Develop (weeks 10–14)

Goal. Build the measurement architecture, standards library, and lighthouse journey blueprint that made the governance model operable.

Method & SOP. Mature governance requires a measurement stack that can see both relationships and transactions, as described in the Measurement discipline from 14-outside-in/02-the-six-disciplines.md. We therefore designed the architecture at three levels:

  1. Relationship layer — quarterly NPS and account-health review for top accounts and dealer cohorts.
  2. Transactional layer — CES and CSAT after login, case routing, renewal, parts fulfilment, and field visit moments.
  3. Operational layer — owner assignment time, taxonomy compliance, telemetry freshness, case handoff count, dealer-direct escalations, and portal login success.

We also built the standards library: experience principles, taxonomy rules, owner-registry structure, and pull-request checklists. The lighthouse journey was unified customer portal login, because it touched identity, entitlements, cross-BU visibility, and named ownership without requiring an immediate core-platform replacement.

AI execution. OpenCode built the telemetry pipeline and dashboard model from portal logs, CRM cases, ERP contract records, and field-service events:

opencode run --agent build "Create a journey telemetry pipeline for Titan.
Inputs: portal_identity.csv, crm_cases.csv, erp_contracts.csv, fsm_events.csv,
dealer_escalations.csv, relationship_nps.csv, transaction_surveys.csv.
Outputs: journey_event_model, owner_assignment_sla, login_success_rate,
renewal_effort_score, dealer_direct_cycle_time, and BU rollups.
Do not invent fields. Mark any missing join key explicitly."

Claude Code authored the standards library and the blueprint for the unified login experience, plus the measurement dictionary with one definition per metric. Codex reviewed the taxonomy and warned that Titan's first draft mixed customer organization, site, and asset identifiers in one owner model; the correction prevented a future reporting mess. GitHub was the working surface: standards lived in markdown, owner registries in YAML, and dashboards were rebuilt from committed metric definitions. Pull requests could not merge unless they named a journey, owner, metric impact, and evidence source.

Human designer judgment. The designer deliberately kept the lighthouse scope narrow. Titan's portal leaders wanted to use the programme to justify a full portal rebuild. That would have delayed credibility. Instead, the blueprint focused on identity, role clarity, case ownership, and cross-BU visibility — the minimum set required to prove that governance and standards could improve experience fast.

Artifact produced. Measurement architecture, standards library, unified-login blueprint, mock dataset, and first-draft 24-month roadmap.

Phase 4 — Deliver (weeks 15–18)

Goal. Launch the governance forums, operationalize review gates, and secure the investment case for the 24-month roadmap.

Method & SOP. This was capability roadmapping and leadership implementation in the sense of 20-the-service-profit-chain/06-leadership-and-implementation.md: leadership had to fund internal quality, invert support toward the frontline, and align incentives. We also used the organizational-change sequencing from 04-service-design-for-business/06-organizational-change.md: demonstrate value, build infrastructure, then embed governance.

The operating cadence was designed as follows:

  • Monthly Executive CX and Service Council.
  • Bi-weekly Journey Owner Forum.
  • Monthly Standards Board.
  • Quarterly Walk-the-Chain executive immersions at a service hub or dealer.
  • GitHub review gates for all customer-facing changes affecting login, cases, renewals, or channel rules.

AI execution. Claude Code generated the council pre-read pack from dashboards and unresolved issues, then drafted the investment case narrative linking friction to revenue, retention, and duplicated cost. OpenCode refreshed dashboard data weekly and automated the KPI pack. Codex reviewed the business case assumptions and stripped out one weak claim about peer benchmark NPS uplifts; only Titan-grounded numbers remained. GitHub automated the rhythm: issue templates for new journeys, PR templates for standards changes, Actions that checked required metadata, and dashboard exports on a fixed schedule.

Human designer judgment. The designer did the work AI could not do safely: deciding which incentives to touch first, which BU GM would own the most politically exposed journey, and how much authority the council needed in quarter one to be effective without causing a backlash. The designer also insisted that the first executive immersion happen in a service hub, not in a conference room, to make the internal-service-quality logic visible.

Artifact produced. Ratified governance charter, owner registry, council cadence, investment case, and 24-month roadmap.

6. Use cases / case studies

Use case 1 — Equipment BU portal consolidation

Scenario. A top-100 food-processing customer bought packaging equipment from one BU and flow-control assemblies from another. Its maintenance team had three Titan logins and no consistent case view.

What was designed. We treated portal consolidation as a journey-governance problem, not an interface problem. The design combined single sign-on, site-level entitlements, a named owner tile, and a minimal shared workspace that surfaced contracts, parts orders, and open service cases without re-platforming every BU application.

AI working session summary. Claude Code mapped the required entitlements and drafted the login blueprint; OpenCode stitched identity and usage telemetry into a shared event model; Codex reviewed whether the design had quietly become a portal-rebuild proposal. Representative prompt:

Map the minimum shared workspace required for a multi-BU account.
Keep only capabilities that reduce customer ambiguity in the first 90 seconds:
identity, site context, named owner, open cases, active contracts, and next action.
Reject anything that depends on replacing a BU core system.

Outcome. In the top-500-account pilot, login success rose from 78% to 94%, monthly active multi-BU users rose from 620 to 1,480, and cross-BU cases with a visible owner rose from 42% to 87%.

Use case 2 — Service-contract renewal across parts and service

Scenario. Titan renewed maintenance contracts through separate parts and field-service motions. Customers often received a parts quote, then a service plan, then a renewal note from a third person.

What was designed. We created a single renewal journey with a 120-day trigger, a shared commercial workspace, renewal standards, and one journey owner accountable for parts coverage, technician scope, and commercial timing.

AI working session summary. Claude Code produced the renewal service blueprint and the RACI draft. OpenCode built the renewal telemetry model from ERP and CRM extracts. Codex attacked the first design for underweighting dealer-led accounts; the final design added channel-specific rules. Sample command:

opencode run --agent build "Join contracts, visits, parts usage, and quote events.
Identify renewals started inside 120 days of expiry, who touched them, cycle time,
and whether the customer had to repeat asset or coverage data. Output renewal_facts.csv
and a per-BU effort summary."

Outcome. Renewal cycle time fell from 27 days to 14 days in the pilot cohort, transactional CES improved from 3.8 to 2.7, and renewal capture for multi-BU contracts improved from 68% to 77%.

Use case 3 — Dealer channel versus direct sales boundary

Scenario. In Iberia and Central Europe, some accounts were sold direct but serviced partly through dealers. When failures occurred, both sides sometimes assumed the other owned the next step.

What was designed. We created a boundary policy that defined channel of sale, service entitlement, dispatch authority, spare-parts authority, and escalation route. The governance innovation was not the rule itself; it was giving one journey owner the right to maintain the rule and bring exceptions to the council.

AI working session summary. Claude Code structured the rulebook; Codex reviewed it for ambiguity and benchmarked common manufacturing channel patterns directionally; GitHub stored the current rule matrix as a versioned artifact with required reviewers from dealer operations and BU service. Example prompt:

Draft a dealer direct boundary matrix.
For each scenario define sales owner, service owner, parts owner, billing owner,
and escalation route. Mark any case where the customer could receive conflicting instructions.

Outcome. Average escalation cycle time fell from 9.4 days to 3.1 days, duplicate dispatches fell 41%, and formal dealer-versus-direct complaints on in-scope accounts fell 33% over six months.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

This engagement only worked because the service design methods were encoded as operating instructions, not left as workshop folklore.

SOP 1 — Maturity assessment SOP

Encoding pattern: method -> evidence requirements -> scoring rubric -> contradiction handling -> output schema -> human review gate.

SYSTEM ROLE: Enterprise CX maturity assessor
OBJECTIVE: Score one discipline from 1 to 5 for one BU and for the enterprise.
STEPS:
1. Read interview evidence and artifact list.
2. Extract observable practices only.
3. Compare against stage rubric.
4. Record contradictions.
5. Recommend the lower score when evidence is mixed.
OUTPUT SCHEMA:
{discipline, unit, score, evidence[], contradictions[], blocker, next_action}
ACCEPTANCE:
- every score backed by at least two evidence items
- no benchmark claims unless marked illustrative
- unknown fields explicit
REVIEW GATE:
- human designer approves any score above 3

Guardrail. No quote, no claim. No artifact, no maturity point.

SOP 2 — Governance-charter authoring SOP

Encoding pattern: journey boundary -> owner -> forum -> decision rights -> metric set -> escalation route -> version control.

ROLE: Governance charter writer
INPUTS: journey scope, sponsor, stakeholder map, metric dictionary, operating constraints
OUTPUTS: plain-language charter, RACI, meeting cadence, required pre-read list
RULES:
- central office may set standards and gates but not absorb BU delivery work
- every journey has one accountable owner
- every metric has one definition and one source of truth
- if dealer channel is affected, dealer lead is mandatory reviewer

Guardrail. Agents may propose options, but only the human designer and sponsors assign final decision rights.

SOP 3 — Standards-library maintenance SOP

Encoding pattern: change request -> evidence link -> metric impact -> owner approval -> PR gate -> publication.

ROLE: Standards librarian
WHEN: a team proposes a new journey rule, metric definition, or customer-facing standard
CHECKS:
1. linked customer evidence present
2. owner named
3. rollback plan present
4. metric impact stated
5. review route complete
PUBLISH ONLY IF:
- all checks pass
- charter owner approves
- council review attached when customer impact is cross-BU

Meta-SOP

The meta-SOP was the same one used across the series: research -> synthesize -> map -> design -> instrument -> govern -> review. Here, the special addition was anti-flattery. Maturity scoring and governance design are especially vulnerable to AI pleasing the sponsor. We therefore added two explicit guardrails:

  • Avoid score gaming. Agents must score operating evidence, never intent or planned initiatives.
  • Flag benchmarks as illustrative. Codex could produce sector comparison memos, but any external comparison had to be marked directional unless backed by verified sources.

Failure modes and mitigations

Failure mode Why it happens Mitigation
Agent flatters maturity scores Executive language sounds mature even when routines are weak Require evidence citations and contradiction fields
Benchmark inflation Agents import unverified sector claims Mark benchmarks illustrative; exclude from ROI math
Central-office bias Corporate sponsors dominate inputs Score BU realities separately before enterprise aggregation
Metric overload Agents list every measurable thing Keep one relationship, one transactional, and a few operational metrics per journey
Governance theatre Charter reads well but has no veto or funding rights Review decision rights explicitly with sponsors
Standards drift Teams edit local copies Version standards only in GitHub repo with PR gates

Causal logic

The economic case followed the Service Profit Chain logic rather than a soft-benefits narrative (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/06-leadership-and-implementation.md). Internal service quality improved first: cleaner identity rules, clearer ownership, common standards, and shared telemetry. That reduced employee friction in hubs and account teams. Better employee conditions made it easier to respond consistently, which reduced customer effort and ambiguity in the journeys Titan chose to govern. Only then did relationship NPS, renewal capture, and retained margin move. This is the same logic used in article 05 for logistics, but applied here to a manufacturing service system rather than a 3PL account experience. See: ../05/README.md.

9. AI tool playbook (per engagement)

Claude Code

Role on this engagement. Primary synthesis and artifact-authoring engine. Claude Code ran the maturity assessment fan-out, drafted the governance charter, wrote the standards library, and maintained traceability between customer evidence and governance decisions.

Concrete usage. Representative prompt:

Build a traceability table from customer pain to governance response.
Inputs: maturity evidence, journey map, standards list, metrics dictionary.
Output columns: opportunity, principle, operating change, metric.

What it excelled at. Turning fragmented evidence into structured artifacts fast. Keeping long-form governance documents internally consistent.

What it failed at. It sometimes over-credited aspirational practices as mature ones until the scoring guardrails were tightened.

When the human took over. Final scoring, political framing, decision-right assignment.

OpenCode

Role on this engagement. Data and telemetry builder. OpenCode assembled the event model, joined BU operational data, and generated the metric spine for dashboards.

Concrete usage.

opencode run --agent build "Generate the Titan journey telemetry mart and metric dictionary.
Show joins, missing keys, and confidence per metric."

What it excelled at. Mechanical data shaping, metric rollups, and repeatable dashboard pipelines.

What it failed at. It needed tight instructions on entity definitions; otherwise customer, site, asset, and contract entities blurred.

When the human took over. Choosing the canonical business definitions and deciding which metrics mattered enough to govern.

Codex

Role on this engagement. Adversarial reviewer and directional benchmarker.

Concrete usage.

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Review the federated governance design for hidden centralization,
weak decision rights, benchmark overreach, and missing channel-governance rules."

What it excelled at. Attacking flattering language, surfacing where charters had no teeth, and spotting where taxonomy choices would age badly.

What it failed at. Its external benchmark instincts were useful but had to be treated carefully; nothing external entered the business case unless independently supported.

When the human took over. Any claim involving external comparison, executive incentives, or organizational politics.

GitHub

Role on this engagement. Operating system for governance artifacts.

Concrete usage. Journey charters lived in journeys/, owner records in owners/, standards in standards/, and review rules in gates/. Pull requests required journey, owner, evidence, and metric-impact fields. Actions generated the council pre-read pack and blocked incomplete standards changes.

What it excelled at. Making governance versioned, reviewable, and non-theatrical.

What it failed at. It cannot create authority by itself. A repo without sponsor backing is still just a repo.

When the human took over. Ratifying charters, enforcing attendance, and using the outputs in real council meetings.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • Titan's weakest disciplines were exactly the ones leaders wanted to skip past. Measurement and governance felt administrative; they were actually the unlock.
  • A central design office without explicit service to the BUs would have failed. The federated model mattered more than the org-chart label.
  • Identity and ownership sound technical, but in manufacturing they are commercial and operational. Customer effort often starts with entitlement confusion, not bad UI.
  • The first maturity draft was too generous. Evidence-based scoring is essential when sponsors are eager to hear progress language.
  • Directional benchmarking helped provoke ambition, but none of it belonged in the financial case unless Titan could prove a local mechanism.
  • Dealer-channel governance must be designed in from the start. Adding channel logic later creates shadow rules and resentment.
  • Standards only work when tied to review gates, named owners, and a maintained metric dictionary.
