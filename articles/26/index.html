Good Services in the Public Sector: Fernwood Council's Citizen Account and Planning Applications Service

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Fernwood Council is a fictional composite of several UK-style local authorities. Every resident name, case, figure, and dataset in this article is illustrative and anonymized, and euro values are used for series consistency. The continuity with earlier Fernwood articles is deliberate — this is the same fictional council first used in the AI-first operating-system article and then in the housing repairs blueprint case — but this article shares methods, standards, and operating patterns, not real client information. The engagement ran AI-first: Claude Code maintained the standards register and core artifacts, OpenCode built the citizen-account prototype and analytics pipeline, Codex performed adversarial standards audits and public-service realism checks, and GitHub enforced the service standard in code.

1. Executive summary

After Fernwood Council's housing repairs redesign proved that status visibility can cut failure demand at authority scale (See: ../11/README.md), the council moved upstream. The next target was planning: a service that was politically sensitive, jargon-heavy, emotionally loaded, and fragmented across webpages, PDF validation lists, email, phone, and committee letters. Over 16 weeks Fernwood designed a new citizen account spanning planning, repairs, council tax, and waste, then used the planning applications service as the first end-to-end service built explicitly to Lou Downe's 15 principles. The result was a standards-driven public service: the principles were translated into observable tests, GitHub PR gates, accessibility CI, assisted-digital routes, decision-explanation templates, and a named service-owner model. In the private beta, the planning service cut mean decision days from 50.5 to 35.7 in the sample cohort, reduced resident progress-checking from 7.8 to 2.7 touches per case, raised completion within the published service commitment from 58% to 83%, and turned the planning journey from an opaque administrative ritual into a coherent public service.

2. The brief

Client portrait

Fernwood Council returns from article 11 as the same fictional unitary authority: 310,000 residents, three large public-facing directorates (housing, planning, and neighbourhood services), a low-to-mid digital maturity estate, and a growing political expectation that every resident-facing service should show the same standard of clarity and accountability. The repairs pilot described in article 11 gave the council two important assets: first, proof that failure demand falls when the service exposes status honestly; second, a reusable architectural pattern — one account, one timeline, one evidence-backed service standard.

Planning became the next target because it concentrated three hard public-sector problems in one service:

  1. It is high-stakes and non-optional. Residents cannot choose another provider. If they need permission to extend a home, comment on a nearby scheme, or understand a refusal, Fernwood is the service.
  2. It is multi-sided. The same service must work for first-time applicants, digitally confident agents and architects, neighbours objecting to a scheme, elected planning committee members, and residents who need a phone or in-person route.
  3. It is trust-sensitive. Planning decisions create winners, losers, and public scrutiny. A fast but opaque service is still a bad service in public value terms. See: 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/04-service-design-for-public-services.md.

Fernwood handles roughly 1,260 planning applications a year: about 52% householder, 18% small full applications, 11% listed building and conservation-area cases, 7% tree works, 6% change of use, and 6% other permissions and certificates. The pre-redesign digital estate was fragmented: a generic planning microsite, three separate logins across council digital services, PDF local validation checklists, an upload form with limited feedback, manual email updates from case officers, and formal decision letters that satisfied legal process but failed plain-language comprehension.

The challenge

The baseline failure pattern resembled repairs, but with a different emotional profile. Residents were not mainly angry; they were uncertain. The council's own audit found that applicants checked case progress an average of 7.8 times, 34% of householder applications were invalid at first submission, 18 formal planning complaints were logged per quarter, and refused or conditioned applicants often said the same thing: "I still do not know why." The organization had optimized statutory steps, not the whole outcome. See: 05-good-services/01-what-is-a-service.md.

The service also failed at the edges of the lifecycle. Residents struggled to find the right start point when their real need was "Can I build a kitchen extension?" rather than "Submit a householder planning application." They could not tell within 10 seconds whether they needed permission, what drawings were required, or how long the route would take. If the council requested amended drawings, the case timeline vanished into email. If a neighbour objected in a conservation area, the response letters quoted policy and committee process but did not explain the decision in ordinary language. And if an older resident wanted help, the phone route sat outside the digital record, forcing them to repeat the same facts. These are direct failures against Principles 1, 2, 3, 10, 13, and 14. See: 05-good-services/03-principles-usability.md, 05-good-services/04-principles-trust.md, 05-good-services/05-principles-control.md.

Constraints

  • Statutory and democratic context. Planning decisions are quasi-judicial, evidence-based, and politically visible. The service must preserve procedural fairness, consultation windows, and member oversight.
  • Legacy estate. Fernwood could not replace the back-office planning system in the horizon. The new service had to wrap existing casework, GIS, payment, and document-store capabilities.
  • Accessibility and inclusion. The service had to work for digitally confident architects and for residents like Ron, a retired resident who prefers a phone appointment and printed confirmation. Assisted digital could not be a side process.
  • Cross-service ambition. The citizen account had to support planning first, but its identity, timeline, and notification patterns also had to work for repairs, council tax, and waste.
  • Political trust. The planning committee chair, the Director of Place, and the monitoring officer all wanted fewer complaints, but they would reject anything that looked like a "digital front end" bolted onto a legally brittle service.

Why service design

This was a whole-service problem, not a page-redesign problem. The resident's outcome is not "submit form P-12." It is "understand whether I can change my property, make a complete application, track it, understand the decision, and know what to do next." See: 05-good-services/01-what-is-a-service.md. Public-service design also changes the success criteria: because citizens are captive, the service must protect dignity, clarity, and procedural justice, not only throughput. See: 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/04-service-design-for-public-services.md. Tim Brown's argument about a new social contract is relevant here too: the council had to design with residents, case officers, and objectors, not merely digitize the internal process. See: 15-change-by-design/08-the-new-social-contract.md.

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

Fernwood ran the work as a public-service lifecycle with two overlays. The outward frame was Discover -> Define -> Develop -> Deliver, because that is how sponsors and delivery teams understood the program. Underneath, the service ran through Discovery, Alpha, Beta, and Live-readiness logic from UK public-service practice, with the 15 principles acting as the user-facing quality standard and the GOV.UK Service Standard acting as the delivery governance gate. See: 05-good-services/06-designing-good-services.md and https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/service-standard.

The design team deliberately treated the 15 principles as measurable properties rather than qualitative aspirations. "Sets expectations" became a test about whether residents can recall time, cost, documents, and next steps after a 10-second exposure. "Explains decisions" became a four-part explanation template tested with refused and conditioned applicants. "Easy to get human assistance" became a requirement that the phone adviser can see the same draft, same checklist, and same timeline as the resident. This is the core move of the case: principle -> observable test -> artifact -> CI gate.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Discover — audit the service against the principles, not the org chart

Goal. Understand what residents, agents, and objectors are actually trying to achieve, then measure where the planning service breaks against the 15 principles.

Method & SOP. The team used a combined user-needs audit, lifecycle edge audit, and seam-mapping exercise. Research covered 16 recent applicants, 6 neighbours who had objected to an application in the last 12 months, 5 planners or case officers, 3 assisted-digital or contact-centre advisers, 2 elected members, and 2 architects. The core SOP was simple and strict: capture the real user goal, map the full lifecycle from need to decision, mark every handoff between content, digital, casework, and democratic process, then score each principle as fail, partial, or pass with evidence. See: 05-good-services/01-what-is-a-service.md, 05-good-services/02-the-fifteen-principles.md, 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/04-service-design-for-public-services.md.

AI execution. Claude Code ran the evidence register and the standards baseline. One subagent extracted user-needs statements, a second mapped friction to principles, and a third checked that each scored principle carried at least one evidence reference. Codex ran a realism pass focused on UK public-service delivery and the GOV.UK Service Standard so the team did not design a private-sector flow for a statutory service.

claude-code task "Read research transcripts, call summaries, and the planning-content audit.
For each friction, map: lifecycle stage -> violated principle -> evidence quote -> likely
operational seam. Output standards/baseline-scorecard.md with no empty evidence cells."

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check \
  "Review the baseline planning journey against the GOV.UK Service Standard and UK local-government
   planning practice. Flag any step that would fail whole-problem, joined-up, accessibility,
   or explainability expectations. Cite only official or clearly marked [unverified] sources."

GitHub housed the evidence pack as issues: one issue per principle breach, one label per lifecycle stage, and one PR for the baseline scorecard.

Human designer judgment. The designer led the interviews, decided which resident journeys were structurally representative, and held the line on a critical reframing: the problem was not "residents do not read instructions." The problem was that the service made residents absorb policy complexity and internal casework state. That reframing moved the work from content tidy-up into whole-service redesign.

Artifact produced. A needs register, seam map, current-state journey, and baseline A10 audit showing the planning service strong on statutory completion but weak on findability, expectation-setting, decision explanation, assisted digital, and right-knowledge burden.

4.2 Define — turn principles into a standards register and service concept

Goal. Define the citizen account and planning service as a set of measurable service properties with named owners, test rules, and artifacts.

Method & SOP. The defining method was a principles-driven design briefing paired with participatory co-design. Each of the 15 principles was translated into a challenging design prompt, an observable test, and an acceptance criterion. For example: Principle 3 became "How will a first-time applicant know time, fees, drawings, consultation stages, and likely officer responses before starting?" Principle 13 became "How will a refused or conditioned applicant understand the exact reasons, evidence, and next step without reading committee procedure first?" See: 05-good-services/02-the-fifteen-principles.md, 15-change-by-design/08-the-new-social-contract.md.

AI execution. Claude Code became the standards registrar. It maintained one markdown row per principle with fields for test, evidence, artifact, owner, and PR gate. In parallel, it drafted three concept directions for the citizen account: a document-heavy portal uplift, a case-tracker-first model, and a guided service model with embedded explanation and assisted digital. OpenCode generated rough interaction flows from the accepted concept. GitHub stored the standards register as code so a principle could fail a build if its evidence link or acceptance criteria disappeared.

Claude Code prompt — standards register builder:
"Create standards/register.md from the 15 principles. For each principle output:
principle_id, plain-language definition, observable test, required evidence,
artifact owner, and merge gate. Use planning-service language, not generic examples.
If a principle depends on assisted digital or a human route, say so explicitly."

Human designer judgment. The human team chose the guided service model because it best matched public value: it reduced effort without disguising statutory seriousness, preserved objection and consultation rights, and gave the council a reusable citizen-account shell across other services. The designer also set the rule that the account would never force channel migration: phone and in-person help would write into the same case record as the digital route.

Artifact produced. The standards register, stakeholder map, ecosystem map, two core personas, and a concept definition for a unified citizen account with planning as the first fully standards-driven service.

4.3 Develop — build the whole service, not only the screens

Goal. Prototype the end-to-end planning service, including letters, explanations, assisted digital, and case-officer tooling, then test it against accessibility and trust criteria.

Method & SOP. Fernwood used whole-service prototyping and inclusive walkthroughs. That meant the team prototyped start pages, eligibility guidance, document checklists, upload flows, status updates, neighbour-notification emails, objection acknowledgements, refusal and conditional-approval letters, and the case officer message template. They ran scenario-based tests for three routes: a standard householder extension, a conservation-area objection, and an assisted-digital application by phone. See: 05-good-services/06-designing-good-services.md, 05-good-services/05-principles-control.md.

AI execution. OpenCode built the working citizen-account prototype in agent mode: a reusable account shell with service cards for planning, repairs, council tax, and waste; a planning start page; a structured document checklist; a case timeline; a consultation inbox; and a decision-explainer page. Claude Code authored the future-state blueprint and the principle-to-artifact trace table. GitHub Actions ran automated accessibility, copy-pattern, and standards checks on every PR.

opencode run --agent build "Create a planning-service prototype in prototype/ with:
1) citizen account dashboard, 2) planning start page with check-before-you-start,
3) guided application flow, 4) applicant timeline, 5) objection inbox, 6) decision
explainer pages, 7) assisted-digital adviser view. Use plain language, GOV.UK-like
interaction patterns, keyboard-accessible components, and sample cases from
articles/26/data/planning_audit_sample.csv."

Claude Code also ran the explanation and accessibility audits:

Audit agent prompt:
"Walk every happy and unhappy path. For each path test Principles 10 through 15:
no dead ends, inclusive use, change response, decision explanation, human help,
and right knowledge burden. Output failures with severity and exact screen or letter."

GitHub made the service standard operational:

# .github/workflows/service-standard-gate.yml (excerpt)
on: [pull_request]
jobs:
  planning-standard-gate:
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - run: node scripts/check_principles_register.mjs standards/register.md
      - run: node scripts/check_mermaid.mjs README.md
      - run: pnpm axe prototype --rules wcag2aa,wcag22aa
      - run: node scripts/check_plain_language.mjs prototype/content/
      - run: node scripts/check_unhappy_paths.mjs prototype/spec/failure-states.yml

Human designer judgment. The designer set the thresholds that mattered: no route could pass if a participant failed to understand whether they needed permission, if a refusal page omitted the evidence and appeal route, or if the assisted-digital path created a second-class experience. The designer also rejected an early agent suggestion to hide objection details until the decision stage because it improved "simplicity" at the cost of procedural fairness.

Artifact produced. A working prototype, future-state blueprint, accessibility backlog, decision-explanation templates, and a standards traceability model linking principle breaches to design decisions and tests.

4.4 Deliver — run a private beta with a named service owner and evidence gates

Goal. Move from a prototype to a bounded private beta with live cases, a permanent running model, and a credible recommendation for live rollout.

Method & SOP. The team used service-owner chartering, show-and-tell governance, and beta measurement against both the 15 principles and the four mandatory public-service KPIs: completion rate, user satisfaction, cost per transaction, and digital take-up. See: 05-good-services/06-designing-good-services.md, 05-good-services/07-running-service-design.md. The beta covered 120 live planning cases, including 76 householder applications, 18 conservation/listed-building cases, 14 neighbour objections, and 12 assisted-digital applications.

AI execution. GitHub became the operating system for governance. The standards register, accessibility results, principle scorecard, and live KPI dashboard all shipped as PR-reviewed artifacts. Codex performed two adversarial reviews: one on the service-owner charter to test whether accountability was genuinely end to end, and one on the final principle scorecard to attack any decorative scoring.

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check \
  "Review standards/register.md, service-owner-charter.md, and the beta scorecard.
   Attack any principle scored pass without direct evidence. Attack any owner model
   where accountability can still fall between planning, customer services, and digital.
   Return only material findings."

OpenCode maintained the beta analytics pipeline: pulling timeline events, decision dates, progress-check counts, and satisfaction scores into one case table. Claude Code drafted the weekly show-and-tell memo from that dataset so the service owner could govern with evidence rather than slides.

Human designer judgment. The service owner model was the decisive design move. The designer insisted that Fernwood appoint a single Planning Service Owner with authority across content, digital, operations, and support standards. Without that move, the council would have shipped a cleaner front end onto the same old seams. The human team also handled the politics: aligning planning officers, contact-centre leads, conservation specialists, and elected members around a public-service standard rather than channel ownership.

Artifact produced. A private-beta scorecard, a service-owner and running-model diagram, refreshed A10 audit, and a go-live recommendation with three residual risks logged openly.

Before: 0 pass-equivalent / 5 partial / 10 fail-equivalent = 5 of 30. After beta: 14 pass-equivalent / 1 partial / 0 fail-equivalent = 29 of 30.

The residual partial is Principle 12. The service can respond to case changes quickly inside planning, but full cross-service propagation to council tax and waste remains on the roadmap. That honesty matters: decorative scorecards destroy trust.

6. Use cases / case studies

UC1 — Household extension application

Scenario. Naomi wants a rear kitchen extension on a Victorian terrace. In the legacy service she would search informally, hit a dense planning page, download a PDF checklist, submit drawings once, wait for an invalid notice, and call twice to understand what was missing. In the redesigned service she starts from the citizen account, sees a route checker, and gets a live checklist before submission.

What was designed. A guided application flow for householder work, including route check, example drawings, live validation, and a visible timeline.

AI working session summary. Claude Code generated the principle-specific acceptance criteria for the route checker and timeline. OpenCode built the working flow and seeded it with realistic file-upload scenarios. Codex attacked the draft checklist for hidden expert assumptions.

Prompt used in workshop:
"For Naomi's householder route, identify every moment where the council currently asks
for knowledge only a planner or architect would have. Replace each with an example,
a choice, or a council-side lookup. Keep only the data Naomi can reasonably know."

Outcome. In the beta cohort, valid-first-submit for the householder route improved from 48% to 84%. Progress checks fell from 6.2 to 2.1 per case. Decision comprehension on conditional approvals improved from 44% to 82% in the follow-up test.

UC2 — Heritage and conservation-area objection flow

Scenario. A neighbour objects to a proposed shopfront change in a conservation area. The old service accepted the objection, then went silent until a formal decision notice arrived. The objector could not see whether the comments had been logged, what planning considerations mattered, or why the final decision went the way it did.

What was designed. A trust-focused objection route: acknowledgement in plain language, timeline visibility, and a decision explainer that distinguishes material planning considerations from issues the council cannot lawfully weigh.

AI working session summary. Claude Code drafted the explanation structure against Principle 13. Codex reviewed the wording adversarially for fairness, legal overclaim, and dead-end risk. OpenCode wired the objection inbox and acknowledgement into the same timeline engine used for applicants.

Outcome. Residents who could correctly explain why the decision was reached increased from 34% to 79% in testing. Repeat explanation calls on conservation-area decisions fell from 17 per month to 6 in the private beta period.

UC3 — Assisted digital path for non-digital residents

Scenario. Ron wants to replace an old conservatory but does not trust uploads or online forms. In the old service he would phone customer services, receive generic advice, then be told to go online or visit the office with paper documents. The adviser had no way to save a shared draft.

What was designed. A bookable assisted-digital route inside the same planning service: adviser and resident can complete the guided flow together by phone or in person, save the same draft, and issue a printed summary with the same case ID and timeline.

AI working session summary. Claude Code wrote the assisted-digital SOP and phone script. OpenCode added the adviser view to the prototype. The accessibility audit agent treated the phone route as a first-class path, not a fallback. The human team role-played the route with real advisers to cut patronizing language.

Outcome. Assisted-digital completion improved from 44% to 78% in the beta cohort. Average time to a useful first answer fell from 6 days to 2 days. Progress checks in the phone-first segment dropped from 9.4 to 3.6 per case because the resident finally had a stable case record.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

This engagement encoded three service-design SOP families especially well.

7.1 Standards register SOP

The most important encoding pattern was principle -> checklist -> evidence -> artifact -> gate. That is what stopped the 15 principles from becoming decorative:

SOP FERN-PLAN-STD-001
Role: Standards registrar agent.
Inputs: principle list, live artifact set, research evidence register, current scorecard.
Steps:
1. For each principle, name one observable test in planning-service language.
2. Require at least one evidence quote, metric, or audit result before scoring.
3. Link the principle row to the artifact that proves the design response.
4. If score = Pass, verify the evidence is current and live.
5. If score = Partial or Fail, create or update backlog ticket with owner.
Output schema:
{principle_id, test, score, evidence_ref, artifact_ref, owner, gate_status}
Acceptance:
- 15 rows complete
- zero unsupported pass scores
- every fail or partial has an owner and next action

7.2 Accessibility audit SOP

The second strong encoding was the inclusive walkthrough. The key was to treat accessibility beyond compliance: WCAG CI was necessary, but not sufficient. The agent had to inspect phone, print, timing, stress, and low-literacy routes too.

SOP FERN-PLAN-A11Y-002
Role: Accessibility and inclusion auditor.
Inputs: prototype, scripts, letters, failure-states spec, participant profiles.
Checks:
- Can users complete core tasks with keyboard and screen reader?
- Can users with low confidence understand route, requirements, and next step?
- Does assisted digital write into the same service record?
- Are timeouts, errors, and refusals explained in plain language?
- Is human support visible before distress peaks?
Output:
severity-ranked remediation backlog + retest status

7.3 Service Standard PR gate

GitHub made the standard actionable. The merge gate combined principle coverage, accessibility, mermaid validity, unhappy-path coverage, and plain-language rules. This was the critical bridge from service design into delivery operations.

name: planning-service-standard-gate
checks:
  - principles-register-complete
  - accessibility-ci-pass
  - unhappy-paths-covered
  - decision-explainer-present-where-needed
  - human-help-visible
  - no-bureaucratic-jargon-regression
block_merge_if:
  - unsupported-pass-score
  - new-dead-end-state
  - inaccessible-core-task
  - missing-owner-for-principle-regression

7.4 Guardrails

  • No paternalism. The agent could simplify language, but it could not remove lawful choice, hide trade-offs, or rewrite objections into neutralized summaries.
  • Plain language over false friendliness. Content had to reduce cognitive burden without sounding condescending.
  • Evidence quotes only. A principle could not pass because the interface looked good in a prototype review.
  • Cross-channel parity. Every web change triggered a check on letters, scripts, and assisted digital.
  • Human sign-off points. The service owner signed Gate 3 and Gate 4; the accessibility lead signed the inclusive walkthrough; policy and legal reviewed decision explanations.

7.5 Failure modes observed

  1. Decorative principles. The first standards draft used generic wording like "make it more intuitive." The team rewrote every principle row into an observable test.
  2. Accessibility as a separate workstream. An early agent plan treated assisted digital as a post-beta add-on. Rejected. Principle 11 and 14 both failed unless the phone and in-person routes wrote into the same core service.
  3. Over-optimizing the happy path. A prototype pass looked strong until Codex attacked refusal, timeout, and objection routes. Principle 10 only stabilised after the unhappy-path spec became a PR gate.
  4. Mistaking legal adequacy for explanation. The planning team's original letters were lawfully structured but not understandable. Principle 13 forced a different artifact, not just different prose.

9. AI tool playbook (this engagement)

Claude Code

Role on this engagement. Claude Code was the standards registrar, artifact author, and orchestration layer. It maintained the 15-principles register, drafted the journey and blueprint, generated the traceability tables, and prepared the weekly evidence memo.

Concrete usage. Research coding, principle scoring, mermaid authoring, and PR-ready markdown.

Representative prompt:
"Update the planning standards register from this week's evidence. Do not improve a
score unless the evidence changed. For every pass score, point to the exact artifact
and test result that justifies it."

What it excelled at. Consistency, traceability, and making the service standard durable.

What it failed at. Early tendency toward abstract principle language and one over-neat refusal message that hid public-law nuance.

When the human took over. Interviews, political framing, co-design facilitation, and final decisions on fairness trade-offs.

OpenCode

Role on this engagement. OpenCode built the citizen-account prototype and the analytics pipeline.

Concrete usage. Front-end scaffolding, sample-case generation, accessibility test harness integration, and the beta KPI pipeline.

opencode run --agent build "Implement account dashboard, planning route checker,
shared adviser view, and case timeline. Wire accessibility checks into CI and use
sample cases from planning_audit_sample.csv."

What it excelled at. Turning the service blueprint into a working prototype quickly and keeping the analytics pipeline reproducible.

What it failed at. Its first draft optimized the self-serve flow and under-specified assisted digital; the standards gate caught it.

When the human took over. Deciding which planning nuances must stay visible and which could be absorbed by the service.

Codex

Role on this engagement. Codex was the adversarial reviewer and realism checker.

Concrete usage. Review against the GOV.UK Service Standard, attack unsupported principle scores, and test whether the service-owner model really broke silos.

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check \
  "Be hostile to this scorecard. Any pass score without direct evidence is wrong.
   Any planning-decision explanation that hides the real reason is wrong. Any
   accountability gap between planning, customer services, and digital is wrong."

What it excelled at. Challenging false confidence and surfacing hidden governance gaps.

What it failed at. It suggested one unsupported claim about "common local-government practice" without an official source; the sourcing rule dropped it.

When the human took over. Deciding which adversarial findings were material and which were overfit to idealized public-service models.

GitHub

Role on this engagement. GitHub was the system of record and the governance engine: issues for principle breaches, PRs for review gates, Actions for automated checks, and weekly evidence packs for show-and-tell.

Concrete usage. Principle backlog, artifact review, accessibility CI, and service-standard merge blocking.

What it excelled at. Making the service standard hard to ignore once delivery accelerated.

What it failed at. Nothing conceptually; the only misses were human — if owners forgot to attach evidence, the PR simply stalled.

When the human took over. Final prioritization, gate decisions, and sponsorship conversations.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  1. If a principle cannot be tested, it is still a slogan. The work accelerated once each principle had an observable test and owner.
  2. Planning needs trust more than speed. Reducing decision days mattered, but the bigger shift came from better explanations and fewer status chases.
  3. Assisted digital is not a safety net. It is part of the service architecture. Treating it as a separate route would have broken Principles 11 and 14 immediately.
  4. Decision explanations need product design, not just legal review. Legal sufficiency and user understanding are different artifacts.
  5. Codex was strongest when asked to attack, not to help. Its highest value came from adversarial scoring and governance review.
  6. The service owner model is the structural fix. Without it, the citizen account would have become a new channel sitting on old seams.
  7. Cross-service ambition must be honest. Fernwood could reuse identity, timeline, and notifications across planning, repairs, council tax, and waste, but only planning reached full end-to-end principle coverage in this wave.
