31 — Riding the Current: Rebuilding Public Transport around the Commuter

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Lighthouse Transit is a fictional composite operator assembled from published patterns across European regional transit agencies; no real operator's internal data appears anywhere. All ridership, cost, and satisfaction figures are illustrative and were generated and checked by the Service Design Agent Platform's fact-check layer. The engagement was run AI-first: every claim in this study can be traced to an evidence row, and the core artifacts are deterministic outputs of the platform, not prose.

1. Executive summary

A regional bus-and-light-rail operator with 600,000 daily boardings was losing riders to cars and ride-hail because the service was planned around vehicle schedules, not journeys. Failure demand—contact-center calls caused by the operator's own errors and gaps—consumed 38% of contact volume. An 18-week AI-first engagement rebuilt the end-to-end commuter journey around four platform-executed service designs: a joined-up realtime information system, a fare simplification, a recovery playbook, and a front-line enablement program. On-time performance went from 71% to 87%, failure demand dropped to 18% of contact volume, rider Net Promoter Score rose from −12 to +24, and digital ticketing uptake grew from 22% to 68% of boardings.

2. The brief

Lighthouse Transit operates in a coastal conurbation of 1.8 million people. Market position: the incumbent and only subsidized operator, but modal share has slipped from 14% to 9% in a decade. Revenue is flat; subsidy per passenger-kilometre is rising. Digital maturity is low: a 20-year-old smartcard system, no realtime passenger information beyond a delayed website, and 78% of boardings still paid with a legacy stored-value card or cash.

The challenge was not one pain point but a system: failure demand. A claimable pattern emerged from six years of contact-center logs—38% of calls were the operator's own making: missed connections not protected, closures not signposted, fares consumers could not reason about, and a complaints process that required paper. Each failure generated a call, each call a cost, and each unresolved call a lost rider.

Success criteria, agreed and measurable (all illustrative): on-time performance ≥ 85%; failure demand ≤ 20% of contact volume; rider NPS ≥ +15; digital ticketing share ≥ 60%; cost per journey down ≥ 8%.

Constraints: no new vehicles; a three-year budget lock; a statutory obligation to maintain every route; union and driver-rostering constraints that forbid ad-hoc service changes; and a regional data-protection regime limiting passenger-level tracking. Why service design: transport is the canonical service-dominant case (book 12)—value is co-created in use, the customer's journey spans multiple operator silos, and the economics run straight through the Service Profit Chain (book 20): operational quality → rider experience → loyalty → revenue stability.

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

The arc maps to the Double Diamond: Discover (research weeks 1–5), Define (synthesis weeks 6–8), Develop (ideation + prototyping weeks 9–13), Deliver (implementation + measure weeks 14–18). Against the TISDD loop (book 01), the same phases are Research → Ideation → Prototyping → Implementation, with synthesis sitting inside Research. Kumar's seven modes (book 17) run underneath: Sense Intent → Know Context → Know People → Frame Insights → Explore Concepts → Frame Solutions → Realize Offerings.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Discover (weeks 1–5)

Goal. Build an evidence register that explains where the commuter journey breaks, quantitatively and qualitatively.

Method & SOP. Contextual inquiry (book 01/04): ride-alongs on 14 routes at peak and off-peak; shadowing of drivers, controllers, and the contact centre; 32 semi-structured interviews with commuters, 6 with drivers, 4 with control-room staff. Complemented by transcript mining of 3 years of de-identified contact-center records. SOP steps: plan → observe → interview → select evidence → tag with source and quote → store as evidence rows.

AI execution. Claude Code's Researcher agent executed the research plan from the platform skill contextual-inquiry: it generated the interview guide, then OpenCode agent-mode processed the contact-center export into a tagged evidence dataset—4,112 rows classified by failure type using the taxonomy from book 19 (failure vs value demand). Codex adversarially reviewed the taxonomy against the raw transcripts and found the "missed connection" bucket was under-counting 9% of cases misclassified as "information"; corrected.

# SOP-as-prompt: classify contact-center reason codes
Method: transcript-mining (evidence-gated)
Task: classify each de-identified call record into failure (operator-caused)
  or value (customer-initiated) demand; tag sub-reason.
Schema output: { call_id, demand: failure|value, sub_reason, quote }
Acceptance: every row has a quote; 10% random sample passes human spot-check;
  taxonomy matches book 19 chapter 6 failure types.
Review gate: Critic agent before synthesis may start.

Human designer judgment. The designer chose the ride-along routes to cover all four geographic clusters, insisted on night-shift observation (the 5:40 am controller shift where most missed-run failures originate), and rejected 3 interview transcripts as boilerplate collected from the agency's own staff rather than riders.

Artifact produced. Research plan (template A6) and an evidence register of 4,112 tagged rows. One finding that later anchored everything: 61% of failure demand clustered in the first and last 45 minutes of the service day—peak shoulders the schedule ignored.

4.2 Define (weeks 6–8)

Goal. Turn evidence into a shared truth: the current-state journey, personas, and a service standard.

Method & SOP. Affinity synthesis (book 09/04), journey mapping from transcripts (platform skill journey-from-transcripts), and behavioural personas (book 14/04, 17/04).

AI execution. The Synthesizer agent ran transcript-mining and affinity-diagramming over the register, producing 23 insight statements with evidence counts. The Mapper agent then ran journey-from-transcripts: it derived the current-state journey from the evidence register—each journey step carries at least one evidenced pain point, and steps with zero evidence were dropped (evidence-gated; no invented moments). Personas were generated by behavioural-personas from the same register: four clusters, each with size, behaviour, and evidence-backed needs. The Critic agent vetoed persona "P2: The Night Nurse" for an uncited frequency claim ("she rides 5 nights a week" had no supporting row); the researcher corrected it to "commutes after 21:00 subject to shift patterns" with two evidence rows.

Human designer judgment. The designer selected the four personas from eight candidate clusters, killing two that were demographically distinct but behaviourally identical. The designer framed the headline reframe—from "we run services" to "riders complete journeys"—and pushed the client to accept the failure-demand framing over the comfort of blaming weather.

Artifact produced. Insight set (23 statements), current-state journey map (A1), persona set (A5), and the first service standard draft (A10 input).

4.3 Develop (weeks 9–13)

Goal. Prototype four mutually-reinforcing service designs and blueprint the target operation.

Method & SOP. Structured ideation (How Might We, NUF test), service blueprinting (book 09/05), and storyboard walkthroughs (book 17). The platform skill evidence-gated-blueprint ensures every blueprint change maps to at least one design principle and that principles trace to insights.

AI execution. The Ideator agent ran structured ideation from 12 "How Might We" frames derived from the journey map, producing a 60-concept longlist; NUF (New, Useful, Feasible) scoring filtered to 18. The Prototyper agent drafted three service concepts (fare capping, living-lines realtime, recovery-by-platform) as storyboards. The Mapper agent then ran service-blueprinting to produce the target blueprint; the Critic agent checked the blueprint's backstage rows against the evidence register—it flagged that driver-roster constraints made the proposed 3-minute connection protection physically impossible on 11 routes, and the blueprint was revised before client review (a defect caught at the artifact gate, not in the pilot).

Human designer judgment. The designer chose fare capping over a flat fare (equity), decided the realtime system must be phone-first (rider research showed no secondary data plan for 31% of low-income riders), and ran the concept-selection workshop with the client, overruling the NUF leaderboard on one concept the unions opposed.

Artifact produced. Concept briefs, the future-state service blueprint (A2), prototype storyboards, and the traceability table (A9).

4.4 Deliver (weeks 14–18)

Goal. Implement with operational integrity and prove value against the success criteria.

Method & SOP. Service recovery design (book 19/06), KPI tree (book 20/05), Good Services standards audit (book 05), and pilot-test-report discipline (book 17).

AI execution. The Measurer agent built the KPI tree from the four success criteria down to operational metrics, deriving the data sources. The Presenter agent rendered the steering-pack from artifacts (never from chat). Good-services audit scorings were produced by good-services-audit against the new service standard. The Librarian agent assembled the handover pack: blueprint, standards, KPI tree, and runbooks as platform artifacts with provenance. GitHub ran the gates: each sprint's artifact PR was auto-validated (schema, evidence referential integrity, mermaid lint) before the client saw it.

Human designer judgment. The designer held the monthly steering meetings, resolved the union consultation sequencing, and made the call to delay the fare go-live by two weeks when the pilot showed fare-capping edge cases in the legacy card readers—an operational, not design, defect.

Artifact produced. KPI tree (A6/template), service standards audit (A10), pilot report, handover pack.

6. Use cases

6.1 The shift worker's night. P1's journey was rebuilt by the fare capping + recovery combination. She tapped a prepaid debit card, the engine capped the day's fare automatically, and a missed night connection triggered an auto-credit plus a taxi-partner voucher at the operator's cost (10% cheaper than the call). Outcome: her journey abandonment in pilot fell from 1-in-3 to 1-in-12. AI session: Prototyper agent storyboarded the failure path; Codex adversarially checked the voucher economics against the cost baseline.

6.2 The occasional rider goes digital. P3 had avoided the app (perceived complexity). The simplification made contactless plain pay-as-you-go; the app became a journey-planning layer, not a wallet. Pilot: app installs up 210%, casual ridership +9% on weekend routes. AI session: OpenCode agent-mode generated the fare-simulation dataset (100k synthetic trips); the Measurer agent derived revenue-neutrality from it.

6.3 The interchange handoff. Late buses at the rail interchange triggered 23% of all failure demand. The new data-sharing protocol turned "bus waits" into "recovery applies"; when a bus holds, the rail connection is auto-flagged. Pilot: interchange wait variance −41%. AI session: Mapper agent drafted the protocol blueprint; Critic verified no clause contradicted the evidence register.

7. SOPs for AI

The engagement ran on the meta-SOP: research → synthesize → map → ideate → prototype → measure → deliver, each step opening only when the previous gate passed.

Encoding pattern: method → step list → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate. Every platform skill encodes this; examples: contextual-inquiry, transcript-mining, journey-from-transcripts, evidence-gated-blueprint, good-services-audit.

Guardrails. Hallucination control: every artifact claim must carry an evidence reference or be marked designer-added; the fact-check layer enforces this at the artifact gate. Data privacy: the register held only de-identified rows; the platform never wrote raw caller identifiers to any artifact. Client review: each gate required a named client stakeholder sign-off logged in the steering record. Human sign-off: the Critic can veto, the designer can overrule the Critic only with a written rationale.

Failure modes observed. (1) The NPS figure drifted between drafts—fact-check caught −12 vs −10 inconsistency across two artifacts and re-gated them. (2) A generated persona cited a nonexistent evidence row; referential integrity failed and the persona was rejected. (3) Codex once recommended a fan-out messaging architecture that violated the budget constraint; the designer rejected on cost evidence, not on trust.

9. AI tool playbook

Claude Code — the design team on this engagement. Agents executed skills, drafted every artifact, and ran the gates. Excelled at: long-context synthesis of 4,112 evidence rows, artifact authoring under schema, deterministic fact-check. Failed at: judging political feasibility (union constraints); the designer owned that. OpenCode — agent-mode data pipelines: contact-center classification, fare simulation, diagram code generation, JSON/csv datasets. Excelled at mechanical volume; needed schema-checked outputs. Codex — adversarial review: taxonomy holes, blueprint feasibility, concept economics. Excelled at finding the 9% misclassification and the 11 impossible connections; sometimes too clever; costlier than the design value. GitHub — issues as design tasks, PRs as artifact gates, Actions running schema + evidence + mermaid lint, boards as phase tracking. Excelled at making the reviewer's veto auditable. SDAP — the filesystem-first platform binding the four tools: 10 agent roles from platform/agents, skills from platform/skills, artifacts as typed objects validated by suite/packages/runtime.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  1. The 61% peak-shoulder finding changed everything; without evidence-gating, it would have been drowned by the loudest interview.
  2. NPS drift across drafts is inevitable; make fact-check deterministic and part of the gate, not a QA step.
  3. Do not let the demo-able prototype outrun the blueprint—two concepts passed NUF but were unbuildable under rostering.
  4. Union consultation is design work; schedule it as a phase, not a stakeholder note.
  5. Driver-rostering data was the single most important dataset and the last one shared; negotiate access at the brief.
  6. Auto-refund economics must include fraud and repeat-failure; Codex's adversarial pass caught the naive version.
  7. The presenter agent should render steering packs from artifacts only; slides built from chat drift within a week.
