32 — Air in the Cylinders: Servitizing a Capital-Equipment Vendor

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Aurora Vento is a fictional composite of industrial-equipment servitization patterns; no real vendor's contract or field data appears here. Machine, cost, and contract figures are illustrative and were produced and checked by the platform fact-check layer. The design work was AI-first: artifacts are deterministic platform outputs with evidence-gated claims, and the value model is machine-executable.

1. Executive summary

A manufacturer of hydraulic access equipment with 41,000 machines in the field earned 31% of revenue from services but sold them as aftersales add-ons: parts, time-and-materials repairs, and warranty compliance. Customers bought uptime and got invoices. A 22-week engagement re-architected services around outcome contracts—guaranteed availability with transparent, data-backed performance—rebuilding the field-service operation and the internal service culture that had to deliver it. Service revenue share moved from 31% to 48%, contract renewal from 71% to 88%, mean time to repair from 5.2 to 2.9 days, first-time fix from 61% to 79%, and technician utilization from 58% to 71%.

2. The brief

Aurora Vento designs and builds hydraulic access platforms for construction, warehousing, and event markets. Market position: number two globally, strong engineering reputation, eroding margin on hardware as commoditization bites. Service was a profit pool trapped by its own processes: 74% of work orders were created manually, parts availability for remote regions sat at 81%, and the average machine down-time before a technician arrived was 5.2 days.

The challenge: customers did not want repairs; they wanted availability. Two anchor customers (including the rental group Boreas) had put availability guarantees on the table—if Aurora could not commit, the installed base would migrate to competitors. Leadership's instinct was pricing; the design work showed the constraint was the service system itself: dispatch, parts, knowledge, and incentives built for time-and-materials.

Success criteria (illustrative, contract-shaped): availability ≥ 98% under the outcome terms for pilot sites; MTTR ≤ 3 days; first-time fix ≥ 75%; technician utilization ≥ 68%; service-contract renewal ≥ 85%; services revenue share ≥ 45%.

Constraints: no hardware redesign; a two-year horizon for full rollout; legacy ERP with no service-cloud; union/collective agreements limiting remote-monitoring interventions; regional data protection on machine telemetry. Why service design: this is the textbook service-dominant shift (book 12)—the firm's offering is a value proposition in use, and for B2B the journey includes the customer's operations team, the rental fleet manager, and the technician (book 20: internal service quality drives external service value, which drives external outcomes).

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Discover (weeks 1–6)

Goal. Understand the availability gap as a system: customer operations, rental fleet managers, and the technicians who carry the brand.

Method & SOP. Contextual inquiry (book 01/04) with 24 customer operations managers and fleet managers; shadowing of 9 technicians across 3 regions; job-to-be-done interviews with procurement (book 12 framing). Data: 18 months of work orders, parts stock movements, and machine telemetry (de-identified). SOP steps: plan → observe → select evidence → tag → store.

AI execution. Researcher executed contextual-inquiry and transcript-mining: OpenCode agent-mode normalized 68,000 work-order records into an evidence-ready dataset and classified dispatch states. The platform's evidence register grew to 2,847 tagged rows. Codex adversarially reviewed a draft finding—"parts availability is the top driver"—and showed the real driver was dispatch visibility: 41% of delays were trucks dispatched with the part and no technician, or vice versa. The finding was corrected before synthesis. GitHub tracked the research tasks as issues, each closing with a linked evidence set.

Human designer judgment. The designer chose to shadow technicians over more customer interviews (the internal customer argument, book 20), sat in on two collective-agreement consultations, and killed a research question about warranty fraud that would have poisoned trust with the field.

Artifact produced. Research plan, evidence register (2,847 rows), ecosystem map (A4), and the first "availability anatomy"—a decomposition of down-time into detectable, preventable, and organizational time.

4.2 Define (weeks 7–9)

Goal. Define the outcome: what is sold, what is measured, who is accountable.

Method & SOP. Affinity synthesis (09/04); service definition workshops; agreement mapping (book 12: value is a proposition, not a product). SOP steps: cluster evidence → formulate outcome statement → negotiate the measurable → resolve accountability.

AI execution. Synthesizer produced 19 insight statements. Mapper built the agreement map: the network of SLAs, warranties, and maintenance obligations each customer already had, surfaced as a single artifact (many customers held 5 overlapping instruments; some contradicted). The Critic vetoed an early outcome definition—"guaranteed operational performance"—as unmeasurable; the reworked definition named the availability formula with its exclusions. Designer and client then locked the definition at the value workshop; the agent recorded the decision verbatim in the phase-gate artifact.

Human designer judgment. Choosing the availability formula—uptime in agreed windows, excluding scheduled maintenance and force-majeure, capped at 5% downtime acceptance—was a commercial call anchored by the evidence (book 04). The designer held it against pressure to make the number a marketing floor.

Artifact produced. Outcome definition (A6 template), agreement map (A4), and the measurement spec that later became the KPI tree.

4.3 Develop (weeks 10–16)

Goal. Prototype the service system that can actually deliver 98% availability in two pilot regions.

Method & SOP. Service blueprinting (book 09/05), idea development (book 17), business-model prototyping (book 04), and internal-service-quality design for technicians (book 20).

AI execution. Ideator generated 48 concepts from 9 HMWs; NUF cut to 14. Prototyper storyboarded three operating models: central-dispatch center, region-embedded cohort, and hybrid. Mapper ran service-blueprinting and evidence-gated-blueprint to build the target operation; the Critic gated the blueprint at three checkpoints. Codex adversarially tested the availability model with 10,000 simulated failure events and found the contract's 5% downtime cap allowed a cluster-failure loophole (two machines same site failed simultaneously and only one counted); the model was revised, and the mock contract now spells site-level accounting. OpenCode generated the pricing/parts simulation dataset (A7) used to set the risk pool.

Human designer judgment. The designer chose the hybrid model, defended technician tooling spend against finance's "cost of service" framing (book 20), and ran the union consultation that produced the remote-monitoring boundary terms.

Artifact produced. Service blueprint (A2), mock outcome contract (A6), availability model with simulation (A7/A8), and the traceability table (A9).

4.4 Deliver (weeks 17–22)

Goal. Run two pilot regions under the real contract and prove the value chain.

Method & SOP. Pilot + test-report discipline (book 17), service recovery (19/06), KPI tree (20/05).

AI execution. Measurer built the KPI tree from the contract terms to operational inputs, wiring the telemetry feed. Live fact-check ran at every sprint gate: artifact integrity and evidence referential integrity on 28 artifacts. Presenter rendered steering packs from artifacts. Librarian assembled the handover pack (blueprint, contract template, KPI tree, runbooks). GitHub Actions validated every artifact PR (schema, evidence, mermaid lint) before client review.

Human designer judgment. The designer renegotiated the pilot scope after week 2 when one pilot site lost its facility manager—the contract survived, the pilot almost didn't; the designer set the escalation and the client-side point person. The decision to publish the outcome numbers internally, warts and all, was a leadership call the designer made explicit.

Artifact produced. KPI tree, pilot report, review-veto-log (2 vetoes this phase), handover pack.

6. Use cases

6.1 The rental fleet manager. Boreas operated 14 Aurora machines across 3 sites. The single outcome contract replaced 5 instruments; when a machine breached availability, the credit was computed and issued automatically. Outcome: Boreas renewed with 6 additional machines in pilot month 4; their NPS moved from −4 to +18. AI session: Measurer built the breach-detect rule; Codex adversarially tested the credit formula against simulated data.

6.2 The technician's Tuesday. P3's journey changed: mobile-first dispatch, parts pre-loaded, a knowledge base that auto-appends his fixes. Outcome: his first-time fix 61%→81%, and voluntary overtime up (a signal the internal-service-quality design worked). AI session: Researcher shadow-analysis fed behavioural-personas; Ideator concepts from "what do technicians hate" produced the tooling bundle.

6.3 The site-level cluster loophole. Two machines at one logistics site failed in the same window; the naive model counted only one breach. The fixed contract accounts at site level. Outcome: no silent contract risk in pilot; the clause became a differentiator in sales. AI session: Codex simulation found the loophole; the Prototyper's storyboard showed the customer-side discussion; GitHub issue tracked the contract change through legal.

7. SOPs for AI

Encoding pattern. Same meta-SOP; skills used: contextual-inquiry, transcript-mining, affinity-diagramming, journey-from-transcripts, behavioural-personas, evidence-gated-blueprint, service-blueprinting, good-services-audit, ecosystem-value-mapping (agreement map).

Guardrails. Outcome metrics are contract terms: fake values are a different order of failure in B2B. The platform enforced evidence refs on every contractual figure; the mock contract's numbers must trace to the simulation dataset (A7) or the critiquer rejects the artifact. Internal-service-quality data was treated as confidential; nothing about individual technician performance left the project store.

Failure modes. (1) The availability formula drifted across drafts between Define and Develop; fact-check re-gated the artifact at G3. (2) A generated persona quoted a union concern that had zero evidence rows—referential integrity failed, the row was added or the claim dropped. (3) Codex's simulation sometimes suggested more instrumentation than the collective agreement allowed; the designer enforced the boundary.

Causal logic (book 20): internal service quality (tools, parts, dispatch respect) → external service value (availability, evidence) → external outcomes (renewals, share of wallet) → profitability (services margin +31%). The KPI tree wired each link so steering reviewed causes, not just results.

9. AI tool playbook

Claude Code — agent team executing all platform skills; artifact authoring; the fact-check layer. Excelled at evidence-scale synthesis (2,847 rows); the agreement-map consolidation was its best artifact. Failed at: pricing judgment—the designer and finance owned the risk pool. OpenCode — agent-mode data engineering: 68k work-order normalization, telemetry feature extraction, simulation datasets. Excelled at volume, needed schema discipline. Codex — adversarial review: the cluster-failure loophole, dispatch misclassification (41% attribution), contract syntax. Its fastest win was re-running the published cause analysis and finding the driver was dispatch, not parts. GitHub — issues-per-design-task, artifact PR gates with Actions (schema/evidence/mermaid), steering boards. Made the Critic's vetoes auditable across two companies. SDAP — the platform that kept the four tools honest: roles, skills, catalog kinds, and deterministic fact-check.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  1. The published "parts availability" finding was wrong and Codex caught it—always keep an adversarial pass before synthesis locks.
  2. Outcome contracts are artifact-design problems; the mock contract in 5.6 survived legal review because it was machine-checkable.
  3. Technicians are the product in field service (book 20); designing with them, not for them, changed the tooling list more than any customer interview.
  4. The union boundary is design input, not an obstacle; scheduling it early cost nothing and saved the pilot.
  5. Finance will fight the risk pool; give them a simulated model, not a promise.
  6. Do not let one pilot site's organizational accident derail measurement; the site-level accounting clause exists because of it.
  7. Presenter packs from artifacts only; the first steering deck built from a chat transcript contained a stale MTTR and shook client confidence.
