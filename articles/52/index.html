01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

A global quick-service restaurant chain ran its rostering, timekeeping, breaks, leave, compliance, and payroll feed on a third-party time & attendance platform. Everyone used it, and nobody agreed on what to fix first. The vendor kept releasing its own roadmap; the business kept a backlog of desired enhancements; restaurant managers, payroll, IT, and compliance each had a different list of what mattered. A stakeholder-weighted prioritisation model built during implementation had been quietly discarded. The result: a substantial backlog, no shared way of ranking it, and a business that did not own the software it depended on.

Over 24 weeks, a two-person team ran a research campaign that replaced anecdote with a quantified map of the service. Twenty interviews spanned crew through area leaders, IT support, people-systems, and payroll across equity-owned and franchised restaurants. A role-branched survey fielded through internal collaboration channels returned 160 completed responses across six role branches — and, tellingly, zero from the area-leader and franchisee branches, a sampling gap the interviews closed instead. Site visits added what no survey can capture: paper deployment charts transcribed twice a day, whiteboards above time clocks, spreadsheet rosters, printed break sheets. A support-queue analysis of ~200 mobile-app tickets over six months quantified the failure demand.

The synthesis classified ~180 pain points across the whole lifecycle — onboarding and access through scheduling, pay, reporting, shift transfer, platform changes, offboarding — tagged by solution type (≈40% technology, ≈25% education, ≈20% integration, ≈10% process) and backlog status (≈25% already planned, ≈40% solvable but unplanned, ≈20% outside platform scope). The triage showed at a glance which problems were the vendor’s to solve, which were the business’s, and which were nobody’s because nobody had named them. Fourteen evidence-based personas, two worked user journeys, and a four-lane service blueprint followed. The engagement closed with a scored Top-10 both sides accepted, reconciled against the vendor’s release calendar — and within months the two largest live fixes (single sign-on, open shifts counted in hours) were in committed releases.

This is not a story about redesigning a product. It is a story about designing alignment: an evidence instrument that made priority disputes resolvable by data rather than by the loudest voice, and that turned a backlog the business did not own into a negotiation asset it could actually use.

What you will learn:

  • How to treat a “fix the system” brief as an alignment problem — and why the service blueprint, not a feature list, is the instrument that resolves it.
  • How a multi-method research campaign (interviews × survey × site visits × support-queue analytics) produces a pain-point taxonomy that makes prioritisation disputes resolvable by data.
  • How the workaround gallery — paper charts, whiteboards, spreadsheet rosters, phantom payroll shifts — becomes the design brief, and why “education” must be a first-class solution category.
  • How to encode the synthesis, triage, and scoring gates as SOPs an agent team can run, without surrendering the human judgments that the method depends on.
02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

The chain operates hundreds of restaurants through a mix of equity-owned and franchised outlets, each with its own payroll cycle and local ways of working. Its people operations are its largest cost surface: every restaurant runs on a roster, every roster runs on hours, and every hour ends in a pay run. The time & attendance platform — a software-as-a-service product supplied and operated by a third-party vendor — sits at the centre of that surface, spanning rostering, timekeeping, breaks, leave, compliance, and the feed into the payroll engine. It replaced a legacy time system, and most managers prefer it; the mobile app was widely described as a “game-changer.” But the platform was straining: the business had accumulated a substantial backlog of desired features across real-time data, mobile functionality, and rostering and break flexibility.

The challenge

The visible ask was a system-improvement exercise: prioritise and implement critical improvements, fix rostering and labour-cost inefficiencies, mitigate compliance risk, reduce staff frustration, and standardise processes across equity and franchised stores. But the engagement’s own framing of the problem went deeper: the core issue lay not just in the system’s current limitations, but in the lack of alignment on priorities for addressing them. That misalignment made vendor collaboration difficult and blocked strategic decisions about enhancements.

The people living with the misalignment said it plainly: the biggest pain point was prioritising development across so many stakeholders — how do you convince the core group what the priorities are? A prior stakeholder-weighted prioritisation model, built during implementation, had been thrown out. Meanwhile the compliance layer was quietly failing: managers faced an overwhelming warnings list and stopped actioning issues, and hard stops on common shift patterns forced managers into fraudulent recording just to get a pay run through. The workarounds were physical — paper deployment charts, whiteboards, spreadsheets, phantom shifts — each one evidence of a missing feature.

Constraints

  • The platform is not ours. The time & attendance platform is a SaaS product the chain does not own; the vendor releases features to all clients on its own schedule, and the chain negotiates rather than commands.
  • Global dependencies. Several services the platform depends on cannot be modified locally; the global parent controls the forecast feed — three weeks of forecast are sent when managers need four, and the transfer carries a two-hour delay.
  • Franchise autonomy. Payroll cycles, pay-period reopening policies, leave handling, and feature configurations differ by franchise group; a single feature configuration cannot be imposed top-down.
  • Regulatory layer. Enterprise-agreement interpretation, visa and minor-hour caps, the five-hour break boundary, roster-notice rules, consent requirements for overtime and public holidays, and record-retention obligations all bind the design.
  • No education platform. A quarter of the pain points would be solved by education and communication, and the business has no engagement platform to deliver them.
  • Integration latency. Role changes take up to two weeks to reach the platform; training completions sync late; reports are capped at a 30-day window; the payroll run closes on a Tuesday-morning cut-off.

Why service design

This was not a backlog-prioritisation exercise on one screen. The experience — being rostered, clocking in, taking a break, getting paid, staying compliant — is produced by a socio-technical system: crew and managers transacting in the platform, IT support feeding it, payroll and learning platforms syncing into it, a vendor releasing into it, a compliance layer policing it. The failures lived below the line of visibility and crossed every organisational boundary. That is precisely what service design exists for (See: 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md). The engagement therefore used the classic blueprint architecture — frontstage, backstage, support processes, physical evidence — to make the whole system legible in one artifact and base a defensible priority list on it. Polaine, Løvlie, and Reason’s arc from research to insight to implementation provided the governing structure (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md); the Service Profit Chain framed the economics: every hour of manager time spent transcribing, compiling, and correcting is internal service quality lost (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md).

03 · Approach

Approach: the AI-first service design process

The engagement followed the Double Diamond with a “shape then build” spine: a shaping phase (research and data collection, synthesis and analysis, a preliminary blueprint draft) followed by a building phase (a collaborative workshop, detailed mapping, validation and refinement, insight development). The first diamond widened from a single platform into the whole service ecology; the second narrowed from ~180 pain points into a scored Top-10 the business and the vendor could both defend.

A note on the AI layer, because it shapes how you read everything that follows. This engagement was deliberately research-led and human-synthesized. The automation was light: transcription, document extraction from the 70-page findings deck, survey aggregation, and a quote-verification discipline that kept every cited voice anchored to its source. There was no agent team drafting insights or running the workshops — the politics of prioritisation across restaurant, payroll, IT, and franchise stakeholders is precisely the kind of judgment that should stay human. What this article contributes is the encoding: every gate that made the work defensible — evidence extraction, pain-point triage, quote verification, prioritisation scoring — is written below as executable SOPs and agent commands, so the craft can run at agent scale next time. The method is the product; the automation is the multiplier.

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research (weeks 1–8)

Goal. Understand how the time & attendance platform contributes to the end-to-end crew experience, how it operates holistically, what pain points and workarounds exist, where the compliance gaps are, and what information is lost between the support centre and the restaurant floor (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md).

Method & SOP. Four converging methods, deliberately triangulated (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md): 20 semi-structured interviews of 10–60 minutes each spanning crew through area leaders, IT, people-systems, and payroll (equity and franchise), plus an above-restaurant round with a franchisee and two area leaders; a role-branched survey — one instrument, an initial role gate, six branch paths — fielded to ~200 targeted respondents with 160 completed; site visits and contextual inquiry at ten-plus restaurants across franchise and equity groups, watching time clocks, printed rosters, deployment whiteboards, and laminated charts; and a support-queue analysis of ~200 mobile-app tickets over six months, ~60 outstanding at one point.

AI execution. Fieldwork stayed human — consent, rapport, and the trust needed to hear about phantom-shift payroll fixes are not delegable. Everything after the recording was lightly automated: transcripts became structured write-ups on a standard template, a quote-verification pass — the engagement’s key hallucination control — checked every cited voice against the source, the survey platform aggregated the role-branched responses, and open-text answers ran through the same pain-point schema as the interviews.

Human designer judgment. Sampling design (branching the survey by role), every live interview, walking the restaurant floor rather than trusting the ticket queue, and the early read that the survey’s zero-response branches were a sampling signal, not a finding — area leaders and franchisees do not fill in surveys, so interviews re-covered those roles.

Artifact produced. Research plan and role-branched guides, evidence register, ~180 pain points tagged with source and role, survey dataset (N=160), support-queue theme analysis, workaround gallery.

4.2 Synthesis (weeks 8–12)

Goal. Convert the evidence stack into insights that pass the acid test: each one must change what the chain designs next (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md).

Method & SOP. Bottom-up affinity synthesis on a shared wall: the designer and client stakeholders worked the wall while the spreadsheet pipeline maintained the digital twin. The pain-point taxonomy was the load-bearing move — every pain point classified twice: once by solution type (technology, education, integration, process) and once by backlog status (already planned, solvable but unplanned, outside platform scope). The distribution that emerged reframed the whole engagement: ≈40% of pain points were technology, ≈25% education, ≈20% integration, ≈10% process — a quarter of the fix was never going to come from the vendor at all.

AI execution. The extraction agent’s first-pass clustering proposed 40+ candidate themes; the human team merged, renamed, and re-clustered freely, with the agent re-propagating edits between sessions. Every insight card used the standard three-part syntax — observed behaviour → root cause → design implication — and had to survive an adversarial challenge pass: a reviewer asked to attack, not agree, checking each cited voice existed, arguing the strongest alternative explanation, and flagging implications the evidence did not imply. Two candidate insights were killed or rewritten in this pass.

Human designer judgment. Naming insights is framing, and framing is political. The team headlined “alert fatigue” over “manager non-compliance,” and “crew are blind to their own data” over “crew don’t use self-service” — each choice redirected blame from people toward the system that shaped their behaviour, and each survived the evidence.

The eight insights (compressed):

Six design principles followed: D1 Evidence before roadmap. D2 The compliant path must be cheaper than the workaround. D3 Live data or nothing. D4 Crew see their own data. D5 Few hard stops, visible warnings. D6 Standardise the platform, vary the tail. Fourteen evidence-based personas were built as “people characterised by an interpretation of evidence” — crew, managers, area leaders, franchisees, and payroll — each anchored to the survey segments and interview voices behind it.

4.3 Mapping (weeks 12–16)

Goal. Build the two alignment artifacts everything else hangs on: the current-state journey maps (what it is like to be a crew member or manager inside the system) and the future-state service blueprint (how the whole organisation produces that experience) (See: 09-mapping-experiences/04-customer-journey-maps.md, 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md).

Method & SOP. Current-state journey mapping from tagged evidence, with phases defined by user goals — onboarding and access, scheduling, pay, reporting, shift transfer, platform changes, offboarding — never internal stages; pain points strictly separated from opportunities. Then future-state blueprinting from the validated journey spine, organised around the classic four lanes: frontstage, backstage, support processes, and physical evidence. Two worked user journeys were walked through with stakeholders as validation devices: clocking in and out (registration failures, PIN resets, the five-hour break bug, break-remaining invisibility, no punch history) and correcting a missed shift in a closed pay period (the refusal to reopen, the phantom-shift workaround, its compliance ripple effects).

AI execution. Diagrams were authored as version-controlled files and diffed in review like software. During the blueprint rehearsal — a column-by-column walkthrough with operations, IT, and payroll — every unanswerable question (“where does the payroll feed show that a correction was applied?”) became a tracked gap, later resolved into a roadmap item or an explicit “out of scope” decision. An adversarial review caught four genuine defects before the roadmap was built, including a missing exception path when a correction is rejected and an unstaffed handoff between restaurant managers and payroll at cut-off.

Human designer judgment. Choosing where the line of visibility sits — crew-facing data (punch times, break countdowns, leave balances, expected pay) made visible; payroll-engine internals kept backstage. Also: refusing to split the blueprint per system or franchise group, which would have re-siloed the artifact and re-created the alignment problem it existed to solve.

Artifacts produced. Two user journeys, the four-lane blueprint across seven lifecycle stages with fail points F-01…F-08, the stakeholder map, and the blueprint-gap ledger.

4.4 Ideation & prioritisation (weeks 16–20)

Goal. Convert insights into a defensible, scored priority list — not a pile of ideas, and not another model that gets thrown in the bin (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/05-developing-ideas.md).

Method & SOP. Structured “how might we” formulation from insight cards, then a scoring workshop with the platform user group: every candidate scored against agreed criteria, compliance ranked first by standing rule. Output: a Top-10 with a weighted priority index, reconciled item-by-item against the vendor’s 2024 and 2025 release calendars.

AI execution. The scoring sheet was automated — criteria, weights, and scores in one table so the workshop could re-rank live. The reconciliation table (Top-10 × vendor roadmap × backlog status) came from the same triage data, so every roadmap decision traced to evidence. A second-opinion pass reviewed the Top-10 for coherence and caught one omission (leave shifts on the mobile shift list, demanded by both survey and ticket data, had fallen out of the draft).

Human designer judgment. The pivotal trade-off of the engagement: the vendor had flagged that 100,000+ rostered shifts sat at exactly the five-hour boundary where a break is triggered. A hard stop would have wrecked thousands of shifts a week and, per the evidence, pushed managers into fraudulent recording. The compromise: a visible warning instead of a hard stop, leaving an audit record while guiding rather than handcuffing. D5 in practice, and the blueprint’s stated philosophy: very few hard stops (visa caps being one), visible warnings everywhere else.

Artifact produced. The Top-10 scored roadmap (Section 5, A8), the reconciliation table, and the stated prioritisation criteria — a defensible, repeated, documented model this time, not a one-off weighting exercise.

4.5 Prototyping & pilots (weeks 18–22)

Goal. Test the riskiest assumptions at the lowest fidelity that answers the question — and, where the fix was already available, run it as a live pilot (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/06-prototyping-methods.md).

Method & SOP. Progressive, evidence-driven pilots rather than a build:

  • Single sign-on rollout (the largest live fix pilot). The support queue’s top cluster — mobile registration failures where an email address blocked shift visibility — traced to the registration flow. Single sign-on went live network-wide and became the fastest, highest-leverage fix of the engagement: the “can’t see my shifts” cluster collapsed.
  • Open shifts counted in hours (next-release fix). Hours calculations silently excluded open shifts; the fix was committed to the next vendor release, closing a gap that made every roster estimate wrong.
  • Manager clock-in for pay (behavioural-change trial). Salaried managers clocking in through the platform was trialled by two franchise groups with a third testing — a payroll-stakes behaviour change needing a benchmark and at least three months’ notice before rollout.
  • The five-hour boundary compromise. The soft-warning option was accepted by the vendor with the risk quantified against the 100,000+ affected shifts.
  • The five-minute training module. A crew member explicitly valued micro-training; the finding seeded the education strand and its delivery format.
  • User-journey validation. The two worked journeys were walked through with stakeholders as synthesis test-beds, confirming pains and gaps per stage before finalising the blueprint.

AI execution. Pilot readouts re-ran the research pipelines — survey responses and ticket themes for the pilot period — to check the fixes moved the clusters they were meant to move. The role-branched survey also functioned as a sampling pilot: its zero-response branches showed where self-selection fails, and the interview correction became a documented method lesson.

Human designer judgment. Sequencing — fixing the registration cluster before the roadmap was final, because the evidence said it was the highest-leverage live fix — and the deliberate decision not to pursue geo-location clocking: location-based punch-in would read as surveillance and damage the trust the mobile app had earned.

Artifacts produced. Pilot readouts for SSO and open-shift counting, the trial benchmark for manager clock-in, the quantified soft-warning compromise, and the micro-training module spec.

4.6 Implementation & roadmap (weeks 20–24)

Goal. Turn the validated findings into a roadmap the business can execute and a negotiation the business can win (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/07-implementation.md).

Method & SOP. Blueprint-to-backlog translation. Every blueprint step and fail point mapped to roadmap items; every roadmap item tagged with its evidence and its backlog status. The triage — ≈25% already planned, ≈40% solvable but unplanned, ≈20% outside platform scope — became the vendor-negotiation instrument: it separated, at a glance, what the vendor should own, what the business should own, and what needed a different owner entirely (education delivery, process change, other platforms).

AI execution. Roadmap tables were generated from the triage data with full traceability (item → insight → evidence). Release communication was templated and routed through the platform user group and franchise network download channels, so the Top-10’s rationale travelled with the roadmap, not behind it.

Human designer judgment. Where to draw the standardisation boundary: the blueprint deliberately scopes “everything up until the manual adjustments” — payroll tails differ by franchise by design, and only the enterprise-agreement interpretation is uniform. Drawing that line kept the blueprint from being blocked by franchise politics, and it is the single most important scoping decision in the engagement. Also: rejecting a paid per-store dashboard add-on (~$20 per store per month) for an in-house report build — a make-versus-buy call the evidence made easy.

Artifacts produced. 2024 and 2025 draft roadmaps, the Top-10 with release windows, the comms plan, and the living-document charter: half-yearly reviews keep the blueprint current rather than a one-off deck.

4.7 Measurement (weeks 24 and beyond)

Accurate, compliant pay every cycle, first time.

The north-star metric of the KPI tree

Goal. Close the loop with a measurement instrument — and be honest about what is not yet measured (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md).

AI execution. The KPI tree was designed as part of the living document: a north-star metric (accurate, compliant pay every cycle, first time), driver metrics (data freshness, correction volume, leave automation, self-service adoption, exception rate), and lagging metrics (support-ticket volume, payroll query load, compliance exceptions, manager time on administration), on a half-yearly cadence.

Human designer judgment, and the honest gap. No formal post-implementation KPI or adoption metrics were documented in the engagement’s own folder — the blueprint shipped, the Top-10 was accepted, the first fixes went live, but the measurement loop was never closed in writing. This is the design’s unfinished loop and the single most important lesson for the next engagement: build the measurement instrument into the blueprint from week 1, not as a follow-up. 06 · Use cases

Use cases

Use case A — A new crew member’s first week of clocking in

Scenario. Dana (persona 1), 19, is hired for evenings and weekends. She registers from the link in her welcome message — and, before the redesign, a subtle registration trap meant a mis-typed email could look like a successful sign-up while silently blocking all shift visibility. The support queue’s largest mobile cluster was built from exactly this failure, multiplied by every new starter, every week.

What was designed. Single sign-on replaces the fragile registration path: Dana signs in once with her existing identity, her roles and shifts follow from her employee record, and the “can’t see my shifts” failure class disappears at the root. Her first week runs on the crew-visibility spine — roster, punch times, and, once the roadmap lands, a break countdown, expected pay, and leave balance.

AI working session (encoded). The support-queue theme analysis that sized this failure class ran as a repeatable extraction job — ticket titles and categories aggregated by theme and role, so the fix aimed at the largest cluster, not the loudest. In agentic mode the same job watches the queue post-rollout and reports whether the cluster collapsed.

Outcome. The registration cluster — the top mobile-app ticket theme for six months — collapsed after single sign-on went live; crew had already rated clocking in “easy” or “very easy” at roughly 8 in 10, so the design work concentrated on the long tail of failures the survey alone could not see.

Use case B — A manager correcting a missed shift

Scenario. A crew member’s clock-in was missed on a busy Friday. The compliant correction — reopening the pay period — is effectively unavailable: pay periods are almost never reopened, and the back-office process is slow enough that managers improvise. The documented workaround: add the missed pay as a phantom shift in the next week — rippling into tax, superannuation, and benefit outcomes for part-timers. This is the clearest case in the whole engagement of a rule creating its own violation: the system made the non-compliant path easier than the compliant one.

What was designed. Correction as a first-class flow, per I-08 and D2: a pay-period reopening review with a named owner and target turnaround; a consent workflow so the crew member agrees to the correction; a “change shift to leave shift” action so a dropped shift becomes leave with or without pay instead of a phantom; and auto-leave for dropped shifts that protects part-timers’ contracted hours. The goal was structural: make the compliant path cheaper than the workaround, so the workaround stops being rational.

AI working session (encoded). The consent workflow’s wording and the reopening review’s SLA were drafted from the payroll interviews and tested against the part-timer compliance cases; an adversarial pass checked the new flow did not re-create a hard stop that would force phantom-shift behaviour elsewhere.

Outcome. Three Top-10 items trace to this use case (reopening review, consent workflow, change-shift-to-leave); the phantom-shift workaround — documented in payroll and manager interviews across equity and franchise — is the KPI the half-yearly reviews will track.

Use case C — Shared employees across the network

Scenario. A crew member works across two restaurants in the same franchise group — common for students and holiday peaks. Their punches, breaks, and leave live in one restaurant’s view; the other restaurant’s manager cannot see them. Compliance checks spanning both sites are done by hand, and role changes take up to two weeks to propagate — during which the employee is rostered under the wrong role, with compliance consequences.

What was designed. A shared-employee view: punches and leave visible across the restaurants the person actually works for; cross-schedule compliance that spans sites; and role-propagation latency treated as a first-class integration defect, not an accepted delay. The principle in play is D3 — the data has to be live and complete wherever the person works, or every downstream decision (staffing, pay, compliance) is built on a partial picture.

AI working session (encoded). The role-propagation and training-sync lags were quantified from ticketing and interview evidence into the integration strand of the triage — the ≈20% of pain points that are neither vendor features nor business education but plumbing.

Outcome. Shared-employee punches and leave made the Top-10; cross-schedule compliance was already on the vendor’s release calendar and moved into the reconciliation table as a joint item.

Use case D — Planning a week of leave

Scenario. A rostering manager approves leave for a crew member taking a week off. Before the redesign, that week meant entering every shift of leave by hand — “painful” was the word used — while approving without accurate balances, which updated only per pay run. The crew member had no mobile view of pending or approved leave, and payroll absorbed a steady stream of balance enquiries.

What was designed. The leave lifecycle automated end to end: leave auto-populated onto the schedule from the approved request; a consent workflow capturing the crew member’s agreement (including leave with or without pay for dropped shifts); real-time balance visibility; and leave shifts on the mobile shift list. Four of the ten Top-10 items trace to this use case — the clearest demonstration that a “feature-shaped hole” (I-07) is a cluster of small design decisions, not one ticket.

AI working session (encoded). The leave items were traced to evidence across interviews, survey open text, and ticket themes before scoring; the scoring sheet kept the four items ranked together rather than fragmented across workshops.

Outcome. Four Top-10 items, one design principle (D2), and a measurable relief target for payroll’s leave-enquiry volume at the half-yearly review.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Three SOPs were encoded, each following the same pattern: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate. In this engagement the steps ran as human-plus-spreadsheet work with light automation; each is written here so an agent team can run the identical gate at scale.

  1. Interview-to-evidence SOP (research). Output: structured write-ups and evidence items. Acceptance: every quoted voice verbatim-verifiable against the source; every pain point carries role type, service stage, and source method. Gate: human spot-check of 10% before merge. The prompt template:
NOTE
You are EVIDENCE-EXTRACTOR. Input: one interview write-up.
Extract discrete pain points. For each item output EXACTLY:
- painpoint_id: ta-<seq>
- role_type: crew | shift_supervisor | assistant_manager |
             general_manager | area_leader | it_support | payroll | people_systems
- service_stage: onboarding | scheduling | pay | reporting |
                 shift_transfer | platform_changes | offboarding
- type: verbatim_quote | observed_behaviour | operational_fact | workaround
- text: the pain statement (never paraphrase a quote)
- severity: 1-5 (participant-rated only; leave blank if never rated)
- workaround: describe any workaround the participant uses, or blank
RULES: quote text must be copy-paste exact from the write-up; if unsure,
drop the item. Never invent a pain point. Output JSONL. Every item must
trace to a source line range you include as source_lines.
  1. Pain-point triage SOP (synthesis). Steps: load evidence items → classify each by solution category (technology / education / integration / process) → classify each by backlog status (already planned / solvable but unplanned / outside platform scope) → reconcile with the support-queue and survey data → report orphans, never drop them. Acceptance: dual-coded subset agreement ≥ 90%; every category decision explainable in one sentence. Gate: human review of the category definitions before coding starts — if coders disagree more than 10% of the time, the definitions are wrong, not the coders.
  2. Prioritisation SOP (ideation → roadmap). Steps: map every roadmap candidate to its insight ID and evidence count → score against the agreed criteria with compliance first → reconcile the Top-10 against the vendor’s release calendar item by item → mark each item’s backlog status → draft the release-communication summary. Acceptance: no item without an insight ID; every vendor-reconciled change annotated; the triage totals consistent with the source table. Gate: scoring workshop sign-off plus an adversarial coherence pass (see failure modes below).

The meta-SOP. The engagement ran research → synthesise → map → prioritise → pilot → deliver, with a hard gate between stages: no synthesis before the evidence register passed spot-check; no blueprint before the insight cards survived the challenge pass; no roadmap before the blueprint rehearsal closed its gap ledger; no comms before the roadmap reconciled with the vendor calendar. Each gate was a review with a named decider — enforced by discipline and a shared evidence table, not memory.

Guardrails.

  • Data minimisation. Agents never see real names: write-ups were pseudonymised to role + code before any automated processing, and the sample dataset ships with transformed values only.
  • Hallucination control. Quote verification runs on every synthesis pass: a quoted string not found in the source fails the check. Insights without evidence IDs fail the triage schema.
  • Workaround as first-class evidence. The triage schema has a dedicated workaround field — the method’s single highest-leverage invention was treating workarounds as design input, not as anecdotes.
  • Human sign-off points. Interview conduct; insight naming; category definitions; the scoring workshop; the hard-stop-versus-warning compromise; the standardisation boundary; release communication.

Failure modes observed. (1) Over-clustering: the first automated pass proposed 40+ candidate themes with near-duplicates; fixed with active-sentence cluster names plus a similarity merge. (2) Sampling mirage: the survey’s zero-response branches could have been read as “these roles have no pain points”; the triangulation rule — every role covered by at least two methods — caught it and interviews re-covered both roles. (3) Research hygiene: one interview file was mislabelled, a personas deck from another project sat in the folder, and a feedback workbook was empty; each had to be flagged for downstream use rather than silently relied on. (4) Phantom errors: tickets described failures that only reproduced under specific version combinations; the analysis had to separate genuine defects from upgrade artefacts before they polluted the roadmap.

Causal logic (Service Profit Chain). The chain ran as Heskett, Sasser, and Schlesinger describe (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md): internal service quality (live data, fewer workarounds, a defensible roadmap, a negotiable vendor relationship) raised frontline capability; external service value rose chiefly through the denominator of the value equation — access costs collapsed for the people inside the service (minutes of manual transcription per day, days of stale data, the cost of not seeing your own hours or fixing an error without breaking a rule). Crew satisfaction, manager time, and payroll reliability followed; reduced failure demand — support tickets, payroll queries, special pay runs, compliance exceptions — produced the economics. The gap is stated plainly in Section 4.7: the folder holds no formal post-implementation KPI or adoption metrics, so the “after” column above is the roadmap’s target state, not a measured claim.

KPI tree (designed for the living document). North star: every crew member paid accurately, on time, first time — without anyone breaking a rule to get there. Drivers: data freshness (age of labour figures), correction volume (phantom shifts, reopen requests), leave automation (manual leave entries), crew self-service adoption, exception rate (compliance issues ÷ published shifts — the area leader’s manual spot-check, automated). Lagging: support-ticket volume, payroll query load, compliance exceptions, manager hours on administration. Reviewed half-yearly per the living-document charter.

09 · AI tools

AI tool playbook

Honest inventory first. This engagement’s toolset was deliberately light — four tools of record, none of them an agent platform: the role-branched survey platform (the 160-response instrument with six branch paths), the ticketing system (the support-queue analysis), the spreadsheet pipeline (evidence register, triage table, scoring sheet — the system of record), and document-extraction automation (deck and slide extraction so the 70-page findings report and interview material were machine-searchable). Synthesis, insight framing, and prioritisation stayed human — the politics of prioritisation across restaurant, payroll, IT, and franchise stakeholders is where delegation fails.

What follows is the same playbook re-expressed for an agentic practice — the engagement’s gates encoded as commands and configuration so the craft survives into a team that runs with agents. Commands encode the same steps; live case execution is unverified.

Extractor — pain-point triage. The highest-leverage automation in the whole engagement: turning 20 write-ups and 160 survey responses into a single triage table, traceable and dual-coded. In agentic mode the extraction, coding, and reconciliation run as one job:

TERMINAL
opencode run --agent extractor "Run the pain-point triage SOP over
interviews/*.md and survey-open-text.csv. Output painpoint-triage.csv with
solution_category and backlog_status per row, source_methods attached.
Then dual-code a random 10% subset against the category definitions and
report the disagreement rate. Anything ambiguous goes to orphans.csv,
never silently dropped."

Critic — the adversarial evidence pass. Every insight card and every roadmap claim had to survive an attack pass. In agentic mode this is a named critic role with a specific brief, run on the artifact plus a concern, never on the whole project:

TERMINAL
codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read synthesis/insight-cards.md and
evidence/evidence-register.jsonl. For each insight: (1) verify every cited
voice exists in the register, (2) argue the strongest alternative
explanation for the observed behaviour, (3) flag implications the evidence
does not actually imply, (4) check the triage categories are used
consistently. Be adversarial."

Reconciler — roadmap scoring and vendor reconciliation. The scoring sheet and the Top-10 × vendor-calendar reconciliation are deterministic once the criteria and evidence are agreed — precisely what agents are good at:

TERMINAL
opencode run --agent reconciler "Read triage/painpoint-triage.csv and
roadmap/candidates.md. Score each candidate against the agreed criteria
(compliance first). Reconcile the resulting Top-10 against vendor-roadmap.csv
item by item; mark shared, vendor-owned, and business-owned items. Flag any
candidate with no insight ID or no evidence count."

Gate configuration. The artifact repository ran the equivalent of a CI gate on every synthesis change — CSV schema check, quote verification, category consistency. Illustrative GitHub Actions configuration — synthetic repository workflow; live execution unverified:

CONFIG
name: artifact-gate
on: [pull_request]
jobs:
  validate:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - name: Triage CSV schema check
        run: python scripts/check_schema.py data/painpoint-triage.csv
      - name: Quote verification

What each tool was for, and where the human stayed. The survey platform handled branching and aggregation; the ticketing system supplied the failure-demand spine; the spreadsheet pipeline was the system of record — but the judgment calls were human: the alignment reframe, the hard-stop-versus-warning compromise, the standardisation boundary, every interview. The tools made the evidence legible; the humans made the decisions the evidence could not make for them.

10 · Lessons

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • The real problem was alignment, not the platform. The client’s own framing — “the core issue lies not just in the system’s current limitations, but in the lack of alignment on priorities” — turned out to be the entire brief. Every design decision that followed served that reframe. A feature-fix campaign would have shipped random improvements and left the stalemate intact.
  • Education is a first-class solution category — with no delivery vehicle. ≈25% of the pain points were solvable with training and communication, not code: feature availability varies store to store with no baseline, most managers had never heard of the school calendar, and report literacy is low. The blueprint named the gap the business had never named: there is no engagement platform to deliver the education strand.
  • Rules create their own workarounds; design the compliant path to be cheaper. The 24-hour swap-notice rule pushed crew to drop-and-repick instead of swapping; the hard stop would have “handcuffed a manager and forced them to record fraudulently”; the pay-period rigidity produced phantom shifts. Each was a system-design decision before it was a behaviour problem. The fix is structural: make the compliant path cheaper than the workaround, then the workaround stops being rational.
  • The workaround gallery is the design brief. Paper deployment charts transcribed twice a day, whiteboards above time clocks, spreadsheet rosters, printed break sheets, phantom payroll shifts — the physical evidence of missing functionality. Every recurring artifact mapped to a roadmap item.
  • Prioritisation models get discarded unless they are defensible and repeated. The stakeholder-weighted model from implementation “was thrown in the bin.” The Top-10 scoring — compliance-first, published criteria, full evidence traceability — is the attempt that survived — and its living-document cadence is what keeps it from the bin next time.
  • Standardisation vs franchise autonomy is a scoping decision, not a settings problem. The blueprint deliberately ends “everything up until the manual adjustments”: payroll tails differ by franchise by design, and only the enterprise-agreement interpretation is uniform. Drawing that line explicitly prevented the blueprint from being blocked by franchise politics.
  • Data credibility is a UX issue. The payroll-vs-platform figure mismatch recurred across area-leader, payroll, and manager interviews — “the figures I get are not the figures I see in the system.” The team treated system-data credibility as a design surface, not an IT complaint.
  • Research hygiene is a real risk in real folders. A mislabelled interview file, a personas deck from a different project, and an empty feedback workbook all had to be flagged for downstream use. Any agentic pipeline inherits this risk — the orphan-report and dual-coding gates exist precisely because folders lie.
  • Integration latency is structural, so treat data flow as design. The two-hour forecast delay, the up-to-two-week role-change lag, the daily learning-platform sync — consequences of who owns each system, not accidents. The blueprint’s integration strand (≈20% of pain points) is the design surface that most engagements never draw.
  • Done differently next time: build the measurement instrument into week 1 (the KPI tree was designed at the end and never measured in writing); name the education delivery vehicle in the roadmap, not as a footnote; and baseline the Good Services 15-principles scorecard early so the before/after has comparative precision (See: 05-good-services/02-the-fifteen-principles.md).
