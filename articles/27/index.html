Service-Dominant Logic in B2B: Value Co-Creation at Forge Industrial

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Forge Industrial is a fictional composite of European industrial equipment makers with large aftermarket businesses. All account names, plant identifiers, figures, and datasets in this article are illustrative and anonymized. This article continues the Forge thread from article 01 and article 18: article 01 improved quoting throughput, article 18 funded the Predictive Care concept, and this article explains the Service-Dominant Logic architecture required to make that concept commercially and operationally credible. The engagement was run AI-first: Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were the primary production tools, with the human designer owning framing, contract boundaries, ethics, and final decisions.

1. Executive summary

After Forge Industrial funded Predictive Care in article 18, the harder question appeared immediately: how should a manufacturer that historically sold machines, spare parts, and reactive field service redesign its business model around value co-created in customer plants? Over a 20-week programme, we used Service-Dominant Logic (SDL) as the strategic lens. We mapped the foundational premises against Forge's current aftermarket model, separated value propositions from value facilitation and true co-creation, redesigned the maintenance journey into an outcome-partnership blueprint, and operationalized the model with AI-built telemetry dashboards, shared playbooks, and joint decision loops. The result was two live outcome-partner pilots — SKAUTO42 finishing week 20 at 97.6% uptime, 74.0% OEE, and 98.4% availability, and ESROLL14 finishing at 98.7% uptime, 79.8% OEE, 99.2% availability, and 6.1 kWh per part — plus one dealer charter for mid-market coverage.

2. The brief

Client portrait

Forge Industrial is a fictional pan-European manufacturer of forming, thermal-processing, and materials-handling equipment sold into automotive, metals, packaging, and advanced manufacturing plants. Annual revenue is approximately €3.4 billion, with €1.05 billion from aftermarket service, parts, retrofits, and service contracts. The installed base spans about 62,000 connected or connectable assets, supported by 2,400 field technicians, 310 remote support specialists, and 11 regional parts nodes. The installed base is technically rich, but the commercial model remained mixed: machines were still sold with a goods-dominant mindset, while service contracts were often reactive add-ons rather than the center of the value proposition. See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/02-service-dominant-thinking.md.

The challenge

Article 18 solved one problem and exposed another. Forge could now produce a stronger service concept brief, finance-reviewed economics, and a credible pilot plan for Predictive Care. But the concept still risked being implemented with old habits:

  • Sales still described the offer as "monitoring plus service response," which sounded like an equipment feature bundle rather than a value co-creation proposition.
  • Contract drafts used supplier-centric language such as "Forge shall deliver uptime improvements," which violates FP7 because the firm can offer only a value proposition, not unilateral value delivery. See: 12-service-dominant-logic/02-the-foundational-premises.md.
  • Service operations lacked a clear boundary between what Forge facilitated backstage, what the customer plant had to contribute, and which moments required direct joint co-creation.
  • Dealer-led markets created institutional friction: incentives rewarded parts sales and emergency visits more than planned interventions, even where predictive service would create better joint value.
  • The board wanted outcome partnerships built on the Predictive Care foundation: availability contracts for auto plants, energy-per-part pilots for metals customers, and a dealer-compatible model for mid-market accounts.

The practical design question was therefore not "what new dashboard should Forge launch?" It was: how should Forge redesign its resource mix, interaction rules, metrics, and institutional arrangements so that uptime, OEE, and energy performance are co-created with customers in use? See: 12-service-dominant-logic/03-value-co-creation.md; 19-service-management-and-marketing/09-from-management-to-logic.md.

Constraints

The programme had real operational and institutional limits. Forge could not offer blanket uptime guarantees for failures driven by customer scheduling decisions, operator misuse, or third-party utilities. Legacy ERP and field-service systems had to remain in place, with thin telemetry integrations only. Several plants would share machine and OEE data only under strict cyber-review rules. Two countries in the target region were served through dealers whose margin model favored parts and breakdown response. Legal also required unambiguous language on exclusions, shared responsibilities, and data retention. These are not side conditions in SDL; they are part of the institutional arrangements that coordinate co-creation. See: 12-service-dominant-logic/04-service-ecosystems.md; 12-service-dominant-logic/05-innovation-and-institutions.md.

Why service design

This was a service design problem because value creation crossed multiple actors, channels, and decision layers: plant maintenance, production planning, Forge remote diagnostics, field operations, parts logistics, legal, dealers, and OEM knowledge. Goods-dominant logic would have responded with better dashboards and more telemetry. Service design, informed by SDL, instead asks how actors integrate resources over time, where value is experienced, which interactions are true co-creation moments, and what rules make the system work repeatedly. Grönroos's service logic makes the managerial point sharply: customers create value in their own processes, firms facilitate that creation backstage, and co-creation happens only in direct interactions. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/09-from-management-to-logic.md. Kimbell's service-dominant thinking then turns that theory into practical design moves: resource integration mapping, value proposition reframing, and outcome-focused concepts. See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/02-service-dominant-thinking.md.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Discover

Goal. Understand how customer plants currently create value, where Forge helps, where it merely ships resources, and where the current model destroys value.

Method & SOP. We combined two methods. First, customer process and value-in-use mapping: the team observed how heads of maintenance, planners, and reliability engineers actually recover uptime in daily practice. Second, a facilitation-versus-co-creation audit: every touchpoint was classified as customer independent use, provider backstage facilitation, or direct joint interaction. This distinction matters because firms too easily label every touchpoint "co-creation" and lose analytical precision. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/09-from-management-to-logic.md. The SOP was: define the beneficiary in context; map plant goals and breakdowns; catalog private, market, and public resources; identify value-destruction points; and quantify the operational baseline. See: 12-service-dominant-logic/03-value-co-creation.md; 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/02-service-dominant-thinking.md.

AI execution. Claude Code orchestrated the qualitative side. One agent summarized each interview batch into a structured value ledger with five fields: plant goal, resource integrated, friction, interaction type, and value signal. OpenCode handled the telemetry and service-history merge for the three pilot accounts, including OEE and energy baselines. Representative prompt and command:

You are SDL-AUDITOR.
Input: interview transcripts, visit notes, weekly maintenance reviews.
For each touchpoint classify it as:
1. customer value creation in use
2. Forge value facilitation backstage
3. direct joint co-creation encounter
4. value co-destruction risk
Rules:
- no claim without quote id or source row
- do not call a touchpoint co-creation unless direct interaction changes the customer's process
- name the beneficiary-defined metric at risk
opencode run --agent telemetry-pipeline "Read pilot/value_use_baseline.csv, pilot/service_orders_18m.csv,
pilot/oee_exports.csv and pilot/energy_logs.csv. Output per plant:
1) uptime baseline
2) OEE baseline
3) availability baseline
4) energy per part baseline
5) emergency freight incidents
Reject any aggregate that does not reconcile to the named plant totals."

GitHub issues tracked each unresolved question: beneficiary-metric-definition, dealer-role-boundary, energy-baseline-governance, and contract-exclusion-language.

Human designer judgment. The designer made the key boundary call: Forge should not promise outcomes on variables the plant controls alone. Production sequencing, operator discipline, and shutdown windows materially shape value-in-use. That is why the later contract model distinguishes co-owned outcomes from supplier-only service levels.

Artifact produced. A current-state maintenance journey, baseline metrics for three pilot accounts, and a value-risk register showing that the plant's pain was not lack of data alone; it was lack of a shared operating model for acting on data.

4.2 Define

Goal. Turn the research baseline into an explicit SDL architecture: what is exchanged, who integrates which resources, how value is determined, and which institutions govern the exchange.

Method & SOP. The Define phase used four linked diagnostics. First, an FP audit applied FP1 through FP5 and FP10 directly to Forge's business model. Second, resource integration mapping identified plant private resources, Forge market resources, and public or ecosystem resources such as safety rules, dealer coverage, and OEM knowledge. Third, a phenomenological value audit replaced internal metrics such as "alerts sent" with beneficiary metrics such as stability of production windows and confidence in planned maintenance. Fourth, an institution audit mapped the regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive arrangements that had to change for outcome contracts to work. See: 12-service-dominant-logic/02-the-foundational-premises.md; 12-service-dominant-logic/04-service-ecosystems.md; 12-service-dominant-logic/05-innovation-and-institutions.md.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the foundational-premises map, the first resource-integration model, and the stakeholder and ecosystem diagrams. The prompt was intentionally doctrinal, because loose language here would corrupt the whole article:

You are SDL-ARCHITECT.
Apply FP1 to FP5 and FP10 to Forge Predictive Care.
Output:
- one premise to business-model implication line each
- one statement on what value is proposed
- one statement on how the beneficiary determines value in use
Rules:
- never say Forge delivers value
- treat machines and telemetry as service distribution mechanisms
- name operant resources before operand resources
- show where customer and partner resources are required

Codex then acted as hostile reviewer on the value equation and servitization analogies:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Review architecture/value_model.md and contract/value_equation.md.
1) Flag every sentence that implies unilateral value delivery.
2) Attack the servitization analogies for overreach.
3) Check whether the value equation double-counts downtime avoidance, margin gain, or energy savings.
Return only defects and missing evidence."

Human designer judgment. The central reframing sentence came from the human, not the models: Forge is not selling a more intelligent machine; it is inviting the plant into a better-maintained production system with explicit roles, metrics, and review rituals. That sentence settled the language for legal, sales, and operations.

Artifact produced. The FP map, the value-in-use loop, stakeholder and ecosystem views, and the institutional barrier list that later drove contract and governance design.

4.3 Develop

Goal. Convert the SDL architecture into a future-state service system: contract logic, blueprint, dashboard, playbooks, and a joint operating model.

Method & SOP. We used value proposition reframing, service blueprinting, and operating-model design. The contract moved from a reactive T&M template to an outcome-partnership structure with four sections: beneficiary outcome and context, Forge resource commitments, customer resource commitments, and joint governance and exclusions. Blueprinting then showed where customer actions, Forge frontstage, backstage systems, and ecosystem supports must align. The operating model became a shared gameboard: one risk queue, one action list, one cadence, one decision-rights charter. See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/02-service-dominant-thinking.md; 12-service-dominant-logic/03-value-co-creation.md.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the outcome-contract skeleton and the value-in-use measurement model. OpenCode built the co-creation dashboard and the uptime telemetry pipeline that fed the weekly and monthly reviews. Representative command:

opencode run --agent app-builder "Build a pilot dashboard for two outcome accounts.
Inputs: uptime, OEE, availability, emergency freight, energy per part, action log.
Views required:
1) plant summary card
2) risk queue by asset
3) joint action tracker
4) monthly value review export
Rules:
- show provider and customer obligations side by side
- every KPI must display source freshness
- no green status if customer data feed is stale"

GitHub hosted a shared repo named forge-outcome-playbooks. Each pilot account had its own folder containing playbooks, decision logs, and review packs. Plant stakeholders were added as reviewers on markdown playbooks, so a production planner could challenge a threshold change in the same place a Forge remote diagnostician proposed it.

Human designer judgment. The key choice was to keep the first contracts modest. We explicitly avoided language that implied total control over uptime. The outcome partnership used a shared metric and bonus-malus discussion, but the enforceable supplier obligations stayed tied to response, diagnosis, parts readiness, planned intervention discipline, and review cadence. That restraint increased trust.

Artifact produced. The future-state blueprint, the co-creation operating model, the first shared dashboard, the redline checklist for contract language, and the mock dataset used to validate metric logic.

4.4 Deliver

Goal. Run the governance loop, confirm early value-in-use movement, and decide what can scale.

Method & SOP. Delivery meant institutional work as much as implementation. The team staged new review rituals, new incentive language, and new data-sharing rules. Weekly risk reviews handled operational moves; monthly value reviews tested whether the service was changing plant performance; quarterly business reviews handled scope, commercial terms, and dealer alignment. This was service innovation as institutionalization, not invention. See: 12-service-dominant-logic/05-innovation-and-institutions.md.

AI execution. Claude Code generated the weekly review pack and drafted revision proposals to the contract schedule when observed behavior suggested ambiguity. GitHub pull requests became the formal gate for playbook changes. One PR would typically include: a threshold change, a new action owner, a revised exclusion note, and a before/after metric snapshot. Codex reviewed the value equation before the board readout and flagged one double count where reduced emergency freight had already been included inside margin improvement.

You are OUTCOME-CONTRACT-DRAFTER.
Input: approved blueprint, decision-rights charter, baseline metrics, exclusions.
Draft only clauses for:
- joint objective and beneficiary context
- Forge commitments that are operationally controllable
- customer commitments required for value creation
- governance cadence and evidence pack
- exclusions and re-baselining rules
Forbidden phrases:
- guarantee uninterrupted production
- deliver value
- eliminate all downtime
- savings assured regardless of customer behavior

Human designer judgment. The designer rejected a sales request to include a blanket 3% OEE uplift promise. The evidence supported a directional improvement range under defined conditions, not an unconditional guarantee. That decision protected the commercial model from value co-destruction later.

Artifact produced. Two live outcome pilot contracts, one dealer charter, the measurement stack, and a board readout that clarified what should scale and what still required tighter institutional alignment.

- beneficiary context and covered asset scope

- guaranteed zero downtime
- unconditional savings claims
- OEE uplift promises without stated customer conditions

6. Use cases / case studies

6.1 Uptime-based contract with a Tier-1 automotive plant

Scenario. This use case continues the same Slovakian Tier-1 account from article 18. The plant had 186 critical Forge assets, but the first SDL scope stayed disciplined at 42 assets with the highest repeat-failure and downtime exposure. Faster quoting from article 01 had already reduced one friction point; the plant still needed fewer emergency surprises.

What was designed. The contract combined risk scoring, remote diagnostic triage, reserved intervention kits for four recurring failure modes, and a monthly value review anchored in uptime, OEE, emergency freight, and avoided repeat jobs. The plant committed to production-window data, operator event logs, and attendance in weekly risk reviews.

AI working session summary. Claude Code drafted the outcome schedule and the value-in-use model. OpenCode built the asset risk queue and monthly review export. Codex attacked the financial logic and forced the team to remove one double-counted downtime assumption. Representative prompt:

Draft an uptime partnership offer for Marta Kolarova.
Lead with plant risk reduction and planned intervention discipline.
State what Forge controls, what the plant controls, and what the first 12 weeks will prove.
Do not use guarantee language.

Outcome (illustrative). The SKAUTO42 outcome-partner pilot went live at €410k annualized value, consistent with article 18. Covered-asset uptime improved from 92.1% to 97.6%, OEE from 61.0% to 74.0%, after-state availability reached 98.4%, emergency freight incidents dropped from 33 to 11 per quarter, and quote turnaround on linked reactive work held at 2 days rather than regressing.

6.2 Energy-per-part pilot for an aluminum extrusion plant

Scenario. A Spanish extrusion plant buying Forge thermal-processing equipment was less sensitive to minute-by-minute stoppages than the automotive account, but energy cost and availability were structurally linked. The plant already had a preventive SLA, yet leadership still saw service as maintenance spend rather than performance leverage.

What was designed. The service model shifted from fixed preventive visits to an energy-per-part pilot: thermal drift alerts, planned burner and seal interventions, and a joint monthly review that combined energy, availability, and production context. The contract priced a base service fee plus a bounded performance component tied to agreed baseline bands rather than absolute savings claims.

AI working session summary. OpenCode merged service logs with plant energy-meter exports and generated baseline bands. Claude Code wrote the energy review template and contract clauses. Codex reviewed the value equation to ensure the service fee did not implicitly claim savings created by unrelated production-mix changes.

Outcome (illustrative). The ESROLL14 outcome-partner pilot improved energy per part from 7.1 to 6.1 kWh, availability from 95.9% to 99.2%, and OEE from 67.1% to 79.8% on the covered line family, with after-state uptime at 98.7%. The pilot created €340k in annualized contract value and, more importantly, gave Forge a second outcome model that was not just uptime-by-another-name.

6.3 Dealer ecosystem alignment for mid-market coverage

Scenario. In two Central European markets, Forge relied on dealers for first-line service on mid-market installed base. Those dealers were commercially rewarded for parts turnover and billable emergency visits. Without institutional change, the Predictive Care and outcome-partnership model would stall outside direct accounts.

What was designed. A dealer co-creation charter defined threshold ownership, escalation rights, data-sharing minimums, margin split on planned interventions, and a shared review scorecard. GitHub held the playbooks, and both Forge and the dealer had to approve PRs that changed local service rules.

AI working session summary. Claude Code produced the first dealer charter draft from the direct-account contract model. Codex reviewed whether the margin split unintentionally rewarded reactive visits. OpenCode created a lightweight dealer scorecard view from the same telemetry pipeline so the ecosystem used one data spine.

Outcome (illustrative). One charter was approved in the pilot period, dealer-routed contract cycle time fell from 12 weeks to 6 weeks, first-time-fix on covered accounts improved from 71% to 82%, and Forge gained a scalable pattern for accounts that will never be served only by direct field teams.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

The engagement encoded three core SDL methods as AI-executable SOPs using one pattern: method → step list → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Value-in-use measurement SOP. Inputs: plant telemetry, service history, customer interviews, and baseline definitions. Output schema: one JSON object per plant containing metric definitions, source tables, uncertainty notes, and customer-controlled dependencies. Acceptance: every metric must name its owner and freshness timestamp.
  2. Outcome-contract drafting SOP. Inputs: approved blueprint, value equation, legal exclusions, and decision-rights charter. Output schema: clauses grouped into beneficiary outcome, Forge commitments, customer commitments, governance, exclusions, and re-baselining rules. Acceptance: no prohibited guarantee language; all outcome statements must name the customer contribution required.
  3. Co-creation governance SOP. Inputs: risk queue, playbook, open actions, and current metrics. Output schema: weekly review pack plus a PDCA issue list. Acceptance: every changed threshold or playbook step becomes a named issue with owner, evidence, and next review date.

Meta-SOP

The engagement's meta-SOP was:

  1. Research the customer process in use.
  2. Synthesize value-creation and value-destruction patterns.
  3. Map resource integration and institutions.
  4. Design value propositions, facilitation layers, and co-creation encounters.
  5. Prototype contract and dashboard artifacts.
  6. Measure value-in-use movement.
  7. Deliver institutionalized routines, not only slides.

Prompt templates

Value-in-use measurement prompt

You are VALUE-IN-USE-MEASURER.
Build one plant scorecard.
For each KPI provide:
- definition
- formula
- source rows or systems
- who controls it
- uncertainty note
Reject any KPI that cannot be tied to the beneficiary context.

Outcome-contract prompt

You are OUTCOME-CONTRACT-EDITOR.
Rewrite the clause set so that:
- Forge commitments are controllable
- customer commitments are explicit
- value claims are honest and bounded
- exclusions are visible early
If a sentence implies unilateral value delivery mark it INVALID.

Co-creation governance prompt

You are JOINT-REVIEW-FACILITATOR.
Create the weekly review pack from the current risk queue.
Output:
- top five risks
- decisions required this week
- action owners by side
- experiments to run before next review
No summaries without evidence ids.

Guardrails

  • Do not over-state control. If the plant owns production windows or operator discipline, the contract must say so.
  • Use honest value claims. Savings ranges are acceptable; unconditional savings promises are not.
  • Keep value beneficiary-defined. The north-star metrics are uptime, OEE, energy per part, risk volatility, and confidence in planning — not alerts sent or jobs closed.
  • Treat data-sharing as institutional work. No dashboard is considered live until the legal and cyber rules are agreed.
  • Human sign-off stays mandatory. Legal language, bonus-malus logic, and exclusion clauses do not auto-merge.

Causal logic. Operational improvements move first: earlier diagnostics, reserved kits, and clearer production-window decisions reduce emergency freight and repeat-job volatility. The three pilots modeled €9.2M in annual value at risk across covered assets and booked €1.18M in recurring annual contract value, so the commercial capture stayed intentionally below the modeled customer value pool. That is good discipline. The design goal was not to maximize first-year extraction; it was to prove a repeatable co-creation model that could win more of the customer's service wallet without making dishonest promises.

9. AI tool playbook (per engagement)

Claude Code

Role: orchestrator, architecture writer, and contract drafter. Claude Code held the SDL vocabulary stable across artifacts, wrote the FP map, produced the first value-in-use models, drafted the outcome-contract structure, and generated the weekly review pack. It excelled at long-form synthesis and keeping every artifact aligned to the same language rules. It failed when asked to compress complex plant context too aggressively; the human designer had to restore nuance before legal review.

Concrete usage:

Draft the monthly value review for SKAUTO42.
Use the approved metric definitions only.
Show movement, uncertainty, customer contribution, and the one decision needed this month.

OpenCode

Role: telemetry pipeline, dashboard build, and metric reconciliation. OpenCode merged service logs, OEE extracts, and energy data; built the co-creation dashboard; and generated the sample dataset used in workshops and reviews. It excelled at repeatable mechanical work and simple app generation. It failed once by rendering a dashboard tile as green when a customer feed was stale; a rule was added so missing freshness can never present as success.

Concrete usage:

opencode run --agent telemetry-pipeline "Refresh three pilot scorecards and export monthly_review.json.
Fail if any KPI source is older than seven days or if plant and Forge totals do not reconcile."

Codex

Role: adversarial reviewer. Codex did not design the service. It attacked the value equation, challenged servitization analogies, and reviewed contract language for hidden overclaims. It excelled at finding double counts and weak causal logic. It was much less useful when asked to propose strategy from a blank page.

Concrete usage:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Review the draft board readout.
List only unsupported value claims, double counts, and phrases that imply unilateral value delivery."

GitHub

Role: shared operating system for co-created playbooks. The repo held account-specific playbooks, decision logs, monthly review packs, and contract schedules. Plant stakeholders reviewed PRs directly. GitHub Actions checked mermaid validity, metric-schema freshness, and prohibited contract phrases before merge. This made the PDCA loop visible and auditable. GitHub excelled as a neutral boundary object across firms. It failed whenever reviewers treated PRs as document approval only; the designer had to coach teams to use comments for real operational disagreement.

Concrete usage:

  • One issue per pilot risk or institutional blocker.
  • One PR per playbook or threshold change.
  • Review required from both Forge and customer side for any rule change.
  • GitHub Actions blocked merge if a metric source was stale or a banned guarantee phrase appeared.
  • Monthly milestone used as the board-ready evidence pack.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • SDL improves strategy only when its language is enforced operationally. Without prompt and contract guardrails, teams slide back into goods-dominant wording within hours.
  • Value facilitation and co-creation must be separated. Dashboards and parts kits are important, but they are not by themselves co-creation.
  • The customer's contribution is part of the service design. If production-window data or operator context is required, make it explicit early.
  • Outcome contracts fail first at the institution layer. Incentives, legal language, dealer margin rules, and cyber review create more friction than the analytics stack.
  • AI makes it easy to overclaim economics. Adversarial review is mandatory whenever uptime, savings, or energy claims appear.
  • One shared repo beats three slide decks. Joint PR review changed behavior faster than workshop theatre.
  • Do differently next time: start dealer incentive redesign two weeks earlier, and instrument confidence or planning-stress explicitly rather than inferring it from meeting notes.
