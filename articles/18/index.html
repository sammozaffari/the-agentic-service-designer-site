Concept Briefs & Value Propositions: Funding Predictive Care at Forge Industrial

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Forge Industrial is a fictional composite of European industrial equipment makers with large aftermarket businesses; all company names, site names, figures, and datasets in this article are illustrative and anonymized. This article continues the Forge thread introduced in article 01: there, structured technician notes cut quote cycle time in one pilot product line; here, the question is how to turn that operational learning into fundable service concepts for the executive board. The engagement was run AI-first with Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub as the primary production tools. The human designer owned framing, evidence standards, client politics, and the final investment recommendation.

1. Executive summary

Forge Industrial sells and services heavy process equipment across Europe. Its aftermarket business handles 1.9 million service orders a year through 2,400 field technicians, thousands of OEM and logistics relationships, and a parts-and-service P&L that matters as much as new equipment sales. After the quoting workflow from article 01 was stabilized, a new bottleneck appeared: good insights were not becoming good investment decisions. Teams had telemetry, parts-demand patterns, and technician evidence, but concepts still reached the board as feature lists, not service propositions. Over a 10-week concept phase, we used Kimbell's concept-brief discipline, service-dominant value-in-use framing, and business-model testing to convert reactive field-service pain into a staged portfolio. The lead concept, Predictive Care, reframed Forge from emergency responder to uptime partner for automotive plants. Two concepts received funding, one was incubated, and the board approved a six-month pilot with a modeled payback under 12 months.

2. The brief

Client portrait

Forge Industrial is a fictional pan-European manufacturer of forming, thermal-processing, and materials-handling equipment sold into automotive, packaging, metals, and advanced manufacturing plants. Annual revenue is approximately €3.4 billion, of which €1.05 billion comes from aftermarket service, parts, retrofits, and service contracts. The installed base spans about 62,000 connected or connectable assets across Europe. The field-service organization includes 2,400 technicians, 310 remote support specialists, and 11 regional parts distribution nodes. On average, each technician touches about 790 orders per year, or roughly 15 service jobs a week, which is plausible but leaves little slack for preventable repeat visits.

This article sits after the narrower quoting redesign described in article 01. That earlier work proved that structured technician notes could reduce quote cycle time on pilot product lines from 9 days to 2. The new mandate was larger: if Forge now had better evidence from the field, how should it shape that evidence into fundable service concepts rather than another backlog of disconnected features?

The challenge

Forge's executive board had a pattern problem. In the prior 18 months, the aftermarket organization had assembled 41 concept proposals for new service revenue. Only 7 reached funded pilot, and just 3 were still active six months later. Post-mortem review showed why:

  • 26 of 41 proposals were feature-heavy and goods-dominant: dashboards, portals, or sensors without a clear service outcome.
  • 29 of 41 had no finance-reviewed P&L, only revenue aspiration.
  • 21 of 41 relied on customer demand assumptions with no cited evidence beyond one sales anecdote.
  • Median time from insight deck to board decision was 11 weeks, mostly because operations, finance, and digital kept reopening the same unanswered questions.

At the same time, the operating signal was obvious. Service-order mining across 24 months showed that 40% of all orders were emergency break/fix, 14% of those emergency jobs repeated on the same asset family within 90 days, and auto-plant customers produced the highest mix of downtime-sensitive interventions. Parts planners saw recurring emergency freight on predictable failure kits. Technicians could name which assets were likely to fail. The OEM engineering teams held firmware and telemetry data. Customers wanted fewer stoppages, less quote churn, and clearer accountability. Forge had the raw ingredients for a service proposition, but not a concept discipline.

Constraints

Budget for the concept phase was €420,000, including customer research, telemetry analysis, workshop time, and business-model work. No ERP replacement was allowed; any concept had to assume current service-management and parts systems with thin integration layers. OEM engineering would share telemetry only with named use cases and clear data-retention rules. Several large customers had strict plant-cybersecurity policies, so cloud-heavy concepts would face procurement friction. Works-council concerns meant any technician-facing concept had to show that automation would reduce rework, not create surveillance.

Why service design

The organization's default response was still the goods-logic trap described in See: 04-service-design-for-business/04-solving-problems-and-creating-value.md: add a dashboard, ship an app, or launch a portal. That logic produces features, not services. Kimbell's toolkit instead treats the concept brief as a boundary object that forces a proposition, a value-in-use promise, service elements, a delivery model, evidence of demand, business rationale, and risks onto one page (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/07-the-toolkit-templates.md). Service-dominant thinking further changes the brief from "what equipment data can Forge sell?" to "what value can plant teams co-create when Forge contributes monitoring, remote expertise, parts, and contracting capability?" (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/02-service-dominant-thinking.md; See: 12-service-dominant-logic/03-value-co-creation.md). Finally, concept development in service design explicitly moves from insights to structured service propositions rather than isolated ideas (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/05-developing-ideas.md).

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

We treated the work as a knowledge-funnel exercise: broad evidence about breakdowns, quoting, parts, and uptime had to be narrowed abductively into a small set of testable concepts. The external shape was Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver. Inside that shape, the real sequence was evidence mining → value-in-use reframing → concept briefing → portfolio decision. Claude Code orchestrated the artifact chain; OpenCode ran the data and mock pipelines; Codex attacked assumptions and payback math; GitHub held the working repo, issues, and gate reviews.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

Phase 1 — Discover (weeks 1–2)

Goal. Build an evidence base that explains where value is lost in the current break/fix service and where value could be co-created instead.

Method & SOP. The discovery spine combined value-in-use contextual inquiry and resource integration mapping (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/02-service-dominant-thinking.md; See: 12-service-dominant-logic/03-value-co-creation.md). We did not start by asking which concept to build. We started by mapping how maintenance teams actually restore uptime: what they do before a breakdown, what information they need, which resources they combine, and where Forge forces them to bridge gaps. Evidence came from three sources: 24 months of service-order data across the 1.9M-order aftermarket estate, 28 interviews (8 heads of maintenance, 6 planners, 5 plant reliability engineers, 4 Forge contracting managers, 3 parts planners, 2 OEM product leads), and 2 plant visits in automotive and metals.

AI execution. OpenCode handled the large mechanical cut of the service-order corpus while Claude Code managed the evidence register and clustering. The mining command was direct:

opencode run --agent data-pipeline "Read service/orders_24m.csv, parts/emergency_freight.csv,
telemetry/alerts_sample.csv and quotes/pilot_line_quotes.csv. Output:
1) service-order mix by category
2) repeat failure rate by asset family within 90 days
3) quote-cycle baseline for reactive visits
4) emergency freight incidents linked to failure kits
5) a 50-row synthetic sample consistent with the headline ratios.
Reject any table whose totals do not reconcile to 1.9M annual orders."

Claude Code then generated evidence-backed insight cards, one agent per evidence stream: customer interviews, technician interviews, and quantitative patterns. GitHub issues tracked each question (concept-demand, asset-repeat-failure, telemetry-coverage, parts-freight) so the sponsor could see unresolved assumptions instead of waiting for a final deck.

Human designer judgment. The hardest call was to treat quote time as a symptom, not the concept itself. Article 01's work had already improved quote turnaround. The designer reframed the real question from "how do we quote faster?" to "how do we reduce the conditions that make urgent quotes and emergency dispatch the default?" That abductive move was the hinge between operational improvement and service innovation.

Artifact produced. A multi-source evidence register with six stable patterns, including the key one: in the automotive cohort, 62% of urgent quotes followed failure modes that had at least one detectable early warning signal in technician notes, parts history, or telematics, but those signals were split across teams.

Phase 2 — Define (weeks 3–4)

Goal. Convert evidence into tightly framed service opportunities and value propositions, not feature wish lists.

Method & SOP. This phase used three linked methods. First, HMW reframing translated insight statements into concept prompts (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/05-developing-ideas.md). Second, service problem reframing forced every opportunity to connect customer outcome and business metric (See: 04-service-design-for-business/04-solving-problems-and-creating-value.md). Third, the value proposition canvas was adapted to service-dominant logic: customer jobs, pains, and gains were paired with operant resources, resource demands on the customer, and co-created value-in-use (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/02-service-dominant-thinking.md).

AI execution. Claude Code created the first HMW bank and VPC draft from the evidence register. The prompt contract was explicit that the output could not become a feature list:

You are CONCEPT-FRAMER.
Input: evidence register with quotes, service-order metrics, customer roles.
Produce for each candidate opportunity:
1. job to be done in customer language
2. pains with evidence quote ids
3. gains with success metric
4. HMW question
5. draft value proposition in value-in-use form
Rules:
- do not output solutions before the job and pain are explicit
- if evidence comes from fewer than two independent sources mark assumption
- phrase the proposition as a service outcome not a software feature
- include the business metric the proposition is expected to move

Codex played the adversarial board member. It reviewed whether demand evidence was real or merely plausible:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Review concepts/framing_pack.md.
For each demand signal, label it as transcript-backed, data-backed, sales-anecdote-only,
or inferred. Reject any concept whose stated market pull depends mainly on anecdote.
Then review payback logic assumptions for volume realism against 1.9M annual service orders."

Human designer judgment. The designer collapsed dozens of candidate statements into one framing principle the board could use: Forge should sell avoided downtime and managed risk where it has a credible service role, not telemetry access for its own sake. That sentence killed three concepts early, including a customer portal idea that had plenty of features and almost no differentiated service value.

Artifact produced. A shared opportunity pack with five viable concept territories: Predictive Care, Telemetry Replenishment, Remote Diagnostics Desk, Quote Assist Plus, and Outcome Contract Lab.

Phase 3 — Develop (weeks 5–7)

Goal. Turn the opportunity pack into concept briefs, business models, and a scored portfolio with one lead concept ready for funding.

Method & SOP. We followed Kimbell's field-by-field concept-brief protocol (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/07-the-toolkit-templates.md) and the idea-to-proposition progression from Polaine, Løvlie, and Reason (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/05-developing-ideas.md). Each concept had to answer the same questions: proposition, value proposition, service elements, delivery model, evidence of demand, business rationale, key assumptions, and risks. Then each concept received a business model canvas pass and a desirability-feasibility-viability score.

AI execution. Claude Code produced the first complete draft of all five concept briefs, one subagent per concept plus one reviewer enforcing field completeness. OpenCode generated the economics scaffolds and synthetic pilot demand scenarios so finance could pressure-test the unit model instead of debating a blank spreadsheet.

opencode run --agent modeler "For each concept in concepts/briefs/*.md,
build a simple economics sheet with revenue stream, implementation cost,
service-delivery cost, assumed attach rate, and payback months.
Use the article constraints: no ERP replacement, EU field-service labor,
realistic parts logistics, and customer cohorts of 20 to 250 covered assets.
Fail if any scenario implies more covered assets than the target segment owns."

The lead concept emerged as Predictive Care: a contract in which Forge combines installed-base monitoring, remote diagnostics, condition-based parts replenishment, and uptime reviews for critical assets in automotive plants. Quote Assist Plus remained important, but as an enabling capability rather than the proposition itself.

Human designer judgment. Finance wanted to pick the easiest concept; service leadership wanted the most strategic concept; digital wanted the most data-rich concept. The designer mediated that conflict by separating portfolio roles: one concept to fund now because it can move the business, one adjacent concept to pilot because it de-risks enabling capability, one concept to incubate, and two to park. That is concept-portfolio design, not a beauty contest.

Artifact produced. Five concept briefs, five business-model canvases, a scored portfolio, and a finance-reviewed P&L for Predictive Care showing €1.50M annual contract revenue, €1.035M annual delivery cost, €0.31M onboarding capex, €0.465M annual gross profit, and modeled payback in 8 months at the pilot-cohort scale.

Phase 4 — Deliver (weeks 8–10)

Goal. Convert the concept portfolio into an executive decision, a funded pilot, and a future-state service blueprint credible enough for operations.

Method & SOP. Delivery in this article meant decision design. The artifacts were a board memo shell, a six-minute concept pitch, a future-state blueprint for Predictive Care, and a staged portfolio decision. The board was not asked to bless innovation in the abstract. It was asked to approve specific funding levels, customer cohorts, assumptions to test, and kill criteria.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the board memo and the Predictive Care blueprint. GitHub pull requests became the review gate for the board pack: finance checked the P&L, service operations checked the delivery model, and legal checked the data-sharing assumptions. Codex reviewed the payback math and the wording of the recommendation so the board packet did not overclaim certainty.

You are BOARD-TEST-PITCHER.
Input: approved concept brief, VPC, BMC, scored risks, economics sheet.
Output:
- one 90-second oral summary
- one 6-slide board narrative
- one table of assumptions to test in pilot
- one explicit ask with funding amount and stop conditions
Rules:
- lead with customer value in use then business value
- include downside case and what would make us kill the concept
- never say customers want this unless evidence is cited
- keep the funding ask inside the stated capex envelope

Human designer judgment. The board did not need more enthusiasm. It needed a recommendation with boundaries. The designer argued for a staged decision: fund Predictive Care now, pilot Telemetry Replenishment alongside it, incubate Remote Diagnostics Desk, and park Outcome Contract Lab and Self-Serve Parts Portal until stronger ecosystem evidence existed. That staged logic protected the strategic move without pretending every concept was equally mature.

Artifact produced. Board-approved portfolio decision, Predictive Care pilot brief, future-state blueprint, and measurement plan with explicit stop conditions after 12 weeks and 6 months.

6. Use cases / case studies

Use case A — From break fix to Predictive Care contract at a Tier-1 auto plant

Scenario. A Tier-1 automotive supplier in Slovakia had 186 critical Forge assets and a history of weld-cell stoppages. The customer was one of the same accounts touched by the faster-quoting work from article 01. Faster quotes helped, but the plant still felt trapped in expensive heroics. Predictive Care reframed the offer from better reaction to fewer emergency reactions.

What was designed. The concept combined three layers: risk scoring on 42 highest-risk assets, remote diagnostic triage before dispatch, and reserved intervention kits for four recurring failure modes. The contract included a monthly risk review and a quarterly business review anchored in uptime and avoided emergency freight.

AI working session summary. Claude Code assembled the concept brief, customer-facing narrative, and blueprint. OpenCode built the sensitivity model for 42, 84, and 186 covered assets. Codex challenged the claim that all four failure modes were truly preventable; one was downgraded to "respond faster" rather than "prevent." Representative prompt:

Build a customer-facing concept summary for Marta Kolarova.
Lead with avoided downtime, not telemetry. Use only evidence already accepted in the concept brief.
Show what the plant contributes, what Forge contributes, and what the first 12 weeks would prove.

Outcome (illustrative). The board funded a six-month pilot. Modeled economics at the initial 42-asset scope: €410k annualized contract value, 31% service gross margin after month 6, 8-month payback, and targeted uptime movement from 92.1% to 97.6% on the covered asset set.

Use case B — Parts replenishment via machine telemetry

Scenario. Forge's parts planners knew that certain seal and sensor kits were repeatedly expedited after the same alert signatures, but replenishment was still triggered manually after failure or during technician diagnosis.

What was designed. Telemetry Replenishment linked alert classes to kit reservation rules. It did not auto-ship. Instead, it reserved stock at the regional node once a validated alert appeared and released the kit when the remote diagnostician or planner confirmed the likely intervention.

AI working session summary. OpenCode reconstructed 14 months of emergency freight records and modeled stock-reservation impact on working capital. Claude Code turned that model into a two-page concept brief with three delivery options. Codex rejected the first economics draft because it counted stock reservations as revenue rather than cost avoidance.

Outcome (illustrative). The concept moved to pilot-now status beside Predictive Care. Modeled effect on the pilot cohort: emergency freight incidents per quarter 96→28, reserved-kit obsolescence held under 3%, and gross-margin uplift of 4.2 points on covered interventions.

Use case C — Remote diagnostics SOP for customer plants

Scenario. Customers often wanted a technician on site immediately, while Forge's remote team knew that some alerts could be resolved or narrowed remotely in 20 to 30 minutes if plant teams had a clean escalation path.

What was designed. A remote diagnostics SOP defined the trigger, triage window, decision tree, data required from the plant, and escalation handoff to field service. It became a service element inside Predictive Care rather than a standalone software offer.

flowchart LR A[Alert or breakdown signal] --> B[Remote diagnostician reviews asset history in 15 minutes] B --> C{Remote action possible} C -->|Yes| D[Guide plant through reset or parameter change] C -->|No| E[Plan site visit with reserved kit and checklist] D --> F[Log learning and next risk window] E --> F

AI working session summary. Claude Code wrote the SOP and customer script. OpenCode generated 12 synthetic incident walkthroughs for rehearsal. GitHub PR review forced the team to add an explicit fallback when customer cyber policy blocked live data access.

Outcome (illustrative). In pilot simulations, 22% of incoming alert cases avoided a full emergency dispatch, median diagnostic time fell from 4.5 hours to 55 minutes, and technicians reported higher confidence because the site visit started with a tighter failure hypothesis.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

The most important lesson in this engagement was that concept development becomes reliable only when the method is encoded as a sequence, schema, and gate rather than "brainstorm until the deck feels persuasive."

Encoded SOP 1 — Concept brief build SOP

sop: FORGE-CONCEPT-001
name: Build service concept brief from evidence
owner: lead_service_designer
steps:
  - id: S1
    name: collect_evidence
    output: evidence pack with quotes, metrics, and unresolved assumptions
  - id: S2
    name: define_beneficiary_and_context
    output: primary persona, operating context, and job to be done
  - id: S3
    name: write_proposition
    rule: phrase as service outcome not feature
  - id: S4
    name: write_value_proposition
    fields: customer_value_in_use, enterprise_value_in_use, beneficiary_contributions
  - id: S5
    name: specify_service_elements_and_delivery_model
    fields: touchpoints, actor_roles, channels, backstage_capabilities
  - id: S6
    name: attach_evidence_of_demand
    rule: every demand claim cites transcript ids or data cuts
  - id: S7
    name: attach_business_rationale
    fields: revenue_logic, cost_logic, capex, payback_months, sensitivities
  - id: S8
    name: list_risks_and_assumptions
    rule: tag unknowns as desirability, feasibility, viability, ecosystem_fit
acceptance_criteria:
  - concept contains proposition, value proposition, service elements, delivery model, evidence of demand, business rationale, and risks
  - no numeric claim appears without a source inside the evidence pack
  - at least one customer metric and one business metric are named
review_gate:
  - codex_payback_check
  - sponsor_readout

Field-by-field evidence rule. Proposition must cite the customer job. Value proposition must cite pains and gains. Service elements must cite at least one failure point the element addresses. Delivery model must name owner, not just capability. Evidence of demand must distinguish customer quote, order data, and sales anecdote. Business rationale must include sensitivity, not just base case. Risks must include what would make the concept fail even if the technology works.

Encoded SOP 2 — Value proposition canvas build prompt

You are VPC-BUILDER.
Input: persona table, evidence register, current-state journey, service-order cuts.
Task: build a value proposition canvas for a B2B service concept.
Output fields:
- jobs
- pains
- gains
- products and services
- pain relievers
- gain creators
- beneficiary contributions
- ecosystem enablers
Rules:
- phrase jobs in the user's working language
- prefer operational pains over generic frustration labels
- include one metric the user already cares about
- if a gain depends on a partner or public resource name it explicitly
- reject outputs that read like a feature backlog

Encoded SOP 3 — Board-test pitch generation

You are BOARD-TEST-PITCHER.
Input: approved concept brief, BMC, scorecard, economics review.
Produce:
1. one-slide decision summary
2. three reasons to fund now
3. three reasons to say no
4. pilot scope with stop conditions
5. exact funding ask
Guardrails:
- do not invent demand signals
- do not round payback in a way that hides sensitivity
- if market pull relies on one customer, say so plainly
- if the concept is really an enabling capability, not a service, classify it correctly

Guardrails

  • No invented demand signals. Any claim like "customers will pay for this" had to point to an interview quote, existing spend pattern, or signed letter of intent. Sales enthusiasm alone was tagged anecdotal.
  • Payback math reviewed by Codex. Codex's role was narrow and valuable: attack volumes, margin assumptions, attach-rate optimism, and double counting.
  • Features are not services. If the output described a portal, dashboard, or algorithm without a customer outcome, delivery model, and actor-role logic, the concept failed.
  • Human sign-off at three points. The designer approved the HMW bank, the sponsor approved the concept shortlist, and finance approved the P&L logic.
  • Privacy and labor review. Concepts touching telemetry or technician workflow had to survive customer cyber review and works-council scrutiny.

Failure modes observed and mitigated

  1. Feature masquerading as proposition. Several first-pass drafts were really interface concepts. The mitigation was a mandatory "value in use" sentence.
  2. Demand inflation. AI will happily smooth thin evidence into a convincing market statement. The mitigation was source tagging and Codex review.
  3. Overconfident payback. Early economics ignored telemetry onboarding friction. The mitigation was a downside case and month-by-month capex burn.
  4. Concepts too large to pilot. Outcome Contract Lab was strategically exciting but too under-specified. The mitigation was to park it explicitly rather than dilute the pilot.

Meta-SOP

Research → evidence register → jobs and pains → value proposition → concept brief → BMC and P&L → board test → blueprint and pilot brief. The gates matter more than the prompts. Prompt quality helps; review discipline prevents bad concepts from becoming expensive programmes.

9. AI tool playbook (per engagement)

Claude Code — orchestration, concept authoring, synthesis. It owned the evidence register, HMW bank, concept briefs, VPC drafts, board memo drafts, and the future-state blueprint. It excelled at keeping multiple artifacts aligned so the same assumptions appeared consistently in briefs, tables, and charts. It failed when prompts were too loose: first-pass concepts drifted back into dashboard language. The human took over for reframing, portfolio role assignment, and sponsor politics.

OpenCode — data pipelines, mock economics, and scenario simulation. It mined the service-order corpus, produced the embedded concept-evaluation sample, generated economic sensitivity sheets, and built synthetic pilot scenarios. It excelled at repeatable mechanical work over multiple data files. It failed once by allowing an attach-rate scenario that exceeded the number of coverable assets in the customer cohort; that is why the command contract explicitly capped cohort realism.

Codex — adversarial reviewer. Codex reviewed demand evidence, attacked the payback math, and challenged board language. It excelled at finding double counts, unjustified extrapolations, and weak causal claims. It failed when asked broad strategy questions; its useful role was narrow and hostile. The human took over to decide whether a defect was fatal or acceptable for pilot learning.

GitHub — working surface and review gate. Every concept, score, and assumption lived in issues and pull requests. Concept brief revisions happened as diffs, not as deck archaeology. Reviewers from service, finance, and digital commented on the same file. A lightweight Actions-style check was used for Mermaid validity and number consistency. GitHub excelled because it turned concept development into versioned work rather than workshop residue.

Representative check pattern:

on: [pull_request]
jobs:
  concept_checks:
    steps:
      - run: node scripts/validate-mermaid.mjs articles/18
      - run: node scripts/check_numbers.mjs articles/18/README.md
      - run: node scripts/check_concept_fields.mjs concepts/*.md

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • A concept brief is only useful when it is strict. The field-by-field discipline mattered more than workshop energy.
  • Customers buy risk reduction in their language. "Avoided downtime" worked; "predictive analytics layer" did not.
  • AI makes it easy to overstate demand. Evidence tagging and adversarial review are not optional.
  • The board does not fund possibility; it funds bounded learning. Predictive Care was funded because the pilot scope and kill criteria were explicit.
  • Enabling capabilities should not masquerade as propositions. Quote Assist Plus was valuable, but it belonged inside the operating model, not as a standalone headline service.
  • Partner logic must be explicit. OEM engineering and logistics were not dependencies in the footnotes; they were actors in the proposition.
  • Do differently next time: involve customer cybersecurity earlier, and run one technician time-and-motion study to quantify the labor release from planned versus emergency work more precisely.
