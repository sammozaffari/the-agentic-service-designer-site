Service Quality Management at Kestrel Energy

00 · Provenance note

Confidentiality & provenance note

Kestrel Energy is a fictional composite client built from recurring patterns across EU energy retailers managing smart-meter rollout, billing modernization, and SME account operations. All names, figures, datasets, touchpoint IDs, and operating-unit metrics in this article are illustrative and anonymized. This phase-2 case study continues the Kestrel storyline from article 08: the personas and rollout improvements are treated as prior work, and the focus here is how that work was institutionalized into a living service quality management system. The engagement was AI-first by design: Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were the daily operating tools, while the human designer owned framing, governance, ethics, and the political work of getting quality reviewed with operations every month.

01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

Kestrel Energy had already done the hard phase-1 work of understanding its customers. In article 08, the utility rebuilt its personas, improved smart-meter self-booking, reduced failed appointments, and exposed how Ingrid, Markus, and Sabine experienced the service differently. What Kestrel still lacked was a management system for service quality itself. Billing and connection journeys were still being run with a thin layer of CSAT, average speed of answer, and complaint counts. That was not enough. Despite better segmentation and several redesigned touchpoints, 14% of all annual contacts were still avoidable failure demand concentrated in billing and connection services. The phase-2 brief was therefore operational: create a standing Service Quality Management system with touchpoint standards, monthly Moments of Satisfaction scoring, failure-demand attribution, cross-functional review, and a Service Profit Chain dashboard linking internal quality to employee confidence, customer value, loyalty, and cost. Over 16 weeks, Kestrel built standards-as-code in GitHub, instrumented a MOS model based on Gronroos technical and functional quality, and established a monthly quality review rhythm co-run by service design and operations. Six months after rollout, avoidable failure demand fell from 14.0% to 8.6% of contacts, billing-query MOS rose from 61 to 78, frontline eNPS moved from +8 to +23, and the annualized value released or protected was about €1.9M.

02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

Kestrel Energy returns here as a fictional EU utility serving 2.4 million household accounts and 180,000 SMEs across two member states. By the time this engagement began, the smart-meter program had moved from 1.1 million installed meters in phase 1 to 1.65 million, or roughly 69% of household accounts. The digital estate was the same broad shape described in article 08: a legacy web portal, a newer mobile app, a contact centre of about 420 seats, billing operations split across two service cells, a central connections desk, and a network of field technicians and meter partners. Digital self-service had improved, but Kestrel still operated like many utilities do: service quality was managed incident by incident rather than as a whole-service capability.

The challenge

The phase-1 persona program did real work. It improved smart-meter self-booking from 22% to 64%, reduced failed appointments from 14% to 6%, cut S1 SME invoice queries by 41%, and lowered price-vigilant switching risk in Markus-like cohorts from 19% to 13.5%. See: article 08. But the board then hit a familiar phase-2 problem: redesign wins existed, while quality still drifted by team, month, and channel.

Kestrel's monthly board pack still summarized service quality with only complaint counts, average handling time, and a top-line CSAT average. That view hid the real problem. Of roughly 760,000 annual service contacts, about 318,000 sat in billing and connection journeys. Around 106,400 of those contacts were avoidable failure demand that customers should never have had to generate in the first place: first-bill confusion after smart-meter installs, estimated-vs-actual bill shock, missed connection appointment prerequisites, broken promise windows, and Sabine-style SME invoice friction. That was 14% of all contacts, or about €617k in avoidable contact cost before counting field revisits, churn, and finance-team rework.

Grönroos is useful precisely here because he separates what the customer got from how the service felt. Kestrel was obsessing over the first and barely managing the second. A bill could be technically correct and still fail on functional quality if no one explained why an estimate caught up after a meter change. A connection could be technically completed and still fail if the promise window, access checklist, and update cadence made the customer feel abandoned. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md.

Constraints

The transformation budget was capped at €280,000 and had to fit inside existing operations. Kestrel could not replace the billing engine or the case-management layer this year. Energy-market regulation constrained billing language, late-payment treatment, and connection communications. The DSO and field-meter partners owned parts of the customer outcome but did not report into Kestrel. The works council required that any quality scoring system be used for process improvement and coaching, not punitive ranking of individual agents. And because phase 1 had already taught the organization to distrust “average customer” thinking, the new quality system had to preserve the persona logic from article 08 while still being simple enough for monthly operating review.

Why service design

A classic operations fix would have added agents and stricter scripts. That would have absorbed more failure rather than removing its causes. Kestrel needed whole-service design in Lou Downe's sense: letters, appointment windows, digital explanations, contact-centre scripts, casework rules, internal SLAs, and review governance all in scope together. See: 05-good-services/06-designing-good-services.md. It also needed a measurement architecture that separated relationship, journey, and operational layers rather than collapsing them into one blunt customer score. See: 14-outside-in/06-measurement.md. And because the real point was institutionalization, not a one-off fix, the internal service chain and service culture mattered as much as any customer-facing copy. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/05-internal-marketing-and-service-culture.md.

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research — baseline the quality truth

Goal. Separate avoidable contact demand from legitimate demand, and expose which touchpoints fail on technical quality, functional quality, or both.

Method & SOP. The baseline combined four streams: contact logs, complaint codes, post-interaction surveys, and operational telemetry from billing, field scheduling, and connection queues. Each touchpoint was audited using Gronroos's split between technical quality and functional quality. For every sampled moment of truth, the team asked: did the customer get the right outcome, and did the service feel accessible, clear, trustworthy, and recoverable? See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md.

AI execution. OpenCode built the baseline pipeline joining contact, complaint, survey, and ops data into one row per service episode. The prompt was intentionally explicit about attribution because “avoidable” is easy for a model to over-assign:

TERMINAL
opencode run --agent build "Read contact_logs_12m.parquet, complaints.csv,
survey_events.csv, billing_ops.csv, scheduling_ops.csv.
Create one episode table keyed by contact_id_hash.
For each row output: touchpoint, channel, avoidable_flag,
mos_technical_score, mos_functional_score, resolution_days,
survey_score, root_cause_owner, operating_unit.
Rules: avoidable means the contact would not be needed if Kestrel had met its
published standard; ambiguous rows must be tagged review_required. Export
monthly aggregates and a root-cause Pareto for billing and connections."

Claude Code then ran case-audit agents over 120 sampled episodes, forcing each agent to name the failed promise, the quality dimension, and the likely owner. GitHub issues were opened automatically for every high-confidence root-cause cluster so the evidence was reviewable before any solutioning started.

Human designer judgment. The critical decision was to refuse the lazy narrative that “customers contact because energy is confusing.” Some demand was legitimate. The designer insisted on distinguishing necessary clarification from Kestrel-generated rework. That political distinction mattered because it determined whether the monthly quality review would become a blame ritual or a true improvement forum.

Artifact produced. Failure-demand baseline, root-cause truth set, operating-unit cut, and the first draft of the touchpoint inventory.

4.2 Ideation — build the quality standards library and MOS model

Goal. Turn research and case evidence into enforceable touchpoint standards with clear measures, owners, and control limits.

Method & SOP. This phase used Gronroos's moment-of-truth mapping directly: every touchpoint received a technical requirement and a functional requirement. The measurement design followed Outside In's three-tier structure: relationship, journey, and operational metrics, so Kestrel would not fall back into the single-metric trap. See: 14-outside-in/06-measurement.md. The service team also adopted the internal standard that every touchpoint must have one named owner, one observable measure, and one review cadence.

AI execution. Claude Code maintained the standards-as-code library. Each standard lived in a versioned YAML file with fields for touchpoint, promise, measure, source evidence, persona sensitivity, and review owner. A representative authoring prompt:

NOTE
You are QUALITY-STANDARD-AUTHOR.
Input: touchpoint audit findings, evidence IDs, current SOPs, and service promises.
Output: one standard file with these fields only:
- standard_id
- touchpoint
- technical_requirement
- functional_requirement
- measure
- control_limit
- owner
- review_cadence
- persona_overlay
- change_reason
Rules:
1. Every requirement must be observable.
2. Measures must be computable from existing telemetry or survey events.
3. If evidence does not support a standard, mark gap_for_human rather than inventing.
4. Include persona overlays for Ingrid, Markus, or Sabine only where the need changes the standard materially.

Codex then adversarially reviewed the library for two things Kestrel historically got wrong: standards written so vaguely they could not be audited, and standards written so optimistically they would force frontline teams to fake compliance.

TERMINAL
codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read standards/ and monthly-pack-spec.md.
Challenge every causal claim and every control limit.
Flag any measure that cannot be calculated from real data,
any promise that operations cannot currently keep,
and any standard that confuses technical quality with functional quality.
Return defects only."

Human designer judgment. The human made two load-bearing calls. First, MOS would not be a generic mood score; it would be a touchpoint-level measure built from separate technical and functional scores, with functional quality weighted slightly higher because that was where Kestrel was actually differentiating. Second, persona overlays would remain overlays. Ingrid, Markus, and Sabine shaped standards, but the standards library itself would stay touchpoint-centric rather than fragmenting into dozens of persona-specific variants.

Artifact produced. Standards library v1, MOS rubric, review cadences, and standard ownership model.

4.3 Prototyping — pilot the quality loop in billing and connections

Goal. Prove that standards, MOS scoring, and monthly review create real operational change in live service units.

Method & SOP. The pilot focused on two high-volume journeys: billing queries and connection booking. Whole-service prototyping was used in Lou Downe's sense: not just screen changes, but letters, scripts, routing rules, handoff permissions, and internal SLAs. See: 05-good-services/06-designing-good-services.md. In parallel, internal marketing was treated as a delivery stream, not a comms afterthought. Frontline staff, scheduling teams, and billing analysts were segmented as different internal audiences with different readiness needs. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/05-internal-marketing-and-service-culture.md.

AI execution. Claude Code generated roleplay packs, standard-change summaries, and monthly quality-review scripts. OpenCode produced the first automated quality pack, combining MOS by touchpoint, avoidable-demand by root cause, open standard changes, and SPC link metrics. GitHub handled the change flow: standards were edited in pull requests, reviewed by service owners, and linked to operational issues.

Human designer judgment. The designer chose to co-run the monthly review with operations rather than as a separate CX forum. That sounds procedural, but it was the governance breakthrough. Once quality review sat inside the operations cadence, standards stopped being advisory and became real operating commitments. The designer also intervened when the first pilot review drifted into individual-agent discussion; the quality system was reset around touchpoints, policies, and internal service failures, consistent with the works-council agreement.

Artifact produced. Pilot review pack, revised standards, internal marketing rollout, and a live issue flow from complaint to root cause to SOP change.

4.4 Implementation — institutionalize the monthly operating system

Goal. Launch a durable Service Quality Management system that survives beyond the designer's involvement.

Method & SOP. The handover model followed the Service Profit Chain logic: review leading indicators first, at the operating-unit level, and make the links between internal quality, employee condition, customer value, loyalty, and finance visible. See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/05-measuring-the-chain.md. Kestrel created five operating-unit views: billing north, billing south, connections central, meter scheduling, and SME onboarding.

AI execution. GitHub Actions built the monthly quality pack automatically. Claude Code refreshed the standards library and drafted the narrative summary. OpenCode rebuilt the dashboard and SPC tables from raw monthly extracts. Codex reviewed the contribution language in the steering paper so the organization did not confuse correlation with causation.

CONFIG
name: monthly-quality-pack
on:
  schedule:
    - cron: "0 5 2 * *"
jobs:
  build:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - name: Build quality aggregates
        run: python pipelines/quality_pack.py
      - name: Validate mermaid and markdown
        run: node scripts/validate-mermaid.mjs articles/22
      - name: Publish monthly pack
        run: python pipelines/publish_pack.py

Human designer judgment. The designer drew the final boundary between what AI could maintain and what humans had to own. Agents could draft standards, analyze MOS, and detect control-limit breaches. They could not approve a standard that changed customer rights, tune the control limits to hit a narrative target, or decide whether a DSO constraint justified an exception. Those remained human decisions with named approvers.

Artifact produced. Monthly quality operating model, SPC dashboard, quality-pack automation, and handover governance.

Service quality framework diagram

Kestrel defined MOS as a 0-100 touchpoint score derived from two separate 1-5 ratings: technical quality and functional quality. The working formula was:

MOS = round(((technical * 0.45) + (functional * 0.55) - 1) * 25)

The slight weighting toward functional quality reflected what the baseline showed: Kestrel's biggest loyalty leaks were no longer raw operational defects alone, but opaque explanations, inaccessible channels, and broken promises.

06 · Use cases

Use cases / case studies

Use case A — Billing-error quality loop after a smart-meter first bill

Scenario. A Markus-like household sees a first bill after smart-meter activation that includes a catch-up adjustment and assumes Kestrel has overcharged him. In phase 1, Kestrel improved the smart-meter journey itself; in phase 2, it tackled the quality-management question underneath that moment.

What was designed. Kestrel created a billing-error quality loop rather than a one-off fix. The first contact now triggers three actions at once: explain the bill in plain language, assign a due-by time, and classify the likely cause. When the same cause appears above the monthly threshold, it moves from case handling into standards review. In this use case, the root cause was not a calculation defect. It was a recurring mismatch between how the billing engine phrased estimated-to-actual reconciliation and what the first-bill explainer promised.

AI working session summary. Claude Code drafted the revised standard and the customer-language explanation. OpenCode traced 14 weeks of first-bill queries and showed that 62% of “billing error” complaints were actually comprehension failures. Codex challenged the team's first conclusion and forced a split between genuine overcharge and explainability defects. Representative prompt:

NOTE
You are MOS-ANALYST.
Compare 200 first-bill contacts tagged billing_error against bill outputs and survey text.
Separate technical defects from explanation defects.
Return only: defect_type, evidence_count, MOS_technical_mean, MOS_functional_mean,
repeat_contact_rate, recommendation.
If the technical defect rate is under 20 percent, do not recommend system change first.

Outcome. In the pilot units, billing-error FCR rose from 48% to 79%, mean resolution time fell from 6.2 to 2.1 days, and repeat contacts on first-bill queries fell from 34% to 14%. More importantly, the root cause fed a standard change and an explainer rewrite, not just an apology script. This is the exact quality-loop behavior Kestrel previously lacked.

Use case B — Meter-reading schedule quality

Scenario. Ingrid-like customers and rural households were still calling after meter-reading schedule changes because reminder messages described the date but not the purpose, access window, or assisted-digital option.

What was designed. The quality standard for meter-reading schedule changes was rewritten around one user need: “I need to know what will happen, when, and what I must do so that I do not miss the visit or worry I have done something wrong.” The revised touchpoint added a purpose line, a narrow window, an access checklist, and a phone route. When a no-access event still occurred, the service-recovery standard required same-day explanation and rebooking.

AI working session summary. OpenCode joined scheduling, IVR, and missed-visit data to isolate avoidable schedule confusion. Claude Code generated three reminder variants and a recovery script. Codex rejected the first draft because it overpromised exact technician arrival times the operation could not meet.

Outcome. Meter-reading schedule confusion contacts fell by 37%, no-access failures on in-scope appointments fell from 6.8% to 4.1%, and the functional side of MOS for this touchpoint rose from 58 to 74. That translated into fewer wasted field visits and a measurable reduction in avoidable operational cost.

Use case C — SME onboarding quality for Sabine

Scenario. Sabine opens a fourth bakery site. The technical connection work is straightforward, but the onboarding experience spans contract setup, invoice structure, portal access, and renewal administration. Historically, quality drift between those backstage units made Kestrel feel fragmented.

What was designed. The new SME onboarding standard defined one named owner, one account pack, and one billing structure agreement before go-live. The quality review treated any first-invoice or access problem as evidence of onboarding quality, not as a separate downstream support incident. The standard also preserved the phase-1 persona directive from article 08: one supplier, one bill, one login.

AI working session summary. Claude Code maintained the onboarding standard and generated the owner handoff checklist. OpenCode joined onboarding cases to first-invoice and first-30-day contact events, revealing that onboarding success had been judged far too early. Codex reviewed the linkage logic and agreed the right measure was not completion of the connection itself, but 30-day quality after the site went live.

Outcome. SME onboarding MOS rose from 54 to 74, first-invoice queries in the S1 cohort fell by 46%, and the renewal-at-risk rate for Sabine-like SMEs moved from 11% to 8%. The economic lesson was simple: onboarding quality is renewal design in disguise.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Kestrel encoded three core SOP families and one meta-SOP. Every one used the same contract: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

7.1 Quality-standard authoring SOP

NOTE
SOP QS-01 QUALITY STANDARD AUTHORING
1. Read touchpoint evidence, complaint themes, and current promise text.
2. Write one technical requirement and one functional requirement.
3. Define one observable measure and one control limit.
4. Name the owner and review cadence.
5. Add persona overlay only if the need changes the standard materially.
6. Link proposed standard to evidence IDs and current root-cause issue.
OUTPUT: standard_id, touchpoint, technical_requirement, functional_requirement,
measure, control_limit, owner, review_cadence, persona_overlay, change_reason.
ACCEPTANCE: observable, computable, no conflicting promise, one owner only.
REVIEW GATE: service owner plus operations lead in GitHub PR.

7.2 MOS analysis prompt

NOTE
You are MOS-ANALYST.
Input: monthly episode table by touchpoint and operating unit.
For each touchpoint calculate:
- mos_mean
- technical_mean
- functional_mean
- control_limit_status
- avoidable_share
- repeat_contact_rate
- top negative verbatim theme
Rules:
1. Use both technical and functional scores; never collapse raw survey text into MOS.
2. If sample size is under threshold, mark low_confidence.
3. Report control-limit breaches before averages.
4. Return one recommendation only when evidence points to a likely root cause.
5. Do not smooth month-to-month volatility unless a human asked for a trend view separately.

7.3 Failure-demand root-cause SOP as code

NOTE
SOP FD-03 FAILURE DEMAND ROOT CAUSE
1. Select all avoidable contacts for the month by touchpoint.
2. Group by root_cause_owner and reason family.
3. Compare current month against prior three-month baseline.
4. Flag any cluster above control limit or growth threshold.
5. Check whether a current standard exists and whether it was kept.
6. Route outcome:
   - standard not present -> create standard proposal issue
   - standard present but broken -> create SOP or training issue
   - standard kept but still failing -> escalate structural defect to ops change
OUTPUT: root cause table, issue list, standard impact, decision recommendation.
ACCEPTANCE: every flagged cluster has evidence count and owner.
REVIEW GATE: monthly quality review chaired by CX ops and service owner.

7.4 Meta-SOP and guardrails

The meta-SOP was stable across the engagement: baseline → standardize → pilot → review → change → measure again. No standard changed without evidence. No MOS story went to the steering group without operational telemetry beside it. No customer-facing promise was changed without the operational owner confirming it could be kept.

Guardrails:

  • No cherry-picking metrics. Monthly review always begins with control-limit breaches and adverse movement before success cases. This follows the lead-lag governance logic in 20-the-service-profit-chain/05-measuring-the-chain.md.
  • Respect control limits. Agents may not suppress volatility by smoothing, rounding, or re-banding unless the review pack explicitly requests a trend view in addition to the raw control view.
  • No quality score without both dimensions. MOS requires technical and functional quality together. A technically correct but emotionally poor episode cannot be reported as “good quality.” See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md.
  • Internal and external measures stay linked. A green customer score with falling eNPS or broken internal SLAs is treated as an at-risk signal, not a success.
  • Human sign-off points. Standard changes, control-limit resets, persona-overlay rules, and anything touching debt, billing rights, or regulated copy require human approval.
  • Evidence discipline. Any generated narrative in the monthly pack must trace to episode data, survey verbatims, or an approved standard file.

KPI tree — Service Profit Chain view

The point of the dashboard was linkage, not reporting volume. Kestrel reviewed two metrics per link at the operating-unit level, consistent with 20-the-service-profit-chain/05-measuring-the-chain.md:

Internal service qualityStandards reviewed in last 90 days; internal SLA adherence between billing, meter, and connections teams

Profit and growthContact cost; avoided field revisit cost; retained value from reduced churn

Causal logic. The useful chain at Kestrel was: better internal standards, clearer internal handoffs, and more credible tools for the frontline (internal quality) raised employee confidence and reduced the sense of being trapped between systems (employee condition). That improved due-by promises, explanation quality, and first-contact resolution (external value). Those changes reduced repeat contacts and protected trust in the billing and onboarding journeys (satisfaction and loyalty). The financial effects came from fewer avoidable contacts, fewer wasted field visits, and lower churn or renewal-risk in the cohorts most sensitive to service quality. See: 14-outside-in/06-measurement.md; 20-the-service-profit-chain/05-measuring-the-chain.md.

A conservative annualized value view made the case legible:

  • Contact-cost reduction: 760,000 × €5.80 → 718,000 × €5.10 = about €746k annualized cost released.
  • Fewer failed connection visits: about 3,800 avoided wasted visits × €38 = €144k.
  • Markus-like households retained through billing-trust improvements: about 1,980 retained accounts × €150 contribution = €297k.
  • Sabine-like SMEs retained through better onboarding quality: about 360 retained SMEs × €1,900 contribution = €684k.

Total conservative annualized value released or protected: about €1.9M. As in article 03, the language used with steering was contribution, not pure attribution.

09 · AI tool playbook

AI tool playbook (per engagement)

Claude Code

Role: operating system for the engagement. It maintained the standards library, authored the MOS rubric, drafted monthly narrative summaries, generated review agendas, and turned complaint clusters into standard proposals.

Concrete usage examples:

NOTE
Update standards/billing/first-bill-after-smart-meter.yml from issue Q-184.
Keep current control limit unless evidence shows 3 months of stable improvement.
Draft the PR summary in operations language, not design language.

Excelled at: structured authoring, consistent artifact maintenance, and turning service-design logic into SOPs and templates.

Failed at: tendency to make standards sound better than current operational capability. Human takeover point: any promise that affected regulated billing communication or operational capacity commitments.

OpenCode

Role: quality data pipeline, monthly dashboard build, and failure-demand attribution engine.

Concrete usage example:

TERMINAL
opencode run --agent build "Rebuild the monthly quality pack from raw extracts.
Output markdown tables for MOS, avoidable demand, root causes, and SPC links.
If any column is missing or sample size is below threshold, fail the run loudly."

Excelled at: reproducible joins across survey, complaint, and operations tables; honest pipeline output; fast rebuild of the monthly pack.

Failed at: statistical judgment if thresholds were left vague. Human takeover point: defining what constitutes avoidable demand and when a spike deserves intervention.

Codex

Role: adversarial reviewer for causal claims, sector benchmarking, and standard feasibility.

Concrete usage example:

TERMINAL
codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read monthly-pack.md and spc-linkage.csv.
Challenge whether any claimed loyalty or financial movement is unsupported.
Flag benchmark claims that lack evidence.
Return defects only."

Excelled at: hostile reading of causal claims and vague standards. It was particularly useful when Kestrel was tempted to tell a bigger value story than the data supported.

Failed at: occasional over-skepticism about utility-operational nuances. Human takeover point: final judgment on what was sector-normal versus genuinely defective for Kestrel's context.

GitHub

Role: standards-as-code repository, review gate, issue system, and automation runner.

Concrete usage examples:

  • Standards changes opened as PRs with named approvers from operations.
  • Monthly quality reviews were created as issues with agenda, pack, and decision log attached.
  • Actions rebuilt the monthly pack and validated the artifact set.

Excelled at: making quality visible, versioned, reviewable, and auditable.

Failed at: nothing material, but it only worked once the team agreed that unreviewed standards did not count as real standards.

10 · Lessons learned

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • A complaint count is not a quality system. Kestrel's breakthrough came from separating technical and functional quality and assigning named standards to moments of truth.
  • Functional quality is where utilities quietly lose trust. Bills can be numerically correct and still drive churn if the explanation is poor. That was Markus's lesson from phase 1, operationalized here.
  • Monthly review must sit with operations, not beside it. When quality review was treated as a CX ceremony, nothing changed. Once it became part of ops rhythm, standards started to move behavior.
  • Internal service quality is never backstage trivia. The billing team could not keep customer promises when meter data and scheduling systems served them poorly. This is exactly the internal service-chain logic in 19-service-management-and-marketing/05-internal-marketing-and-service-culture.md.
  • Agents will optimize for narrative neatness unless you force evidence and control limits. The anti-smoothing guardrail was not theoretical; the first draft quality pack tried to explain away a real spike in connection failures.
  • Persona continuity matters in operations. Ingrid, Markus, and Sabine were not research artifacts left on the wall. They remained active overlays on standards and review decisions.
  • Done differently next time: instrument image effects explicitly. Gronroos's model says brand image filters quality perception; Kestrel measured trust indirectly through survey text, but a direct trust item would make the loyalty linkage stronger.
