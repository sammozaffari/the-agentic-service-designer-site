01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

A global quick-service restaurant chain rolled voice-AI order takers into its drive-thrus — and quietly deleted the one human moment the channel had always guaranteed: the spoken order conversation. Efficiency went up; the personal touch went down, and the chain's own data said it plainly: drive-thru experiences "often lack personal touch and meaningful interactions." The technology stayed. Over roughly 14 weeks, a small programme team redesigned where and how humans show up around it: four named touchpoints, a scripted-but-ownable hospitality ritual, and a two-arm surprise-and-delight experiment run for one pre-selected hour a day.

+~14 ptsoverall drive-thru satisfaction, both pilots
+~3.5 ptsorder accuracy vs national, delight hour
~3,700survey responses across ~520 restaurants
10pilot restaurants · 5-vs-5 experiment

The work was deliberately low-tech. No new systems, no base-labour increase, no speed impact, no margin erosion — hard constraints, not aspirations. Pilot 1 rolled a hospitality training system (per-touchpoint cards, poor-vs-good training videos, leaderboards, bingo) across 10 restaurants, training roughly 200 crew in the first month. Pilot 2 ran a 5-vs-5 two-arm experiment — a giveaway arm versus a zero-cost handwritten-note arm — during one daily "delight hour," the tokens given to crew before customers. Results: drive-thru satisfaction up ~14 points, service satisfaction up ~7, friendliness up, negative-experience share down, no measurable speed cost — and in the intervention hour, test restaurants ran higher on order accuracy and friendliness mentions than national, lower on negative comments. The most honest finding was a null result: the tokens never appeared in customer comments. The gift was not the point; the interaction was.

What you will learn:

  • How to treat an automation rollout as a role-redesign problem rather than a technology problem — naming the human moments a machine deleted and training them like any operational skill.
  • How to run a two-arm experiment inside a live restaurant operation: one pre-selected hour, every receipt touched, a controlled comparison, and a measurement stack that separates pilot 1's effect from pilot 2's.
  • How to report a null finding honestly — the tokens no customer mentioned — and still make the case that the programme worked, because the evidence pointed at the interaction, not the gift.

The agent team (Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex) ran comment classification, the delta pipelines, and adversarial review, and produced the example artifacts; insight naming, script tone, test design, and the gates stayed with me.

02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

The client is a global quick-service restaurant chain with a dense drive-thru footprint: thousands of restaurants, a loyal but impatient customer base, and a multi-year automation agenda. A voice-AI order taker was already live across a wave of drive-thrus — it takes the spoken order, shows it on a confirmation screen, and alerts crew by headset when a customer needs help. A QR-on-receipt guest feedback survey had collected roughly 3,700 drive-thru responses over the trailing months. The chain was winning on speed and losing on warmth, and its own data said so first.

The challenge

The stated brief, in the client's own words: "We are implementing multiple new technologies to enhance our drive-thrus. While this enhances efficiency, it risks diminishing the personal touch that has been central to our brand. We need to balance technological advancement with meaningful human interactions." The data underneath it: drive-thru experiences "often lack personal touch and meaningful interactions." The channel had replaced the one guaranteed human moment — the spoken order — with a machine voice. Team members had no behavioural script for their new role, and crew confidence for customer interaction sat at ~4.1 out of 5.

The team's reframe was blunt: this is not a technology problem — it is a role and behaviour problem. The voice-AI did not need removing; the crew needed to know where they were now the experience, and how to be it. The direction-setting question carried the engagement: "Ultimately what is our goal? What do we want to be famous for? Is it that we want to be world class in customer experience and hospitality?"

Constraints

Six guiding principles were hard, non-negotiable filters for every concept; any idea that failed one was dead on arrival:

  • Low-to-no technology requirements. The human layer must not need new hardware, software, or integrations.
  • No negative impact on speed. The intervention must not cost throughput — a drive-thru second is a commercial fact, not a feeling.
  • No increase in base labour. No new staffing model, no extra hours; the ritual had to run inside existing rosters.
  • Low complexity. Something a restaurant manager can brief in a pre-shift huddle, not a multi-week change programme.
  • No margin erosion. Surprise-and-delight gestures must not decrease overall margins, and they must never be only an apology or a reaction to a negative event.
  • Four-week implementation horizon. Each pilot had to be runnable inside a single reporting period.

Why service design

This was not a marketing or script-writing problem. The experience came from a socio-technical system — the voice-AI's recognition layer, the headsets, the confirmation screen, the pay and pick-up windows, the waiting bay, and ~200 crew whose role the machine had silently redefined. The failures sat below the line of visibility: crew unsure what their job was, customers speaking to a robot with nobody listening, apologies ad hoc at the payment window instead of by design. That is what service design exists for (See: 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md). Polaine, Løvlie, and Reason's arc — research to insight, ideas, prototyping, implementation — governed the structure (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md), and the Service Profit Chain supplied the economics: internal service quality (crew enabled, confident, purposeful) drives external service value, satisfaction, and loyalty (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md). The psychology framing — the "70% of people base their buying experience on how they're treated" research — was brought to the client as published material, not client-original, and it anchored the sponsor conversation.

03 · Approach

Approach: the AI-first service design process

The engagement followed a Double Diamond with a compressed execution spine: discover → define → pilot 1 → pilot 2 → decide — fourteen weeks, two live pilots, one decision gate. Every phase ran with AI collaborators: Claude Code for research synthesis and artifact authoring, OpenCode for data pipelines and sample datasets, Codex for adversarial review, and the chain's own systems — the guest feedback platform and the voice-AI's escalation telemetry — as systems of record.

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research (weeks 1–3)

Goal. Understand what the automation had changed for customers and crew, and where the human moments still lived.

How will the customer know what to say? What do we hear through the headset?

Crew member questions, first weeks after the voice-AI rollout
Reading the evidence

Service was already the strongest available driver of satisfaction — more than half of the ~2,600 verbatim comments praised a crew member before any intervention ran.

Method & SOP. Triangulated desk work rather than greenfield fieldwork: a competitor scan of how other quick-service brands handled the automation-versus-humanity tension; desktop research on automation and social disconnection in service environments; and — the load-bearing evidence — the chain's own guest feedback: ~3,700 drive-thru responses with ~2,600 verbatim comments. The team mapped the automated flow end to end, customer and crew side, from the ground-loop trigger to the waiting bay.

AI execution. A Claude Code subagent team ran the comment-classifier SOP in Section 7: one agent extracted themes (service, product quality, speed, accuracy, value), one verified every quoted snippet verbatim against the export (the key hallucination control), one triaged crew-recognition comments into a separate stream — every claim traced to source rows. The feedback platform's API supplied the weekly numbers.

Human designer judgment. The decision to treat the ~3,700-response dataset as the primary evidence base (not new interviews — the window was too tight), the psychology research as sponsor framing, and mapping the happy path before full journey breadth.

4.2 Synthesis (weeks 4–5)

Goal. Turn the evidence register into a small set of insight cards that would survive contact with the operations team.

Method & SOP. Collaborative affinity synthesis in the three-part syntax — observed behaviour → root cause → design implication — followed by a Codex challenge pass and a half-day wall session with ops and marketing.

AI execution. Claude Code proposed clusters with active-sentence names (the bare-noun bucket trap was disabled in the prompt); Codex attacked each card with the strongest alternative explanation it could find. One card — "automation stripped the spoken order" — survived; one hypothesis — "customers want the robot gone" — was killed: the evidence said customers never asked for the machine to leave, they asked to be treated well.

Human designer judgment. The naming of the six final insights, the decision to keep the null-finding on tokens as a card rather than a footnote, and the framing of service as "the measurable product."

4.3 Mapping (weeks 4–5)

Goal. Locate the human moments the automation had erased and design the crew role around them.

Method & SOP. The customer and crew happy paths were mapped for the automated flow, then fused into a touchpoint architecture: four points of human connection — the order point, the payment window, the pick-up window, the waiting bay — each with a training card of micro-behaviours and scripts. The fail points all resolved to one ritual: the voice-AI's recognition failures (bad days, uncomfortable customers, app orders, communication trouble) end in an in-person apology at the payment window. The automation is front-stage technology but back-stage labour: its voice is the brand's voice, so the human layer must catch what the machine cannot — tone, empathy, eye contact, and the apology.

AI execution. Claude Code authored the lane diagrams and the first-pass training cards; OpenCode built the letter-coded leaderboard mock.

Human designer judgment. The four-touchpoint cut itself (not five, not two), the "automation is back-stage labour" reading, and the decision that the waiting bay — not the order point — was where surprise-and-delight belonged.

4.4 Ideation (weeks 4–5)

Goal. Design a pilot 2 concept that could run inside the guiding principles: no new tech, no speed cost, no base labour, no margin erosion, no apology-only gestures, four-week horizon.

Method & SOP. How-might-we over the insight cards; every candidate idea run through the six principles as a decision filter; feasibility reviewed for complexity and cost.

What was designed. A two-arm surprise-and-delight experiment. Giveaway arm: small branded merchandise and occasional 10% discounts, delivered gamified (spin the wheel, pick a number, lucky dip, flip a card). Handwritten-note arm: branded affirmation cards crew could add a note to — zero cost. Both arms: one token with every receipt during one pre-selected hour. Plus a surprise-first mechanic: managers surprised crew with tokens for a week before the customer hour, so crew could "experience being surprised and delighted and then reciprocate this to customers."

What was rejected. Food and voucher giveaways (register-management codes, tracking friction); numbered-customer targeting (complexity, gimmick risk); the 10% discount as a standalone mechanic (it needed commercial sign-off, so it folded into the giveaway arm); and any apology-only gesture — banned by principle.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the concept sheets; Codex stress-tested the design and flagged that the automation-rollout variable would confound the two arms unless balanced — the arms were split across automation-era and non-automation stores deliberately.

Human designer judgment. The intervention hour was chosen by operational economics: a pre-selected low-volume hour carried lower transaction volume (less gift stock, less cost, less storage), lower staffing stress, and higher baseline satisfaction — a cost, labour, and measurement optimisation in one. The bounded hour also made the hype exclusive: "one hour, every receipt, maximum effort — not required to sustain over long periods," which sharply reduced training burden.

4.5 Pilot 1 — hospitality rollout (weeks 6–9)

Goal. Install the four-touchpoint hospitality behaviours as a trained, reinforced operational skill across 10 restaurants (a cluster of suburban restaurants, 5 automation-era + 5 control), then measure.

Method & SOP. The training system: manager and rollout meetings with all 10 restaurant managers; poor-vs-good per-touchpoint videos plus a what/why/what's-in-it-for-me intro; key cards at each touchpoint; a comms board; a pre-shift poster and run-sheet; bingo; and a store-by-store satisfaction leaderboard with weekly winners. Baseline was captured two weeks before launch; rollout happened on a single day; hypercare ran to the end of the reporting period. Roughly 200 crew were trained, ~50 by mid-morning on day one.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the training scripts from the insight cards (the micro-scripts below came out of this pass); the feedback platform produced the weekly reports; OpenCode maintained the leaderboard mock.

Human designer judgment. The scripting standard — "we encourage the team to make it their own so it sounds natural and feels good for them" — authenticity over compliance, written into every card. The grandma rule and the exact-wait-time language came from the restaurant managers in review, not the design team.

Artifacts. The training kit (Section 5), the leaderboard, the weekly reports, and the hypercare log.

4.6 Pilot 2 — surprise-and-delight, two arms (weeks 10–14)

Goal. Test whether a surprise moment layered on the hospitality foundation could lift satisfaction and word-of-mouth without breaking the guiding principles.

Method & SOP. A 5-vs-5 two-arm experiment (giveaway vs handwritten-note) with a control group, run during one pre-selected hour daily (a pre-selected low-volume hour) for one reporting period only. Every receipt in the hour received a token. Internal surprise came first: for one week managers surprised crew with tokens, then crew surprised customers. A "surprise licence" let one crew member per shift gift a free upsize, item, or discount. Mystery shoppers monitored execution; a crew survey ran at week 1; weekly reports continued. The programme was promoted only by word of mouth — doubling as a free uptake detector.

AI execution. OpenCode ran the delta pipeline (test vs national, intervention hour only) and generated the sample dataset (Section 5, A6); Claude Code drafted the weekly readouts; Codex adversarially reviewed the after-action analysis, including a direct challenge to the token null-finding.

Human designer judgment. Keeping the experiment honest: the hour was fixed and pre-registered; stock was sized from historical transaction data with an uplift adjustment; and the team requested hourly-level reports from the feedback platform — a capability it didn't have built in, and which was never fully delivered.

4.7 Measurement and the decision (week 14)

Goal. Attribute the results, tell the truth about what didn't work, and give the business a decision.

+~7 ptsservice satisfaction · ~54% → ~61%
−~4 ptsnegative-experience share, all channels
+~3 ptsfriendliness in drive-thru comments
~200crew trained across 10 restaurants

Method & SOP. Weekly reporting through both pilots; period-over-period cuts to separate pilot 1's effect from pilot 2's; test-vs-national comparisons restricted to the intervention hour; the reintroduced mystery-shopper program; and an after-action review structured context → what was done → results → recommendation.

Results. Test-group overall drive-thru satisfaction rose from the high 60s to the low 80s (+~14 points) and service satisfaction rose ~54 to ~61 (+~7) across both pilots; friendliness rose ~3 points and negative-experience share fell ~4 overall, ~3 in drive-thru (Section 8). In the intervention hour, test restaurants beat national on service satisfaction, service quality, order accuracy, positive sentiment, and friendliness mentions (Figure 5), with negative comments lower and order speed flat. The tokens themselves appeared in almost no comments — the honest null finding. The giveaway arm edged the note arm on average. One caveat is owed: a ~14-point lift measured over a short pilot window on 10 restaurants that knew they were being watched carries novelty and Hawthorne exposure, so sustained measurement through the national rollout is the follow-up that decides how much of the effect holds.

The interaction was the gift.

The programme's honest null finding

The decision. The recommendation was adopted: pilot 1's hospitality behaviours become the foundation of the chain's drive-thru operating standard, rolled out nationally through the annual operating calendar; pilot 2's surprise-and-delight hour becomes a periodic engagement driver rather than a daily standard. Expansion was proposed to the front counter, delivery, and self-service channels with consistent human-connection metrics.

06 · Use cases

Use cases

Use case A — The mishear handoff

Scenario. The voice-AI mishears an order — a "bad day" for the speech recognition, an uncomfortable customer, an app order, a communication breakdown. Before the programme this was a silent failure: the wrong food arrived, discovered at the pick-up window, the interaction souring at the moment the brand could least afford it.

What was designed. A named handoff ritual. The recognition system alerts crew the moment a help request fires; crew take over before the customer gets frustrated; the apology happens in person at the payment window — "serve them in person," the card says — with a light, human touch about the machine still learning. The order is re-verified with eye contact and warmth, and the failure becomes the moment the brand is actually experienced.

AI working session. The voice-AI's escalation telemetry (alert tones, takeover buttons) was mapped into the blueprint as a back-stage trigger for a front-stage behaviour. Claude Code drafted the takeover lines; Codex flagged the original joke as risky for unfamiliar customers — softened before rollout.

Outcome. Escalation-related complaints trended down in the test group across both pilots.

Use case B — The waiting bay, kept honest

Scenario. A slow spell; cars parked in the waiting bay with no information and no contact. Silence reads as abandonment.

What was designed. Exact-wait-time honesty, progress updates, and number-plate-and-order recall: crew greet the parked car by the order, give a real number ("about five minutes for fresh chicken"), update when the kitchen slips, and use the wait for a surprise gesture. The grandma rule — treat every customer as if it were your own grandmother — was the coachable standard, not a values poster.

AI working session. OpenCode pulled the bay-wait verbatim comments so the team could hear the silence as customers wrote it; Claude Code grouped them into the wait-time and no-update behaviours on the card.

Outcome. Negative comments during the intervention hour ran ~2.5 points lower than national; bay-related comments in the test group shifted from "waited with no word" to "they told me exactly how long."

Use case C — Surprise the crew first

Scenario. A surprise gesture delivered by crew who have never received one tends to land as a chore. The team inverted the sequence.

What was designed. One week before the customer hour launched, managers were given tokens to surprise their own crew — the intent being that crew "experience being surprised and delighted and then reciprocate this to customers." On top ran the make-somebody's-day challenge: one crew member per shift may gift a free upsize, item, or discount at their own judgment.

AI working session. Claude Code drafted the challenge briefing and manager cheat-sheet; Codex reviewed the gifting rules against the margin principle and flagged that the free-item licence needed a per-shift cap — one gesture per shift.

Outcome. The week-1 crew survey rated the training materials useful and customer confidence up from the ~4.1 baseline; managers reported visibly higher willingness to engage at the windows — a training mechanic disguised as a perk.

Use case D — The delight hour rhythm

Scenario. Sustained delight is expensive and exhausting; one hour is not.

What was designed. One pre-selected hour per day (a pre-selected low-volume hour) for one reporting period only. Every receipt in the hour receives a token; the hour is briefed at pre-shift; the team gives it full attention because it is bounded — "not required to sustain over long periods" — which sharply cut the training required. Promoted only by word of mouth, the hour doubled as a free uptake detector: customers asking about it at other times were organic proof the surprise was spreading.

AI working session. Hourly transaction and sales data for the 10 test restaurants (intervention hour vs the hour after) was pulled so the hour's economics — gift cost absorbed by a low-traffic window — could be checked against the margin principle. OpenCode computed the test-vs-national deltas for the after-action deck.

Outcome. During the hour, test restaurants beat national across the service metrics (Figure 5) with no speed cost; the tokens were invisible in the comments — the interaction was the gift.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Three SOPs were encoded for agent execution, each following the same pattern: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Comment-theme classifier SOP (research and measurement). Output: JSONL theme items from the feedback platform's verbatim comments. Acceptance: every quoted snippet verbatim-verifiable against the export; every item carries theme, sentiment, recognition flag, and source row; no PII survives. Gate: human spot-check of 10% before merge. The prompt template:
NOTE
You are COMMENT-CLASSIFIER. Input: one row of a drive-thru feedback export.
Classify the verbatim comment. Output EXACTLY:
- item_id: CC-<source_row>-<seq>
- theme: product | service | speed | accuracy | value | other
- sentiment: -2..+2
- recognition_flag: 1 if the comment praises a crew member by name or role
- token_flag: 1 if the comment mentions a giveaway item or handwritten note
- text: the verbatim comment (never paraphrase)
RULES: quote text must be copy-paste exact from the export; if the comment
contains a person's name, a store code, or any identifying detail, replace
it with [NAME]/[STORE] before output and set pii_redacted: true. Never
invent a theme for an empty comment. Output JSONL.
  1. Delight-hour operating SOP (implementation). The one-hour ritual as SOP-as-code — schedule, owner, steps, acceptance, gate — so any restaurant manager or agent can execute or audit it:
CONFIG
sop: QSR-HOSP-001
name: Delight-hour operating procedure
version: 1.2
owner: restaurant_manager
schedule: daily 14:00-15:00, one reporting period only
steps:
  - pre_shift: brief the hour, assign one token bearer per window
  - stock: verify token stock sized from historical transactions
  - execute: one token with every receipt in the hour
  - script: crew may adapt the line; must sound natural
  - handoff: voice-AI mishears > crew takeover before frustration
acceptance:
  - every receipt in the hour carries a token (mystery-shopper check)
  - zero complaints about the gesture itself
  - throughput within +/-1% of the hour before
gate: weekly ops review; period-end decision on continuation
constraints: no margin erosion; no apology-only gestures; no base labour
  1. Measurement SOP (measurement). Weekly satisfaction, throughput, and complaint reports from the feedback platform; crew survey at week 1 of each pilot; mystery-shopper visits; and the attribution discipline — period-over-period cuts (before pilot 1 vs the pilot-1 period vs the pilot-2 period) so the two pilots' effects can be separated, plus test-vs-national comparisons restricted to the intervention hour.

The meta-SOP. The engagement itself ran research → synthesize → map → ideate → prototype → pilot → measure → decide, with a hard gate between stages: no ideation before the insight cards passed the challenge pass; no pilot 2 before pilot 1's weekly reports were reviewed; no scale recommendation before the null finding was confronted. Each gate was a review with the operations sponsor.

Guardrails.

  • Customer privacy. No real customer PII in any prompt, ever: agents worked on the pseudonymized export with names, store codes, and receipt references redacted, and the sample dataset is generated with a fixed seed.
  • Hallucination control. Quote verification ran on every synthesis pass: a quoted string not found verbatim in the export failed the review. Insight cards without source rows failed schema validation.
  • Commercial sensitivity. Store-level satisfaction ladders and hourly transaction economics stayed internal.
  • Human sign-off points. Training content; the intervention-hour selection; concept selection for both arms; pilot 1 go; pilot 2 go; the scale decision.

Failure modes observed. (1) Over-clustering in the first synthesis pass — 60-plus near-duplicate clusters; fixed with active-sentence names plus a similarity merge. (2) Thin afternoon samples: most test restaurants returned only a handful of responses per daypart, so hourly-level attribution was never fully delivered — the team requested the capability and reported the gap openly. (3) The AI-ordering-experience question drew zero responses — the voice-AI's direct effect was never measured; the team measured around it. (4) The token null finding was nearly buried in the after-action deck because it read as a failure; the review gate kept it in — and it became the programme's most useful lesson.

Causal logic (Service Profit Chain). The chain ran as Heskett, Sasser, and Schlesinger describe: internal service quality — crew re-skilled, given a script they could own, rewarded publicly, and surprised themselves — raised frontline capability; external service value rose through the service layer specifically (service satisfaction +~7, friendliness up, recognition comments up); satisfaction and loyalty followed; and the economics stayed clean because the guiding principles were constraints, not hopes: zero base-labour increase, no speed impact, gift cost absorbed by the low-traffic hour (the note arm at zero cost). The defensive effect deserves its own line: negative-experience share fell and negative comments ran lower — the programme prevented dissatisfaction at least as much as it delighted, and prevention is the cheaper win.

KPI tree (abridged). North-star: service satisfaction in the drive-thru channel. Leading drivers: touchpoint behaviour execution (mystery-shopper score), wait-time honesty (bay comments), surprise execution (token coverage in the hour), order accuracy at the intervention hour. Lagging: overall satisfaction, negative-experience share, friendliness share, repeat-visit intent, crew confidence and tenure. Reviewed weekly in the pilots; proposed monthly in run-state, extended to the front counter, delivery, and self-service channels.

10 · Lessons

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • A simple, repeatable ritual beats grand gestures. The intervention was compressed to one pre-selected hour: "ability for the team to give it 100% attention, not required to sustain over long periods." The hour cut training burden, gift cost, and operational stress at once — constraints were converted into design features, not accepted as limitations.
  • Let the crew own the script. "We encourage the team to make it their own so it sounds natural and feels good for them" was written into the training cards. Authenticity over compliance is what made the micro-scripts land; a compliance poster would have produced robot apologies at the pay window — the exact failure the voice-AI was already causing.
  • Experience-then-reciprocate is a training mechanic disguised as a perk. Surprising crew before they surprised customers produced the week-1 crew-survey gains; the internal gesture taught the behaviour more cheaply than any video.
  • The gift is not the point; the interaction is. The tokens never appeared in customer comments — a null result on the gift, redeemed by everything around it. The giveaway arm still edged the note arm on averages, leaving an unresolved cost-vs-outcome tension for scaling; the team chose the honest framing over a hero narrative.
  • Measure what you claim, and admit what you can't. The after-action materials said it themselves: "metrics to be refined," "can we get to the hourly level?," period-over-period cuts to separate pilot 1 from pilot 2. The AI-ordering-experience question drew zero responses — the voice-AI's direct effect was never measured. Reporting the gaps before anyone else could is what made the numbers believable.
  • Word of mouth doubles as a measurement device. Promoting the delight hour only by word of mouth meant customers asking about it at other times were organic uptake evidence — free tracking of the exact behaviour the programme wanted to cause.
  • Done differently next time: pre-register hourly-level reporting before the pilot starts (the capability arrived late and thin samples limited attribution), and score the baseline with the Good Services principles in week 1 rather than discovering the gap later (See: 05-good-services/02-the-fifteen-principles.md).
