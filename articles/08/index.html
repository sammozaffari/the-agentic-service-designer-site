Personas from Messy Data: Evidence-Backed Segmentation at Kestrel Energy

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Kestrel Energy is a fictional composite client, assembled from patterns observed across several European energy retailers running regulated smart-meter rollouts; no real utility, customer, or employee is depicted. All names, figures, datasets, clusters, and quotes are illustrative and anonymized — every customer identifier in this article is a synthetic hash, and every quote comes from a fictional participant code. The article shares knowledge and process — the persona pipeline, SOPs, prompts, validation gates — never client specifics. The engagement ran AI-first: Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were the primary working tools, with the human designer retaining framing, ethics, and sign-off authority.

1. Executive summary

Kestrel Energy, a fictional EU utility with 2.4 million household and 180,000 SME accounts, was midway through a mandated smart-meter rollout and a digital account-service relaunch — and was designing both against a segmentation no one trusted: four demographic buckets ("young families", "seniors", "urban professionals", "business") inherited from a 2019 marketing study. The organization had, meanwhile, been sitting on an embarrassment of evidence it could not use: 2.4M customer records with inconsistent fields, 1.9M contact-log rows, 400 interviews and 60 diary studies from three prior research efforts, 28,400 survey verbatims, and privacy-scoped billing and usage telemetry. Over 20 weeks, one service designer and an AI agent team turned that mess into a defensible behavioral segmentation — 7 household and 4 SME clusters fused from quantitative clustering and qualitative typology — and three decision-grade personas that now anchor the smart-meter journey, the digital service, and every journey map and brief in the portfolio. On illustrative figures: persona coverage 96.4% of accounts, self-serve adoption 41% → 57%, contact volume −21%, price-vigilant switching 19% → 13.5%, and ≈ €2.6M annualized contact-cost avoidance.

What you will learn:

  • How to build personas from hybrid evidence — CRM records, contact logs, interviews, diaries, telemetry — with every claim traceable to a source row, not to vibes.
  • How to fuse quantitative clustering (k-means, hierarchical) with qualitative typology, and how to cross-check the fusion with an independent adversarial derivation.
  • How to validate personas like a measurement instrument: coverage, distinctness, size tests, anti-stereotype guardrails — and how to land them in the organization so journey maps and briefs actually get rewritten.

2. The brief

Client portrait

Kestrel Energy retails electricity and gas to roughly 2.4 million household accounts and 180,000 SME accounts across two EU member states, with a field force of about 900 installation and maintenance technicians, a 420-seat contact centre, and a digital estate consisting of a web portal (2016 vintage), a mobile app relaunched in 2024, and a chatbot of contested value. The national regulator's metering mandate requires substantially complete smart-meter deployment by 2032; Kestrel had installed 1.1 million meters (46% of households) when the engagement started. Digital maturity was middling: 41% of household accounts were monthly-active on digital self-serve, and the contact centre absorbed about 950,000 contacts per year at a blended cost of €7.10 each.

The challenge

The smart-meter program was underperforming in exactly the places customer understanding should have predicted. Only 22% of install appointments were self-booked digitally; the rest were booked by phone after an average of 1.8 reminder letters, and 14% of appointments failed on the day (customer not home, access blocked, meter cabinet not found). Post-install, the "energy insights" feature — the flagship justification of the rollout — had 9% monthly engagement. Meanwhile the contact mix told its own story of unmet needs: billing and tariff confusion drove 31% of all contacts, meter readings 11%, install logistics 17%. And the executive team had three mutually contradictory beliefs about "the customer": marketing swore by the 2019 demographic buckets, operations insisted "customers just want to be left alone," and the digital team believed everyone was one good push-notification away from app engagement. All three were assumption-based views, never tested against the data the company already owned (See: 09-mapping-experiences/03-understanding-the-user.md — research-based vs assumption-based understanding).

The deeper problem was that the evidence existed but was unusable raw: 214 distinct spellings of move-out reason codes, duplicate accounts from a 2021 billing-system migration, inconsistent address and tariff fields; contact logs mixing agent-coded reasons with free text; and three research programs (2022 billing, 2023 app, 2024 smart-meter) whose 400 interview transcripts and 60 diary studies had never been synthesized together. Nobody could say, with evidence, how many kinds of customer Kestrel actually had.

Constraints

Budget €210,000 all-in; 20 weeks to a validated persona set because the smart-meter program's next planning gate was fixed; GDPR plus national metering-data rules that treat quarter-hourly usage data as near-special-category (the engagement was restricted to daily aggregates, pseudonymized, EU-resident processing, no individual-level disclosure — cluster-level outputs only); a 2011-era customer master with batch overnight sync; and a hard rule from the data protection officer that no model output could ever name or single out an individual customer.

Why service design

This was textbook customer understanding work in the Outside In sense: an industrialized capability that converts evidence into decision-grade personas the whole organization can reason against (See: 14-outside-in/04-customer-understanding.md). Kumar's Know People → Frame Insights arc supplied the synthesis discipline — observations to findings to insights, with the acid test that an insight must change what gets designed (See: 17-101-design-methods/04-mode-3-know-people.md, 05-mode-4-frame-insights.md). Polaine, Løvlie and Reason's data → information → insight hierarchy gave the team its shared epistemology (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md), and Martin and Hanington's behavioral-persona standard — archetypes as boundary objects, not demographic portraits — set the artifact bar (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/05-representational-and-synthesis-methods.md). Demographics describe; behavior predicts. Kestrel needed prediction.

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

The engagement followed the Double Diamond with the Mode 3 → Mode 4 arc as the spine. The designer ran every phase with named AI collaborators: OpenCode for the data-cleaning and feature pipeline, a Claude Code agent team for clustering runs, qualitative synthesis, and persona authoring, Codex for the independent typology cross-check and adversarial review, and GitHub as the system of record — every claim a PR, every gate a review, every quote verified in CI.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Discover (weeks 1–6): audit the mess, surface the assumptions

Goal. Establish what evidence exists, what state it is in, and what the organization currently believes — before touching a model.

Method & SOP. Two parallel moves. First, an assumption-mapping workshop with 14 stakeholders (marketing, operations, digital, metering program, DPO) to extract every working belief about "the customer" into an assumption register — hypothesis views to be tested, never truth (See: 09-mapping-experiences/03-understanding-the-user.md). Second, a structured source audit: every dataset profiled for volume, schema drift, missingness, join keys, and lawful basis, logged against a POEMS-flavored inventory of the service's people, objects, environments, messages, and services (See: 17-101-design-methods/04-mode-3-know-people.md).

AI execution. Claude Code profiled the five source systems in one afternoon using a profiling SOP: column-level missingness, cardinality, format drift, and candidate join keys per extract — work that would have taken an analyst two weeks. The output was brutal: 2,412,318 household records, of which 61,204 (2.5%) were migration duplicates; 214 move-out spellings collapsing to 9 real codes; 18.2% of records missing at least one of address, tariff, or payment-method fields; contact reason-codes that disagreed with the free text in 23% of sampled rows. The assumption workshop produced 41 belief statements, of which the audit later confirmed only 17. GitHub carried the register: one issue per assumption, closed by evidence, not by opinion.

Human designer judgment. The designer negotiated the DPIA boundary with the DPO before any pipeline was built: daily usage aggregates only, pseudonymization at ingestion, cluster-level outputs only, no household-level model scores leaving the analytics zone. This constraint shaped everything downstream — and is the reason the final personas are defensible in front of the regulator.

Artifacts produced. Source inventory, DPIA, assumption register (41 items), data-quality baseline.

4.2 Define (weeks 5–10): clean, build features, cluster

Goal. Convert five messy sources into a clean feature store, then find the quantitative partition that best separates behavioral kinds of customer.

Method & SOP. The data → information rung of the hierarchy (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md): normalize, deduplicate, pseudonymize, then engineer behavioral features across six spectrums — payment posture, channel posture, contact posture, price posture, digital posture, consumption posture — the behavioral-spectrum approach of behavioral persona construction rather than demographic bucketing (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/05-representational-and-synthesis-methods.md). Then cluster: k-means across a k sweep, hierarchical Ward as an independent partition, compare.

AI execution. OpenCode ran the entire pipeline in agent mode. The pipeline was specified as code and reviewed as a PR:

opencode run --agent build "Build pipeline/ in Python 3.12:
1. ingest/: read raw exports (customer master, contact logs, billing,
   telemetry daily aggregates, survey verbatims). Validate every row against
   pydantic schemas in schemas/; quarantine rejects to quarantine/ with reason.
2. clean/: dedup by fuzzy address+name key (threshold 0.92), normalize the 214
   move-out spellings to the 9-code table in ref/moveout_codes.yaml, then
   pseudonymize: customer_id -> HMAC-SHA256 with key from env KES_PSEUDO_KEY.
   Never write raw names or full addresses downstream.
3. features/: one row per account -> 34 features (annual_spend, payment_method,
   direct_debit_failures_24m, contacts_24m, channel_mix_phone_share,
   reason_mix_top1, price_related_contact_share, portal_mau, app_mau,
   self_serve_success_rate, smart_meter_flag, daily_kwh_cv, ev_pv_flag,
   tenure_years, move_count_5y, ...). Write features/household.parquet and
   features/sme.parquet. Log every transformation count to run_report.md."

The clustering ran as a Claude Code agent team: one agent swept k-means for k = 4…12 with silhouette, Calinski-Harabasz, and stability under 50 bootstrap resamples; a second ran Ward hierarchical clustering cut at 5…9; a third reconciled the partitions (adjusted Rand index 0.71 at k = 7 for households — good agreement between independent methods). The k = 7 household solution (silhouette 0.41) and k = 4 SME solution (0.44) were selected; Codex challenged k-selection adversarially and its strongest point — that C5 and C7 might be one cluster — was tested and rejected: merging them collapsed silhouette to 0.36 and mixed radically different churn risks.

In parallel, the qualitative stream: the 400 transcripts and 60 diary studies were processed by the transcript-to-evidence SOP (Section 7) into 4,712 evidence items, affinity-clustered on the synthesis wall into a qualitative typology of 9 behavioral types — built with zero access to the quantitative clusters, deliberately.

Human designer judgment. Feature selection is framing: the designer excluded raw income proxies and property-value estimates from the feature set on both ethical and predictive grounds (they stereotype more than they predict), and kept direct-debit failure count because it predicts service need, not worth.

Artifacts produced. Cleaned feature store (2.35M household + 178k SME rows after dedup), data-quality report, candidate partitions, qualitative typology v1.

4.3 Develop (weeks 9–14): fuse, author, validate

Goal. Fuse the quantitative partition with the qualitative typology into one segmentation, author personas anchored to verified quotes, and pass validation gates before anyone is allowed to use them.

Method & SOP. Fusion is the Frame Insights move: two independent lenses on the same population, reconciled explicitly (See: 17-101-design-methods/05-mode-4-frame-insights.md). Then persona authoring as boundary objects with design directives (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/05-representational-and-synthesis-methods.md, 14-outside-in/04-customer-understanding.md), then instrument-grade validation.

AI execution. The fusion workshop (9 people, one day) mapped the 9 qualitative types onto the 7+4 quantitative clusters: 8 mapped cleanly; one qualitative type ("the delegation-dependent elder") split across C2 and C5 — resolved by keeping it as a cross-cluster modifier (delegation flag) rather than a cluster. Codex then ran the engagement's key cross-check: an independent typology from qualitative data only, instructed to ignore the existing segmentation entirely:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read synthesis/evidence-register.jsonl and
research/diary-summaries/. Derive a behavioral typology of Kestrel customers
from first principles: behavioral dimensions, then types, each type defined by
observable behavior and anchored to evidence IDs. Do NOT read
segmentation/clusters.md or any file mentioning cluster codes. Then compare
your typology to segmentation/clusters.md and report: convergence, divergence,
and which side has the better evidence per disagreement. Be adversarial."

Convergence: 9 of 11 clusters matched Codex's independent types; both divergences improved the model (Codex split "eco-optimizers" from a tech-enthusiast group k-means had merged into C4; the fusion kept C6 separate, justified by EV/PV telemetry).

Persona authoring used the quote-anchored prompt in Section 7: Claude Code drafted 3 decision-grade personas (A5) from the fused profiles, every attribute carrying an evidence ID, every quote verbatim-verified in CI. Validation gates: G1 coverage — 96.4% of accounts assignable at confidence ≥ 0.6 (target ≥ 90%); G2 distinctness — silhouette 0.41/0.44, minimum cluster share 4%, every persona pair separated by ≥ 2 significantly different behavioral features; G3 anti-stereotype audit — an agent pass plus a human panel against the audit template (A6): no attribute without evidence, none that merely decorates a demographic, no deficit framing.

Human designer judgment. The designer killed a proposed eighth household persona ("the vulnerable customer") — ethically real, methodologically wrong: vulnerability is a state that moves across clusters, not a type. It became a service-standard overlay instead. Naming the three flagship personas was also a human call: names had to be memorable to a field technician, not to a marketing department.

Artifacts produced. Fused segmentation (7 + 4 clusters), 3 decision-grade personas (A5), validation report, the persona development pipeline diagram (Section 5).

4.4 Deliver (weeks 13–20): adoption is the deliverable

Goal. A persona set nobody uses is a poster. The deliverable is cutover: journey maps, briefs, campaign segmentation, and service standards rewritten against the new clusters.

Method & SOP. Socialization per Outside In: personas embedded into PRDs, journey reviews, and frontline onboarding, with an outer-loop owner accountable (See: 14-outside-in/04-customer-understanding.md).

AI execution. GitHub carried the cutover as 68 issues: regenerate each of the 9 active journey maps per cluster lens, rewrite 12 campaign briefs, update the smart-meter booking journey (A1/A2), and add cluster codes to the campaign tool's segment picker. Claude Code regenerated journey maps as mermaid PRs with the new persona lens and evidence tags, each requiring the journey owner's review. OpenCode built the adoption dashboard: which teams reference which persona in which artifact, weekly.

Human designer judgment. The designer ran the two-hour persona immersion for the contact-centre leadership and the metering program board, and made the one irreversible call: the 2019 demographic buckets were formally retired — removed from the campaign tool, not merely deprecated. Second conventions beside the first is how segmentations die.

Artifacts produced. Persona card set, cutover backlog (68 issues, 61 closed at handover), adoption dashboard, handover SOP pack.

Persona validation checklist

PERSONA VALIDATION CHECKLIST — run at G1-G3, all boxes required
[ ] Coverage: >=90% of population assigned at confidence >=0.6
[ ] Distinctness: mean silhouette >=0.35; each persona differs from every
    other on >=2 behavioral features (p<0.05, effect size reported)
[ ] Size: every cluster >=3% of population or explicitly justified
[ ] Evidence: every persona attribute traces to >=1 evidence ID; every
    quote verbatim-verified against the transcript store (CI)
[ ] Convergence: independent derivation (second method or second model)
    matches >=8 of 11 clusters; divergences documented and resolved
[ ] Stability: cluster assignment stable under bootstrap resampling (ARI)
[ ] Anti-stereotype audit: completed template, human panel sign-off
[ ] Utility: each persona has >=3 testable design directives
[ ] Privacy: no individual-level scores exported; cluster-level only
[ ] Review date set (max 12 months)

6. Use cases

Use case A — Digitally disengaged seniors: the assisted digital path

Scenario. Ingrid (persona 1) receives the smart-meter install notice. Before the redesign, the letter's only door was a QR code; her cluster generated 78% of its bookings reactively by phone after reminder letters, and 14% of appointments failed.

What was designed. The assisted digital path: a two-door letter (QR and a phone number answered within 3 minutes), a proactive outbound call to C2 and delegation-flagged accounts at week 2 if unbooked, an agent booking screen offering the same 2-hour slots as the self-serve picker, a paper confirmation with an access checklist in 14pt type, and a day-before call naming the technician. Delegation is first-class: with Ingrid's consent, her son can hold the booking and receive reminders while the account stays hers.

AI working session. Claude Code drafted the letter at CEFR B1 from the design directives, then a subagent red-teamed it against the artifact audit's jargon list ("metering point", "estimated consumption" — both removed). OpenCode seeded the booking prototype with the mock dataset so hallway tests used realistic slot capacities; Codex reviewed the delegation consent flow and caught an over-broad default (delegates could see billing history — narrowed to appointment logistics only).

Outcome (illustrative). C2 self-serve-or-assisted booking 22% → 81%; failed appointments in C2 18% → 5%; letter-triggered calls to the contact centre −44%.

Use case B — Price super-vigilants: the smart-meter alerts service

Scenario. Markus (persona 2) is the segment Kestrel was losing: 19% annual switching, 4.1 broker-site checks a year, and a track record of leaving within 60 days of an unexplained tariff letter. The energy-insights feature he should love sat at 9% engagement because it showed kilowatt-hours, not euros.

What was designed. The alerts service: spend-threshold alerts in euros ("your October spend is tracking €38 above your 12-month norm — main driver: EV charging on-peak"), a tariff-fit check that runs his actual usage curve against his tariff quarterly and shows the arithmetic either way — including when staying put costs him money versus a Kestrel alternative — and a tariff-change letter that shows the calculation before the justification. Honesty is the retention strategy: the segment's own data said theatrics accelerate switching.

AI working session. The threshold model ran through the cluster-run SOP pattern: an OpenCode pipeline computed per-cluster seasonal norms from daily aggregates; Claude Code drafted alert copy with the anti-stereotype rule applied ("vigilant" not "obsessive"); Codex attacked the tariff-fit economics — showing customers a cheaper Kestrel tariff cannibalizes margin — and steering accepted the trade explicitly: a retained vigilant customer on a fair tariff outvalues a churned one.

Outcome (illustrative). C3 switching 19% → 13.5%; alerts engagement 61% monthly among opt-ins (opt-in 74% of C3); price-related contacts −29% as self-answered questions moved into the app.

Use case C — SME facilities multitaskers: consolidated billing

Scenario. Sabine (persona 3) manages 3 sites and receives 6 invoices on 4 different dates through 3 separate portal logins. Her cluster generated 44% of SME invoice queries and held the highest SME churn risk at renewal, when contract letters went to the wrong site.

What was designed. Consolidated billing: one monthly invoice across sites and fuels with a per-site breakdown matching her accounting export format, one login with a portfolio view, one due date, and a renewal calendar pushed at 90/60/30 days to her registered contact — not the site address. The design directives came verbatim from the persona card: "one supplier, one bill, one login."

AI working session. GitHub carried the build as 14 issues traced to blueprint step IDs; the invoice layout went through three PR iterations with Sabine's accounting-package export as the acceptance test. Claude Code generated the mock consolidated invoice from the dataset for the advisory-panel review; the panel's one change (group by site, then by fuel — not the reverse) went in before build.

Outcome (illustrative). S1 invoice queries −41%; consolidated-billing uptake 68% of S1 within two quarters; SME renewal-at-risk rate in S1 17% → 11%.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Four SOPs ran as agent-executable contracts, each following method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Persona-build SOP. Steps: load cluster profile → pull all evidence items tagged to the cluster → draft attributes only where ≥1 evidence ID exists → attach verbatim quote (CI-verified) → run distinctiveness tests → run anti-stereotype audit → human panel → publish as PR. Acceptance: no attribute without evidence; ≥3 testable design directives; quote verification green. The prompt template:
You are PERSONA-BUILDER. Input: segmentation/cluster-C2-profile.json plus
synthesis/evidence-register.jsonl filtered to cluster C2.
Draft a persona card following templates/persona-card.md EXACTLY.
RULES: every attribute must cite at least one evidence_id present in the
register; the quote field must be copy-paste verbatim from a register item
(include participant_code and source_lines); if the register does not support
an attribute, write "NO EVIDENCE" and leave the field blank — never infer.
Stats fields: compute only from the numbers in the cluster profile.
Output: markdown card + a json sidecar of {attribute: [evidence_ids]}.
  1. Anti-hallucination SOP (quote verification). Every synthesis or persona PR triggers a CI job: each quoted string must exist verbatim in the transcript store; each evidence ID must resolve; each statistic must recompute from the feature store. A quote not found fails the build. This caught 11 fabricated or misattributed quotes across the engagement — every one plausible, every one wrong.
  2. Anti-stereotype SOP. A dedicated agent pass runs the audit template (A6) on every persona draft before the human panel sees it: evidence-or-remove, demographic-justification, deficit-framing rewrite, inverse test. The agent proposes; the panel disposes. Prompt fragment: "Rewrite any attribute that describes a person as a deficiency of the person rather than as a property of the service failing them."
  3. Cluster-run SOP-as-code (A6, CLS-RUN-002): parameters, metrics, and acceptance gates as versioned YAML; a run that misses a gate is documented as rejected, not tuned until it passes — that is how you avoid p-hacking a segmentation into existence.

The meta-SOP. Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver with hard gates: no clustering before the DPIA and data-quality baseline; no persona authoring before fusion and the Codex cross-check; no publication before G1–G3; no cutover issue closed without the journey owner's review. Gates are GitHub milestones with required reviews — process enforced by tooling, not memory.

Guardrails.

  • Data minimization. Daily usage aggregates only; pseudonymization at ingestion with the key held outside the analytics zone; no income or property-value features; no model scores below cluster level leave the environment. The DPO reviewed the feature list as a PR before the pipeline ran.
  • No individual-level disclosure. All published artifacts are cluster-level. Personas describe a kind of customer, never a customer; the dataset sample (A7) uses synthetic hashes and banded values.
  • Evidence traceability. Every persona attribute traces to a source row via evidence IDs; quotes verbatim-verified in CI; statistics recomputed from the feature store on every PR.
  • Human sign-off points. DPIA boundary, feature list, k selection, persona naming, anti-stereotype panel, publication, retirement of the old segmentation.

Failure modes observed.

  • The "average persona" trap. The first k-means run at k = 5 produced a large mush cluster (41% of accounts) whose persona read like everyone and no one — the statistical mean dressed as a character. Mitigation: distinctiveness tests (each persona must differ from every other on ≥2 behavioral features) killed it; k = 7 separated the mush into C2 and C5, two populations with opposite service needs.
  • Cluster-name drift. Early agent drafts named clusters by demographics ("elderly low-income") even when the separating features were behavioral; the active-behavior naming rule ("keeps the paper trail", not "elderly") was enforced in review — naming is still a human gate.
  • Over-trusting agent-coded contact reasons. The 23% disagreement between reason-codes and free text would have silently skewed the contact-posture features; the pipeline recodes from text and treats agent codes as a weak signal only.
  • Codex context errors. Its independent typology initially invented a "prosumer exporter" cluster from two diary participants; the evidence-density rule (a type needs ≥3 independent sources) resolved it. Adversarial counsel requires verification, never averaging.

Causal logic (Service Profit Chain). The economics run through the denominator of perceived value (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md): cluster-aware sequencing and the assisted digital path cut customer effort (one letter, one call, a 2-hour window), which cut failure demand (−200k contacts/yr), which cut cost-to-serve and freed agents for the contacts that genuinely need humans. Estimated annualized effects (illustrative): contact-cost avoidance ≈ €2.6M; failed-appointment avoidance ≈ €0.9M (8 pts × ≈ 55k installs/yr × €200 truck-roll); retained margin from C3 switching reduction ≈ €1.1M (18,500 fewer switches × €60 contribution). Against €210k engagement cost and ≈ €600k implementation, payback inside six months. The softer return is decision quality: the metering program now plans capacity by cluster, and tariff-fit campaigns convert at 2.4× the old demographic targeting.

KPI tree (abridged). North-star: share of journeys completed without failure demand, by cluster. Drivers: self-booking rate, first-contact resolution, alert self-answer rate. Lagging: contact volume, switching rate, NPS. Guardrail metrics: delegation-consent complaints (0), privacy incidents (0), persona drift (quarterly revalidation).

9. AI tool playbook

Claude Code — orchestration, agent team, persona authoring. Ran the source profiling, the transcript-to-evidence pipeline over 4,712 items, the clustering agent team (k-means sweep, Ward, reconciliation), the synthesis wall, persona drafting under the evidence-only rule, and all mermaid artifacts. Excels at: schema-bound extraction at scale and first drafts that arrive inside the guardrails. Failed at: cluster naming (demographic drift), and it cannot run the fusion workshop — nine humans reconciling two typologies in a room is the job. Human took over: naming, framing, facilitation, sign-off.

OpenCode — data pipeline and feature engineering. Built the ingest/clean/features pipeline in agent mode from the brief in Section 4.2, including the pydantic quarantine and the HMAC pseudonymization vault; generated the seeded mock dataset (A7); ran the alerts-threshold norm computations in use case B. Excels at: self-directed multi-file data engineering and honest run reports — its run_report.md caught the 61,204 duplicates before any human did. Failed at: statistical judgment — it proposed k = 9 on a marginal elbow improvement while bootstrap stability collapsed; the SOP's acceptance gates, not the agent, decide. Human took over: feature ethics, k selection, thresholds.

Codex — independent derivation and adversarial review. The engagement's second brain, run via codex exec --skip-git-repo-check with a scoped concern: the k-selection challenge (one good point, tested and rejected), the blind qualitative-only typology (9 of 11 convergence, two model improvements), the delegation-consent review (one scope fix), the tariff-fit economics attack (one explicit trade accepted by steering). Excels at: disagreeing productively from a cold start — "do NOT read the existing segmentation" is the load-bearing instruction for a genuine cross-check. Failed at: occasional evidence-thin inventions (the "prosumer exporter" cluster); every claim verified against the register before action.

GitHub — the system of record. Issues as assumptions and work items (41 assumption issues, 68 cutover issues), PRs as review gates (persona cards, pipeline, letters), milestones as stage gates, CODEOWNERS routing (DPO on anything touching features or telemetry). Actions CI: mermaid lint, markdown lint, CSV schema validation, quote verification against the transcript store, and a privacy job that fails any PR exporting sub-cluster-level identifiers. Failed at: nothing material; the board tells the truth because the designer grooms it every Friday.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • The mess was the research. The 214 move-out spellings and the 23% reason-code disagreement were not janitorial trivia; they were the first evidence that Kestrel's channels and codes were designed around internal convenience. The data-quality report became an insight artifact in its own right (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md).
  • Verbatim quote verification is the highest-ROI automation in AI-assisted synthesis. Eleven fabricated or misattributed quotes caught by one grep-class CI check. Without it, the personas would have been beautifully cited fiction.
  • The average-persona trap is the default failure of clustering. Any k that is too small manufactures a statistical mean and invites you to give it a name and a stock photo. Distinctiveness tests are the vaccine; enforce them as gates, not aspirations.
  • Independent cross-checks must be genuinely blind. The first Codex cross-check was contaminated — it had read the cluster file in context and merely agreed. Re-running with "do NOT read segmentation/" produced real divergence and two model improvements. Blindness is a prompt property, not a courtesy.
  • Vulnerability is a state, not a segment. The killed "vulnerable customer" persona was the hardest call of the engagement; as a service-standard overlay it protects better than a persona, because it applies to every cluster at the moments vulnerability actually occurs.
  • Adoption is a cutover, not a launch. Retiring the 2019 buckets from the campaign tool — deletion, not deprecation — made the new segmentation real. Two conventions side by side is how segmentations die.
  • Privacy constraints improved the model. The daily-aggregate rule forced behavioral features that generalize (posture, mix, cadence) instead of intrusive features that overfit. Data minimization was a modeling advantage, not a tax.
  • Done differently next time: baseline portfolio NPS per assumed segment in week 1 (the −4 baseline was reconstructed retrospectively and is the weakest number here), and involve the contact-centre knowledge team at feature engineering — their reason-code taxonomy would have saved a pipeline iteration.
