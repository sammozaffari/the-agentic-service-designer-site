01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

A franchisee group inside a global quick-service restaurant chain had spent years optimizing one number: speed. Yet guests kept complaining — and when the group finally read over 100,000 comments as one dataset, the complaints were not about speed. Roughly two-thirds of service complaints involved execution competence — confusion, disorganization, failed coordination — while speed appeared in just over half. The cross-mention analysis showed why: slowness is usually a symptom of incompetence, not the reverse. The chain's own leadership put it in one line: customers forgive slow competence but not fast chaos.

100,000+guest comments analysed
67%of service complaints are execution failures — vs 54% speed
+1.5 ptservice-satisfaction lift in the drive-thru trial
4 → 1customer-experience frameworks consolidated

Over a 16-week design arc, a one-designer-plus-agents team converted that finding into a strategy pivot: competence-first training, four competing customer-experience frameworks consolidated into one hospitality-values framework, and hospitality repositioned from “delight” to risk reduction — one negative experience erases roughly four positive interactions. A drive-thru trial proved it — satisfaction and accuracy up, speed flat — and a national team competition corroborated it: experience-focused stores out-scored the system on the chain's balanced scorecard. Remediation ran on store-level clusters, each labelled by its dominant failure signature.

02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

The client is a franchisee group within a global quick-service restaurant chain — several hundred high-volume restaurants across a multi-market footprint: counter service, kiosks, a mobile app, drive-thru lanes, and third-party delivery. A successful drive-thru hospitality initiative — scripting, station-level training, recognition — was to expand to the whole restaurant. The brief: restore the balance between technology-driven efficiency and meaningful human interaction at every touchpoint, and refresh the national customer-experience values framework so the system could deliver it.

The challenge

Operational data made the same point from two directions: hospitality was simultaneously the biggest reason customers left and the biggest reason they stayed, yet team-service satisfaction sat at barely half of guests system-wide, and over half of all complaints were service-related. Meanwhile the operating culture had institutionalised a different number — “Speed has been the metric for so long” was the leadership's own diagnosis, and the reward systems followed it.

The values stack was fragmented too: four competing customer-experience frameworks coexisted in the field, none focused enough on hospitality — and teams cannot execute a value they cannot name. Three intertwined problems followed: a mis-calibrated operating metric, a competence gap disguised as an attitude problem, and a values language too scattered to change behaviour.

Success criteria (agreed with the client)

Metric Baseline Target
Drive-thru service satisfaction 51% of verified guests satisfied +1 pt
Service satisfaction across all touchpoints 51% +24 pts over the programme horizon
Friendliness mentions (drive-thru only) Baseline share of verified comments +3 pts

Team job satisfaction Baseline survey index +2.6 pts

Constraints

  • Speed is institutionalised. The dominant metric rewards clock time; teams may read a competence-first programme as “slowing us down” — the mitigation is built in: doing it right the first time is faster overall.
  • Field capacity. Multiple system-wide rollouts were already competing for the same restaurant managers and coaches; the programme had to be phased and gated, not launched.
  • Measurement honesty. Success is tracked only on verified guest feedback — responses attributable to a real transaction — even though that deliberately excludes other channels.
  • Data gaps. A large share of comments could not be attributed to a channel; the team had to name the blind spot and build rules to shrink it.
  • Global alignment. A franchisee-level framework must stay compatible with the chain's global hospitality standards still being defined.

Why service design

This was not a training problem, a scheduling problem, or a marketing problem — it was all three at once, produced by a socio-technical system whose real failures live below the line of visibility (See: 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md). The Service Value Equation supplied the economic frame: perceived value is value delivered minus access cost — and the largest access costs here were not seconds in the queue but confusion, failed coordination, and the emotional tax of an order you cannot trust.

03 · Approach

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research — the corpus, the complaints, and the benchmarks

Goal. Establish what service failure actually is — at scale, not anecdotally — across every touchpoint and channel.

Three people touched my order and it still came out wrong.

Guest comment, verified feedback corpus
Reading the corpus

Roughly two-thirds of service complaints trace to execution competence — confusion, disorganization, failed coordination — while speed appears in just over half. Slowness is usually a symptom of incompetence, not the reverse.

Method & SOP. Triangulated secondary analysis: sentiment analytics over a rolling 12-month window of verified guest feedback plus a year of formal complaints; a store-level export of more than 100,000 service-related comments; a churn-feedback export of “I won't return” comments; and external benchmarks to make the internal data credible.

AI execution. The classification stack coded the corpus against a taxonomy co-defined with operations — seven service themes, from missing or wrong items through training gaps — under an encoded SOP: theme, sentiment valence, and confidence per comment; low-confidence comments routed to human review. Output: the evidence register — 100,000+ coded comments, channel splits, per-theme volumes, version-controlled.

Illustrative host command — pattern only; live execution unverified.

TERMINAL
theme-lab classify corpus/service-comments.csv --taxonomy taxonomy/service-v7.yaml
  --channels delivery,drive-thru,in-store,app,kiosk --min-confidence 0.8
  --low-confidence-review queue:human-review --write evidence/register.jsonl

Human designer judgment. The taxonomy itself — which themes exist at all — is a design decision, not a data decision. The designer and operations leads argued three rounds over whether “ignored / no acknowledgment” was its own theme or a demeanour subtype; it stayed separate because its failure signature (invisible queue abandonment) has a different intervention than rudeness.

Artifact produced. Coded evidence register, channel split, churn-driver rates, benchmark annex.

4.2 Synthesis — the decomposition that redirected the strategy

Goal. Convert 100,000+ coded comments into a decision, not a theme list.

Method & SOP. The load-bearing move was a decomposition. The team split the service dimension into two constructs — speed (raw clock time from order to receipt, quantitative) and execution (competency and capability to execute processes correctly, handle complexity, coordinate effectively, qualitative) — classified every service complaint into speed-only, execution-only, or both, and ran a cross-mention test: do speed complaints mention execution failures, or the reverse?

AI execution. The decomposition workbench re-coded the service-complaint subset against the two constructs and produced the overlap table: roughly a third speed-only, just under half execution-only, about a fifth both. The cross-mention test returned the asymmetry that became the program's keystone: execution failures appear in only a small share of speed complaints, while speed appears in about a third of execution complaints. The direction of causation is readable from that single pair of numbers — incompetence causes slowness, not the reverse.

Illustrative host command — pattern only; live execution unverified.

TERMINAL
decomp decompose evidence/register.jsonl --constructs speed,execution
  --overlap speed-only,execution-only,both --cross-mention
  --out exhibits/decomposition.md --json exhibits/decomposition.json

Human designer judgment. Naming the two constructs is framing, and framing is political: the designer insisted on “execution” rather than “efficiency” — efficiency still reads as a speed cousin, and the whole argument depends on treating competence as the independent variable. The same session produced the headline insight: guests forgive slow competence but not fast chaos; perceived competence is the trust currency; hospitality is risk reduction, not delight. An adversarial pass attacked each insight card; one candidate fell on a weak quote, two required stronger evidence.

Artifact produced. Decomposition exhibit, cross-mention pair, six insight cards:

4.3 Mapping — the touchpoint journey and the store-level clusters

Goal. Build the two alignment artifacts everything else hangs on: the touchpoint journey with fail points, and the store-level cluster exhibit that turns a national dataset into local action.

Method & SOP. The journey spine was defined by guest goals, never internal stages: order entry (counter, kiosk, app, drive-thru speaker) → payment → production and pack → handoff (counter, drive-thru window, delivery pickup, parked-car delivery) → post-visit feedback. Fail points were annotated from the theme volumes, each pushed to its root behind the line of visibility. In parallel, the store-level export was segmented into clusters by dominant failure signature rather than ranked as a leaderboard.

AI execution. Cluster segmentation grouped high-volume locations by leading theme: recovery (missing items, refund friction), wait (drive-thru-heavy sites), training gap, and cleanliness. The journey diagram was authored as version-controlled markup, diffed in pull requests like software; the segmentation also produced the monitoring design — weekly theme pulls with cluster-level ranks, flagging rising trends versus the prior week.

Human designer judgment. Choosing where the line of visibility sits. The kiosk channel is deliberately engineered as line-of-visibility management: routing eligible card-paying guests to self-service order entry frees crew to pack and hand off — with the standing rule never to make a guest feel wrong for choosing the counter.

Artifact produced. Touchpoint journey with fail points, store-level cluster exhibit, weekly-monitoring spec.

4.4 Ideation — one framework instead of four

Goal. Convert the insight set into a coherent, executable values framework and a behaviour-change system — not a poster.

Method & SOP. Framework consolidation: four competing customer-experience frameworks collapsed into one memorable hospitality-values framework — hospitality (proactive, safe, yourself), accuracy (know it all, make sure the guest gets it all), taste (looks good, tastes good) — each pillar with behavioural standards decomposed to station level (front counter, drive-thru order taker, pay window, tap-and-go, pickup, kiosk, delivery, parked cars) and a short script per high-impact scenario: a verbal confirmation at the speaker, a missed-item recovery line, a frustrated-guest line.

AI execution. The drafting stack produced first-pass pillar standards and scripts from the insight cards; the designer rewrote tone and sequencing. An adversarial pass reviewed the framework against the decomposition — specifically the decision to omit speed: operations argued for putting it back, the data kept it out — with execution in ~2/3 of service complaints, a framework that names speed names the wrong problem.

Human designer judgment. The acronym decision, the scripting voice, and the rejection of “magical moments” framing in favour of prevention — all judgment calls the agents could not make.

Artifact produced. The hospitality-values framework; per-station standards and scripts; the data argument that justifies omitting speed.

4.5 Prototyping — the drive-thru trial

Goal. Test the competence-first thesis at pilot scale before committing the system.

Method & SOP. A drive-thru hospitality trial — the expansion's origin — measured seven metrics on verified guest feedback before and after. The trial readout is the permission structure for everything that followed:

Metric (verified guest feedback) Delta
Service satisfaction +1.5 pts
Service quality +1.9 pts
Order accuracy +3.5 pts
Order speed +0.2 pts
Positive sentiment +3.3 pts
Negative comments −2.6 pts
Friendliness mentions +4.1 pts

AI execution. The metric pipeline computed every delta from the same query set — no hand-typed numbers. Crew also reported higher job satisfaction and confidence in post-trial check-ins, folded into the framework's team-side messaging.

Human designer judgment. The trial result is the case study in one table: satisfaction and accuracy rose while speed barely moved. That is the competence-first thesis confirmed live — guests did not experience the programme as slower; what they noticed was accuracy and care. A second natural experiment corroborated it: stores focused on customer experience out-scored the system average on the chain's balanced scorecard.

Artifact produced. Seven-metric trial readout; qualitative team feedback; the expansion proposal grounded in both.

4.6 Implementation — phased rollout and the remediation playbook

Goal. Take the validated framework to the system without breaking the field.

+3.5 ptsorder accuracy at the trial drive-thrus — the largest operational gain
+1.5 ptsservice satisfaction on verified guest feedback
+0.2 ptsorder speed — effectively flat, as the thesis predicted
−2.6 ptsnegative comments across the trial stores

Method & SOP. A staged rollout: the hospitality programme launched first across the group's restaurants (training kit, station stickers with scenario videos, recognition mechanics, the parked-car waiting board); the consolidated values framework followed at the next leadership forum, then system-wide sustainability and scorecard alignment. The rollout was reprioritised once when competing system rollouts absorbed the same field capacity; the plan was rephased, not rushed.

AI execution. Monitoring automation ran alongside: digital observation forms (five-minute capability checks) feeding a real-time dashboard with automated weekly reports to area leaders. The store-level remediation playbook was executed as designed:

Sequencing the gates, negotiating the rephase, and the recognition design: layered tiers (bingo hooks, hero pins, socks awarded by area leaders, competitions, national team scoring, quarterly awards), with surprise physical awards deliberately chosen to avoid gaming the numbers.

4.7 Measurement — the honest metric

Goal. Close the loop with a metric that cannot be gamed.

AI execution. The measurement model tracks verified guest feedback only — responses attributable to a real transaction — deliberately kept off the chain's balanced scorecard, with that alignment gap named as an open issue. Capability checks run on digital observation forms with a real-time dashboard; high-scoring crew earn recognition, and managers sustaining high averages receive small bonuses. The KPI tree (Section 8) ties every leading indicator to the north-star metric.

Human designer judgment. The team inherited a manual observation instrument that managers distrusted — “results won't be accurate” — and redesigned it into the digital form with automated reporting rather than defending the old tool. That willingness to kill a beloved instrument on field feedback is the governance loop working.

06 · Use cases

Use cases

Use case A — Drive-thru hospitality at scale

Scenario. The programme's origin: a drive-thru hospitality initiative that proved the thesis in one lane and now has to hold at hundreds of stores.

What was designed. Per-station standards for the order taker, pay window, and handoff; verbal order confirmation (“that's two boxes and two drinks — correct?”); pack-check at the window; the parked-car waiting board that makes an invisible wait state explicit. The trial metrics — satisfaction and accuracy up, speed flat — became the scale argument.

AI working session. The classification stack scored trial-period comments against the pre-trial baseline for the seven deltas; the decomposition workbench confirmed the competence signature in the trial stores, and the adversarial reviewer checked the expansion deck's claims against the evidence register before the approval gate.

Outcome. Service satisfaction +1.5 pts, order accuracy +3.5 pts, negative comments −2.6 pts; speed unchanged.

Use case B — Delivery handoff accuracy

Scenario. Third-party delivery is a large and fast-growing channel, and its complaints cluster on handoff: the driver's pack, missing items, orders not ready when the driver arrives.

What was designed. The pack-check moved to the delivery pickup point with a visual checklist; the handoff standard includes verifying the bag before the driver leaves; the remake script covers the driver-returns case without penalising the guest. Delivery pack accuracy became a named monitoring theme in the weekly cluster report.

AI working session. Delivery-channel comments were separated from the unattributed pool where possible; the coding rule for driver-versus-store blame was tightened after two misclassifications in review.

Outcome. Delivery handoff complaints fell as a share of the channel's comments across the pilot group; remake volume declined with the pack-check.

Use case C — Kiosk routing as line-of-visibility management

Scenario. Self-service kiosks can absorb order entry — or create a new failure mode when guests cannot find their items.

What was designed. Kiosk suggestion criteria: self-service only for card payers without special discounts; never for cash, seniors, or staff discounts. Bulk and combo items remain counter-only — a designed trade accepting kiosk menu constraints in exchange for pack time and fewer complaints. The standing rule: never make a guest feel wrong for choosing the counter.

AI working session. The channel segmentation quantified the kiosk friction themes; the routing policy was drafted against the complaint mix and reviewed against the accessibility edge cases before release.

Outcome. Crew time redirected to packing and drive-thru flexibility; kiosk-related complaint volume stable while counter accuracy improved.

Use case D — Complaint recovery and store-level remediation

Scenario. The asymmetry principle — one negative experience erases roughly four positive ones — makes recovery a first-class design surface.

What was designed. A fast refund and remake script; acknowledgment standards (“we heard you, here is what happens next”); and the cluster remediation playbook: recovery programme for the high-volume cluster, peak labour review for the wait cluster, micro-training for the training-gap cluster, cleanliness blitz for the hygiene cluster — each with weekly monitoring against the prior week.

AI working session. The segmentation scripts labelled clusters and tracked theme ranks week over week; automated reports flagged rising trends to area leaders before they became complaints.

Outcome. The worst-performing clusters' negative-comment share declined faster than the system average across the monitoring window.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Four SOPs were encoded for agent execution, each following the same pattern: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Classification and theme-coding SOP (research). Input: raw comments plus the co-defined taxonomy. Output: JSONL coded comments — theme, sentiment valence, confidence, channel when attributable. Acceptance: every comment coded or routed to human review; no force-fitting below the confidence floor; every aggregate traceable to the coded store. Gate: human spot-check of a sample per batch, plus weekly misclassification review.
  2. Decomposition and cross-mention SOP (synthesis). Steps: isolate the service-complaint subset → recode against the two constructs (speed, execution) → compute the three-way overlap → compute cross-mention rates. Acceptance: construct definitions frozen before coding; no comment counted twice; the direction-of-causation claim carried only by the cross-mention pair. Gate: adversarial review pass plus the human framing session.
  3. Cluster segmentation and monitoring SOP (mapping / implementation). Steps: aggregate store-level theme volumes → label clusters by dominant failure signature → assign primary and secondary interventions → produce weekly theme ranks with prior-week deltas. Acceptance: clusters named by failure signature, never by store; every intervention mapped to a cluster; rising trends flagged, never buried. Gate: area-leader review of the weekly report.
  4. Measurement and readout SOP (measurement). Steps: pull verified feedback only → compute deltas from the same query set → draft the readout as a reviewable pull request. Acceptance: every number regenerated from the query set; no hand-typed metrics; scorecard-alignment gaps named, not hidden. Gate: programme-lead sign-off before any external readout.

The meta-SOP. The engagement ran research → synthesise → map → ideate → prototype → implement → measure, with a hard gate between stages: no ideation before the insight cards passed the decomposition; no framework decision before the trial evidence; no rollout phase before its approval gate. Each gate carried explicit options — proceed, escalate, pause, cancel, revisit — with status on timeline, cost, resource, quality, and risk, enforced by tooling, not memory.

Guardrails.

  • Hallucination control. Every figure in every readout must trace to the coded evidence register; a quoted guest comment not found in the store fails the review. Aggregates recomputed from source data, never "remembered."
  • Verification discipline. Success metrics restricted to verified guest feedback; unverified channels never used for headline claims.
  • Attribution honesty. Comments without channel attribution are counted and reported as a named data gap, never silently dropped.
  • Human sign-off points. Taxonomy finalisation; construct definitions; insight framing; the acronym decision; measurement-model placement; every rollout gate.

Failure modes observed. (1) Over-clustering: the first segmentation pass produced a theme per store; the rule — clusters named by failure signature, not location — fixed it. (2) A confident but wrong synthesis read: the first cross-mention draft implied speed causes execution failures because both co-occur; the construct-definition rule caught the direction error. (3) Taxonomy drift: the classification agent began merging “ignored” into “rude” mid-batch; the schema validator rejected it.

The chain ran as Heskett, Sasser, and Schlesinger describe: internal service quality — tools, scripts, recognition, aligned incentives — raised frontline capability; external value rose chiefly through the trust side of the value equation, because perceived competence collapsed the emotional access cost of ordering; satisfaction followed; loyalty, churn reduction, and reduced failure demand produced the economics. Business-case anchors: guests with an excellent experience return nearly twice as often, and a sustained lift in excellent experiences corresponds to roughly US$900 of additional annual revenue per store (third-party research framing).

Asymmetry economics. Because one negative experience erases roughly four positive interactions, the marginal value of a prevented failure exceeds the marginal value of a created delight moment. That single inversion — hospitality as risk reduction, not magic — converted the programme from “soft” to “load-bearing” in the business case (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/08-service-design-and-business.md).

KPI tree (abridged). North-star: verified service satisfaction across all touchpoints. Leading indicators: order accuracy (pack-check completion), perceived competence (execution-theme share), acknowledgment (script adherence), wait experience (wait-theme trend). Lagging indicators: negative comment rate, churn-relevant themes, team confidence and tenure, remakes and refund volume. Reviewed weekly in rollout, monthly in run-state; scorecard alignment tracked as an open item.

09 · AI tools

AI tool playbook

Sentiment analytics and classification stack — the corpus engine. Coded 100,000+ comments against the co-defined taxonomy; computed sentiment valences and channel splits; produced every aggregate in the evidence register from one query set. Excels at consistent large-scale coding and repeatable aggregates. Failed at taxonomy judgment — it happily merged themes the humans had fought to separate; the schema validator and weekly misclassification review were the leash. Human took over: taxonomy, edge-case rules, cluster naming.

Decomposition workbench — the analytical keystone. Recoded the service-complaint subset against the frozen constructs and produced the overlap table and cross-mention pair. Excels at precise set operations over large coded corpora. Failed at causal framing — the first exhibit draft implied the wrong direction; the construct-definition rule caught it. Human took over: construct naming and the headline insight.

Adversarial reviewer — the second opinion. Attacked the insight cards, the decomposition exhibit, and the expansion deck against the evidence register — killed one candidate insight, forced evidence upgrades on two others, caught an over-claim in the expansion proposal. Excels at disagreeing productively — “be adversarial” is a load-bearing instruction. Failed at flagging stated client constraints as defects (real-time reporting the field tools cannot do); every finding was verified before action — reconcile by verifying, never by averaging.

10 · Lessons

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • The decomposition reframed the engagement — everything else followed from it. Splitting “time” from “execution” and proving the causal direction turned a vague “be nicer, be faster” brief into a competence-first programme with a business case. The client's own working theory — the problem is speed — was contradicted by its own data.
  • Incompetence causes slowness, not the reverse. The cross-mention asymmetry (execution failures in ~7% of speed complaints; speed in ~34% of execution complaints) is the single most persuasive exhibit in the whole program. Throughput fixes treat a symptom; competence fixes treat the cause.
  • Bad service hurts more than good service helps. The asymmetry principle — one negative experience erases roughly four positive interactions — converted hospitality from a soft ideal into risk reduction. Prevention out-earned delight.
  • One memorable framework beats four correct ones. The consolidation was a clarity win, not a branding exercise — it shipped with behavioural standards, scripts, and station-level granularity. The operations pushback that tried to put speed back into the framework was settled by the data, not by argument.
  • Verify before you celebrate. Verified-feedback-only measurement kept the headline metrics honest, and the scorecard-alignment gap was named rather than hidden. The manual observation instrument managers distrusted was killed and redesigned — a governance loop that listens.
  • Data gaps must be named. The largest slice of the channel split was unattributed phrasing; reporting it as a named gap — and building rules to shrink it — protected the analysis from overclaiming.
  • Capacity reality forces staged rollout. Competing system rollouts meant the programme was rephased, not rushed. The discipline of proceed/escalate/pause/cancel gates kept the momentum honest.
  • Done differently next time: baseline the good-services scorecard in week one (the framework audit happened in synthesis, costing comparative precision), and involve operations leadership at synthesis rather than prototyping — the acronym debate would have been shorter with the decomposition in front of them earlier.
