The Service Profit Chain in Logistics: Halcyon's Account Experience

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Halcyon Logistics is a fictional, composite European third-party logistics provider. Every name, account, figure, and dataset in this article is illustrative and deliberately anonymized; nothing here describes a real client or a real company's performance. The article exists to share knowledge and process — how an AI-first service design engagement was run end to end — not to disclose client specifics. The engagement was executed with an AI-first stance: Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were the primary working tools, with the human designer directing framing, client relationships, and every consequential decision.

1. Executive summary

Halcyon Logistics, a mid-market European 3PL with 12 warehouses and roughly 4,200 B2B accounts, was losing shippers it believed it was satisfying: annual account churn sat at 11.8% while management dashboards showed "acceptable" SLA attainment of 87.4%. We applied the Service Profit Chain (Heskett, Sasser & Schlesinger) as the engagement's explicit design theory-of-change, instrumented all five links with unit-level data, and redesigned the B2B account experience around four interventions: a weekly service review ritual, exception-based proactive alerts, an account manager cockpit, and returns visibility. A 12-week pilot with 60 accounts lifted pilot-cohort SLA attainment from 87.4% to 93.1%, shipper NPS from +6 to +21, and gross margin per account by 11.3%, while dispatcher eNPS moved from -12 to +18 — evidence for the chain, not just for the redesign.

2. The brief

Client portrait. Halcyon Logistics is a fictional Rotterdam-headquartered 3PL serving mid-size European shippers: retail, FMCG, industrial parts, cold-chain pharma, and e-commerce SMBs. It operates 12 warehouses (NL 4, BE 2, DE 4, PL 2), a partner carrier network of ~140 hauliers, and in-house customs brokerage for EU–UK flows. Revenue is €285M at a blended gross margin of 14.2%. Digital maturity is mid-table: a solid TMS and WMS, telematics on 62% of linehaul movements, but account communication still runs on email, phone, and monthly PDF statements.

The challenge. Commercial leadership believed the churn problem was pricing. Operations believed it was carrier reliability. Both were partially wrong. Failure demand told the real story: 31,400 inbound contacts per year to account managers and dispatch, of which 41% were "where is my shipment" (WISMO) status chases and 22% were invoice or proof-of-delivery disputes. Shippers experienced Halcyon as a black box between booking and invoice: when something went wrong — a dock delay, a temperature excursion, a missed customs window — they found out by chasing, not by being told. Account managers (38 FTE) spent an estimated 55% of their week re-keying status across five systems and firefighting; annualized voluntary turnover among AMs was 31%, and among dispatchers 34%. In Service Profit Chain terms, internal service quality was broken, and the damage propagated predictably outward (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/02-internal-service-quality.md).

Success criteria (agreed with the client):

  1. Pilot cohort (60 accounts): SLA attainment ≥ 92%, shipper NPS ≥ +18, churn ≤ 7% annualized.
  2. Failure demand: WISMO contacts down ≥ 30% within the pilot.
  3. Internal links: dispatcher eNPS from -12 to ≥ +10; AM voluntary turnover trending below 20% annualized.
  4. A working measurement system linking all five chain links at account-manager-portfolio level, reusable after the engagement.

Constraints. Budget €310k for the engagement; no TMS replacement in-year (the cockpit had to sit on top of existing systems); GDPR and customer data-sharing consent for any shipper-visible telemetry; contractual SLA language could not be altered without legal review; 20-week runway to a board readout.

Why service design. The problem was not a touchpoint problem; it was a system problem spanning warehouse ops, dispatch, account management, invoicing, and the shipper's own receiving docks. Service design's end-to-end mapping and blueprinting discipline (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/07-designing-and-managing-the-service-process.md) is built for exactly this: aligning frontstage promise with backstage capability. The Service Profit Chain supplied the causal theory and the measurement architecture; Grönroos's perceived-quality model supplied the encounter-level diagnosis (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md).

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

We ran the standard double-loop process — Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver — mapped to the TISDD iteration loop (research, ideation, prototyping, implementation) and Kumar's seven modes (sense intent, know context, know people, frame insights, explore concepts, frame solutions, realize offerings). The Service Profit Chain was overlaid as the causal spine: every research artifact and every design decision was tagged to the link it served.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

Phase 1 — Discover (weeks 1–5)

Goal. Establish the chain baseline: what each link actually measures today, and where the chain is broken.

Method & SOP. Three instruments in parallel. First, 30 semi-structured interviews with Halcyon account managers (30 of 38 AMs, 45–60 minutes each), run against a standard interview guide covering tooling friction, encounter quality, and recovery authority. Second, a relational survey of 1,200 shipper contacts (28.6% response rate from 4,200 accounts) capturing NPS, perceived reliability, proactiveness, and share-of-wallet intent. Third, an 18-month mine of operational data: 1.84M shipment records, 96,000 logged exceptions, SLA attainment per account, and contact-reason tagging from the telephony and CRM systems. The SOP for the chain audit followed the Service Profit Chain Audit discipline: score each link's instrumentation, identify lead/lag gaps, and triangulate HR, ops, CX, and finance data at portfolio level (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/05-measuring-the-chain.md).

AI execution. Claude Code ran the interview pipeline as a fan-out of synthesis subagents: one agent per batch of five transcripts extracted coded quotes against a codebook derived from Grönroos's six quality criteria (professionalism, attitudes, accessibility, reliability, recovery, reputation), then a reducer agent merged codes and produced an evidence-linked findings file. Representative prompt:

Task: synthesize AM interview transcripts 011-015.
For each transcript extract: (1) tooling friction events with verbatim quote
and timestamp, (2) moments where the AM could not resolve a shipper issue
without escalation, (3) emotional load indicators, (4) improvement ideas
the AM volunteered. Output: JSON per transcript with fields
{quote, code, chain_link, severity_1_5}. No quote, no claim: every finding
must cite a verbatim. Unknown = "not evidenced".

OpenCode ran in agent mode to build the data pipeline: it ingested TMS/WMS exports, joined telephony tags to accounts, and produced the contact-reason distribution and per-account SLA series; it also generated the anonymized mock dataset used throughout this article. Codex was used for two things: a deep-research pass benchmarking EU 3PL SLA and visibility norms (returned as a citable memo, marked [external benchmark, directional]), and an adversarial review of our chain-baseline math (codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "review baseline.md: check that churn, contact volumes, and SLA aggregates are internally consistent; flag any claim not derivable from the data dictionary"). GitHub hosted the research repo: each interview batch was an issue, synthesis outputs came in as pull requests, and a CI action validated that every finding carried a verbatim quote reference before merge.

Human designer judgment. The designer chose the codebook, conducted the five most politically sensitive interviews personally (including two AMs who had resigned), negotiated data-access consent with the works council, and rejected an early agent synthesis that over-weighted a single eloquent AM's narrative. Agents counted; the designer decided what the counts meant.

Artifact produced. The chain baseline: internal service quality broken (AM tool CSAT 2.6/5; five systems per status lookup; backstage IT ticket median resolution 51 hours), employee satisfaction low (engagement index 61%; dispatcher eNPS -12), external value degraded (87.4% OTIF SLA; only 12% of exceptions communicated proactively), customer outcomes mediocre (shipper NPS +6; top-box "completely satisfied" only 19%), and loyalty/profit leaking (11.8% churn; gross margin per account €9,640). Phase 2 — Define (weeks 6–9)

Goal. Convert research into a causal model of the account experience and a prioritized opportunity set.

Method & SOP. Journey mapping from transcripts and telemetry (the shipper journey: quote, book, ship, track, invoice, reconcile, returns), then a Service Profit Chain mapping workshop that deconstructed each link into Halcyon-specific behaviors and named a lead and lag indicator per link (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/01-the-service-profit-chain.md). Pain points were classified as technical-quality failures (the shipment was late — the "what") versus functional-quality failures (nobody told the shipper — the "how"), per Grönroos (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md). This classification mattered: 68% of high-severity pain points were functional, not technical — shippers could tolerate a delay they were warned about far better than an on-time mystery.

AI execution. Claude Code's mapping agents built the journey map draft directly from the coded corpus: each phase node carried its pain points with quote counts, and the emotion line was derived from sentiment-coded survey verbatims cross-checked against contact-volume spikes. Codex adversarially reviewed the draft journey for teleological bias ("you are mapping the shipper you wish existed — show the evidence for the reconcile phase pain ranking"). The opportunity matrix (impact × effort) was generated by an agent, then re-ranked in a two-hour client workshop. GitHub issues tracked each opportunity as a work item with a linked evidence pack; the PR for the causal model required COO sign-off as a review gate.

Human designer judgment. The designer framed the theory-of-change statement the whole programme would be judged against — "if we fix the internal tooling and give AMs proactive exception signals, external value and loyalty follow; pricing is not the lever" — and defended it to a skeptical CFO with the unit-level linkage data. Codex's review caught a genuine error: our first draft claimed a correlation between dispatcher tenure and SLA at account level that the data only supported at warehouse level; the claim was downgraded before it ever reached the client.

Phase 3 — Develop (weeks 10–14)

Goal. Design the future-state account experience and prove its mechanics before the pilot.

Method & SOP. Full-spectrum service blueprinting of the redesigned exception-handling service (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/07-designing-and-managing-the-service-process.md), plus encounter design for the four highest-stakes moments of truth (delay warning, dock congestion, temperature excursion, invoice dispute) with explicit technical and functional standards per encounter (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/03-managing-the-service-encounter.md). Four interventions were designed together, because each served a different chain link:

  1. Weekly service review ritual — a 25-minute standing call per account with a fixed agenda (see A6), converting the AM relationship from reactive firefighting to managed episodes in a relationship (encounters vs. relationships, 19/03).
  2. Exception-based proactive alerts — a rules engine watching TMS/WMS/telematics events; when an exception breaches a threshold, the shipper is told before they ask, with impact and options.
  3. Account manager cockpit — one screen replacing the five-system swivel: live SLA per account, open exceptions, alert history, health score.
  4. Returns visibility — returns tracked as first-class shipment objects with the same alert rules, ending the "blind until warehouse receipt" gap.

AI execution. OpenCode built the working artifacts in agent mode: the account-health analytics job (daily score per account from SLA trend, exception rate, contact sentiment, and payment behavior) and a mock alert rules engine with seeded demo data so the client could experience the cockpit against realistic volume before any production integration. Claude Code authored the blueprint and the SOP-as-code documents, and maintained a traceability file linking every blueprint change to the journey pain point it answered. Codex ran a second adversarial pass on the alert thresholds, arguing (correctly) that our first dock-delay rule would over-alert retail shippers during promotional peaks; thresholds became segment-parameterized. GitHub Actions ran CI on the case repo: mermaid lint on every diagram, markdown lint on every artifact, and a schema check on the SOP-as-code YAML.

Human designer judgment. Legal review of customer-facing alert language was human-led: no alert may promise compensation or admit liability; SLA wording remained contractual and unchanged (see guardrails, §7). The designer also made the explicit scope call to exclude the invoicing engine rebuild — the blueprint fixes disputes through visibility and earlier communication, not through re-platforming finance.

Artifact produced. The future-state blueprint for the proactive-alerts service (A2, §5), the cockpit mock, the alert decision rules as SOP-as-code, and the pilot plan: 60 accounts in three cohorts of 20 (retail, cold-chain pharma, industrial SMB), each with a matched control set from the same segment, 12 weeks, with the full chain measured at portfolio level (See: 04-service-design-for-business/07-measuring-and-evaluating.md).

Phase 4 — Deliver (weeks 15–20)

Goal. Run the pilot, measure all five links, and produce the rollout business case.

Method & SOP. Before/after pilot evaluation with control grouping: pilot cohorts received the four interventions; matched controls continued the legacy service under the same market conditions. Measurement followed the three-tier stack — experience metrics (Tier 1), operational metrics (Tier 2), financial metrics (Tier 3) — wrapped in the Service Profit Chain's five-link structure so every Tier metric mapped to a chain link (See: 04-service-design-for-business/07-measuring-and-evaluating.md, 20-the-service-profit-chain/05-measuring-the-chain.md).

AI execution. GitHub ran the pilot tracker: one issue per account cohort per week (60 accounts × 12 weeks as a project board with automated rollups), exceptions filed as issues tagged by rule ID, and a weekly scorecard PR generated by a Claude Code agent from the cockpit exports and reviewed by the designer before merging to the readout branch. OpenCode maintained the analytics job and regenerated the account-health scorecard daily. Codex performed the final review of the chain math in the board readout — checking, for instance, that the margin-per-account lift was not an artifact of two large pharma accounts joining the pilot (it was not; the lift held at +9.8% with them excluded).

Human designer judgment. The designer ran the first four weekly service reviews alongside each AM to model the ritual, then stepped back. Mid-pilot, one cohort's NPS dipped; the designer traced it to alert-fatigue from an over-tight threshold, tuned the rule, and documented the change in the tracker — a steering use of measurement, not a reporting one.

6. Use cases

UC-A — High-volume retail shipper with recurring dock delays

Scenario. De Vries Home (fictional; ~30 shipments/week into 6 retail DCs, ~1,560 per year) suffered recurring 3–5 hour dock delays at Halcyon's Eindhoven site on promotional Mondays. Detractor NPS (-18) and a live tender.

What was designed. The account entered the retail pilot cohort. The rules engine carried a segment-parameterized dock-congestion rule (predicted queue > 90 min → severity-2 alert with re-slotted options). The weekly review made Monday peaks a standing forward-look item; the root-cause SOP traced the pattern to a fixed carrier arrival stack between 08:00 and 10:00, and the fix staggered slots by carrier.

AI working session. OpenCode replayed 18 months of Eindhoven gate events to tune the congestion threshold (Codex had warned the naive 60-minute rule would over-alert; the replay confirmed a 90-minute prediction window cut false positives from 34% to 7%). Claude Code drafted the shipper-facing alert text variants; the designer chose the two that went to legal. Sample agent command: opencode run "replay WH-07 gate events 2024-07..2026-01 against rule DCK-02 at thresholds 60/90/120 min; report precision and recall per threshold".

Outcome (illustrative, 12 weeks). Monday dock delay incidents with shipper pre-warning: 94%. De Vries NPS moved from -18 to +9; the tender was withdrawn; share of wallet rose from 41% to 52%.

UC-B — Cold-chain pharma account with exception handling

Scenario. Nordpharm (fictional; 720 annual shipments, GDP-compliant cold chain) experienced a reefer unit fault mid-linehaul on a Friday evening in week 6 of the pilot.

What was designed. A severity-1 temperature-excursion rule: telemetry breach of the 2–8°C band for > 15 minutes triggers an immediate case file, dispatcher re-plan, and a mandatory AM call within 1 hour — plus automatic documentation pack for the consignee's QA team. Contractual wording was untouched; the alert states observed conditions and options, never liability.

AI working session. Claude Code generated the exception runbook from the SOP-as-code YAML and dry-ran it against four historical excursions from the data mine, producing a table of what would have been communicated, when, and to whom. Codex reviewed the runbook for GDP-adjacent compliance language and flagged one sentence that implied guaranteed product integrity; it was rewritten. GitHub tracked the live exception as issue pilot/nordpharm#w06-exc-01 with the full timeline.

Outcome (illustrative). Detection to shipper notification: 22 minutes (vs. a baseline of "the consignee usually tells us"). Product diverted to a cross-dock cold room; zero write-off. Nordpharm's QA lead cited the documentation pack in the renewal meeting; account renewed for 2 years at +6% volume.

UC-C — Small shipper onboarding to the cockpit

Scenario. Atelier Brons (fictional; 40 shipments/month, e-commerce SMB) had never had an AM beyond a shared pool; contacts were 100% reactive.

What was designed. A light-touch path: cockpit access with a 30-minute onboarding call, alert subscription tuned to delivery-window breaches only, and a monthly (not weekly) review slot. The goal was to test whether the model scales down-market without drowning AMs.

AI working session. OpenCode generated a seeded sandbox cockpit pre-loaded with Atelier Brons's own last 90 days of shipments (mock-anonymized), so the onboarding call used the shipper's real-looking data. Claude Code produced the onboarding script and a one-page quick-start; the designer delivered the first call and refined the script after three sessions.

Outcome (illustrative). Atelier Brons's inbound contacts fell from 3.1 to 0.9 per month; pulse score 5/5. AM time cost: 55 minutes/month — viable for the long tail. Onboarding script became the standard for all SMB accounts.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Four SOPs were encoded for agent execution using one pattern: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Chain-measurement SOP (spc_measure.yaml): weekly, an OpenCode job pulls the five-link metrics per AM portfolio; a Claude Code agent renders the scorecard markdown; acceptance criteria require every number traceable to a named source table; the designer's PR review is the gate. This is the measuring-the-chain method (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/05-measuring-the-chain.md) as a scheduled pipeline.
  2. Proactive-alert decision rules as SOP-as-code (excerpt below): rules are data, versioned in Git, dry-run against history before activation, and legally fenced.
  3. Interview synthesis SOP: batch of five transcripts → coded JSON → verbatim-required acceptance → merge gate (prompt in §4 Phase 1).
  4. Root-cause SOP (T3 in A6): the same YAML the ops team uses is what the agents execute and audit against — one source of truth for humans and agents.
alert_rules:
  - id: DCK-02
    name: dock congestion forecast
    scope: warehouses [WH-07, WH-09]
    condition: predicted_queue_min > threshold[segment]
    thresholds: {retail: 90, pharma: 45, industrial: 120, ecom_smb: 120}
    severity: 2
    action: alert_shipper_with_options [reslot, hold, crossdock]
    comms_template: COMM-04
  - id: TMP-01
    name: temperature excursion
    condition: out_of_band_minutes > 15 AND band == "2-8C"
    severity: 1
    action: [open_case, dispatch_replan, am_call_sla_1h, qa_docpack]
    comms_template: COMM-07
guardrails:
  - never state liability, compensation, or guaranteed outcomes
  - SLA wording must match the contract annex verbatim
  - shipper telemetry sharing requires signed consent flag == true

Prompt template — account-interview synthesis (used for all 30 AM interviews and 40 pilot shipper check-ins):

You are a service design research synthesizer. Input: one transcript.
Codebook: [six Grönroos criteria + chain links 1-5].
Output JSON: findings[], each {quote (verbatim, timestamped), code,
chain_link, severity_1_5, sentiment}. Rules: no quote no claim;
do not infer demographics; flag contradictions with the survey data
as type: "conflict". End with a 5-line summary a COO can act on.

Meta-SOP and gates. Research → synthesize → map → ideate → prototype → measure → deliver, with a human gate at every transition: the designer signs the synthesis before mapping, the client signs the causal model before design, legal signs comms templates before pilot, and the steering group signs the readout before rollout.

Guardrails. Hallucination control: verbatim-required findings, source-table-traceable metrics, and Codex adversarial review of any causal claim. Data privacy: GDPR consent flags enforced in the rules engine; all shipper-visible artifacts anonymized; mock data generated by OpenCode for demos. Client review gates as above. Contractual language: alerts describe observed conditions and options only.

Failure modes observed. (1) Agent synthesis initially over-weighted articulate interviewees — mitigated by quote-count normalization. (2) The first alert rules over-alerted during promotional peaks — mitigated by historical replay tuning and segment thresholds. (3) A linkage claim survived only at warehouse level, not account level — caught by Codex review; downgraded. (4) An agent-drafted alert implied product-integrity guarantees — caught by the legal fence; template hardened.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • The chain only persuades when measured at portfolio level. Enterprise averages hid everything; AM-portfolio linkage tables changed the CFO's mind. Aggregate reporting is where causal claims go to die.
  • Proactiveness beats perfection. Shippers forgave technical failures they were warned about; 68% of severe pain was functional. We nearly scoped an SLA-improvement programme that would have missed the real lever.
  • Alert fatigue is a design failure, not a tuning detail. The mid-pilot NPS dip traced to one over-eager threshold. Every alert rule now carries a false-positive budget from day one.
  • Agents need verbatim discipline enforced in CI, not in prompts alone. "No quote, no claim" worked only once the merge gate rejected unsourced findings automatically.
  • Codex's skepticism about our own math was the highest-value AI contribution of the engagement. Budget adversarial review as a first-class phase, not an afterthought.
  • Legal guardrails must live in the rules engine, not in a style guide. The one near-miss on liability language came from a hand-edited draft, not from a template — the fix was to make hand-edits impossible.
  • The weekly ritual scaled better than expected because it was timeboxed and agent-prepared. AMs resisted until the pre-read was generated for them; then attendance hit 88%.
