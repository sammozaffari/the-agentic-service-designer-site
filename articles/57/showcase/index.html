Concept · Ten modules · Designed September 2026

The ideal state: safety reporting for a restaurant network

The research said what the reporting service should do. This is what it would look like if someone built it. Ten modules, one design system, and a numbered note on every screen saying which finding drove the decision and which alternative it rejected.

What this is
A concept, designed in September 2026 from the December 2025 research. Not a shipped product and not the platform the restaurants use.
Evidence behind it
13 interviews, 55 survey responses, 8 restaurants, 2 form walkthroughs, and 232 typed insight records tied to a five-stage blueprint.
How it was built
Hand-written HTML and CSS on a token-based design system, screenshot at twice scale. Every component was checked against shipped products on Mobbin before it was drawn.
Anonymisation
Organisation, vendor, sites and people removed. Every name, number and record on these screens is invented for the concept.
Start here

What you are looking at, and why it exists

The problem. A safety incident and hazard reporting platform, configured for one workflow across several hundred restaurants: a team member notices something, scans the poster, fills in the form. The research found a different workflow in almost every restaurant. The team member tells the manager. The manager decides whether it is worth logging, logs it if so, and sometimes closes it without telling anyone. Reporting volume varied widely between sites and the platform's own data could not say whether a quiet restaurant was safe or silent.

What the research changed. The business assumed location explained the difference, so the sample was designed to test that: eight restaurants across high- and low-reporting sites, higher- and lower-crime suburbs, metropolitan and regional. Location did not predict it. The manager did. That finding reorganised the recommendations, and it organises these screens.

Why a concept and not a redesign. The platform was a configured vendor product that could not be rebuilt, so the delivered recommendations were about what the form asks and what managers do. This concept answers the next question an employer asks, which is what the service would look like if the software followed the evidence. Each module below is one item from that product's navigation, redrawn from the findings.

How to read a module. The finding sits above the screen. The screen is shown whole, at its native size, with numbered markers. Each marker names the finding, the decision it drove, the alternative rejected and the shipped product the pattern was checked against. The states that matter sit underneath. [The case study](../index.html) carries the research, the method and the estimate of what the internal tool saved.

Module 01

Quick Add: reporting from the floor

What the research found

The form asked questions the reporter could not answer. Incident type came first, with options like first aid or lost time that nobody can predict while their hand is under the cold tap; the notifiable-to-the-regulator question sent people to a compliance matrix. Even area safety leads admitted asking the restaurant's safety champion which option to choose. Misclassified reports were sent back from above, and everyone learned that reporting was a way to get something wrong.

The redesign inverts the order. The reporter says what happened and how it happened, in the words they would use to a manager. Severity, classification and the regulator question move to the manager's review, after the welfare check, when the outcome is actually known. The safety team supported the inversion because it also gives them cleaner data.

Everything on the reporter's side is built for one thumb, mid-shift, on a personal phone, in a restaurant whose phone policy may not allow it. Nothing is asked that the system already knows.

1

The restaurant is already known

FindingThe research watched reports being made during a shift. Time pressure was real but smaller than it felt: the cost people described was stepping off the floor during a rush.

DecisionThe restaurant and its number are carried by the session, so the first screen asks nothing the system can already answer.

RejectedA site picker on the first screen, which asks a casual team member for a number they have never needed to know.

2

Something happened, in plain words

FindingIncident type came first on the old form, with options like first aid or lost time that nobody can predict while their hand is under the cold tap.

DecisionTwo choices, written as sentences a team member would say out loud, with the definition underneath. The industrial vocabulary never appears on the reporter's side.

RejectedA dropdown of incident types, which was the field people got wrong and were corrected on from above.

Checked against Clubhouse, report an issue

3

A hazard is a condition, not an event

FindingThe platform's hazard categories contained events rather than conditions, so a wet floor and a fall landed in the same list and the reporter had to guess which one they were looking at.

DecisionThe hazard path is a separate entry that describes a condition that could cause harm, and it skips every question about an injury.

RejectedOne combined form with conditional fields, which still opens on the question the reporter cannot answer.

Checked against Google Maps, adding a report

4

The guide sits where the hesitation is

FindingTeam members trusted their managers and nobody described feeling afraid to report. The barrier was the form, the phone and the threshold: not knowing whether a thing counted.

DecisionWorked examples in the reporter's own language sit on the deciding screen, and the promise that follows them is the one the rest of the flow keeps: you will not be asked anything you cannot answer.

RejectedA help link to a policy page, which moves the person away from the task at the moment they were already unsure.

Checked against Intercom, help centre

5

One thumb, one action

FindingRestaurant visits found phone policies that differed by site and reports being made standing up, mid-shift, on a personal phone.

DecisionA single full-width action pinned to the bottom of every step, at a size that works with one thumb and wet hands.

RejectedNext and Cancel side by side, which puts the destructive action under the thumb that is already moving.

Checked against Clubhouse, report an issue

Module 02

Dashboard: the restaurant manager's shift

What the research found

Location did not predict safety culture. The manager did. Where a manager talked about safety, thanked people for reports and showed the system on the first shift, reporting was strong regardless of where the restaurant was. That finding made the manager's screen the most important screen in the product.

So this dashboard is not a reporting console. It is a shift tool: what needs a person today, what is overdue, what was fixed and who reported it, and the routine that the research found to be the strongest lever. The vendor product opened on a permissions wall where the dashboard should have been.

The two charts answer the question the safety team could not answer from the platform's data: whether a quiet restaurant is a safe one. Hazards and incidents are plotted separately, because a restaurant finding hazards before they hurt anyone looks nothing like one that reports only after an injury.

12345678

Figure 2The restaurant manager's dashboard at 1440 by 900. Four counts, a weekly trend read in a sentence, the open reports with the next human act as their status, and the loop closed on the right.

1

One way in, and it is a verb

FindingThe platform assumed a team member would scan a poster and fill in the form. In practice they told the manager, who logged it or did not, so the manager is the heaviest user of the system.

DecisionThe manager's primary action is the same action the team member gets on a phone: Report something. It sits above the navigation, not inside a module, so logging on someone's behalf is one click from anywhere.

RejectedA New incident button inside the Incidents module, which makes the reporter choose the record type before they have described anything.

Checked against Vanta, action tracker

2

Sorted by what needs a person, not by record type

FindingReports sat waiting because nothing told the manager which ones needed them today; the platform's lists were organised by the record's type rather than by whether anyone was waiting.

DecisionThe first tile is Needs your review, counted across incidents and hazards together, with the reason written underneath in words a manager reads once.

RejectedA tile per module (incidents, hazards, actions) which reports the shape of the database rather than the shape of the shift.

Checked against Airtable, projects dashboard

3

Fixes are counted, and so is where they came from

FindingMinor reports were closed without a word, and over time people came to believe that reporting helps with a claim but changes nothing. The restaurant where a fault was fixed the same day had a story people kept telling.

DecisionFixed this period is a headline number, split to show how many came from the team's own reports. It is the number the manager repeats at the huddle.

RejectedCounting reports received, which rewards volume. The safety team's view was that an incentive on hazard reports gets you reports, not fewer hazards.

Checked against Airtable, projects dashboard

4

Two series, because the ratio is the signal

FindingReporting volume varied widely between restaurants, and low volume could mean a safe restaurant or a silent one. The platform's data could not tell the difference.

DecisionHazards and incidents are plotted side by side every week. A restaurant finding hazards before they hurt anyone looks different from one that reports only after an injury, and the reading is written under the chart in a sentence.

RejectedA single total-reports line, which is the number that could not distinguish a safe restaurant from a silent one.

Checked against Airtable, projects dashboard

5

The status is the next human act

FindingThe form asked for incident type and severity first, with options like first aid or lost time that nobody can pick while their hand is under the cold tap, and classification errors were sent back from above.

DecisionA report's status names what a person does next: welfare check due, action assigned, fixed and awaiting sign-off. Classification is an outcome the manager records later, so it never blocks the report.

RejectedStatus as a workflow code (open, triaged, pending classification), which describes the database's state rather than anyone's obligation.

Checked against incident.io, incident list

6

The loop, closed on the manager's screen

FindingReporters rarely heard what happened after they reported, and minor reports were closed silently.

DecisionA standing panel names each fix, who reported it and how long it took, with one action that prints the notice for the break room. Telling people is a default, not a task someone remembers.

RejectedA monthly email to the reporter, which depends on a mailbox most team members do not read on shift.

Checked against Vanta, action tracker

7

Hazards located, because the blueprint located them

FindingEvery observation in the research was tied to a stage of the incident journey and to the role it concerned, which is what let the findings point at exact places in the service rather than at themes.

DecisionHazards are counted by area of the restaurant, so the pattern points at the fryer line or the back door rather than at a category name.

RejectedA hazard-type breakdown, which sorts by the taxonomy the reporter was never able to apply.

Checked against Airtable, projects dashboard

8

The routine is part of the product

FindingWhere a manager talked about safety, thanked people for reports and showed the system on the first shift, reporting was strong regardless of where the restaurant was. Induction varied from a full demonstration to nothing.

DecisionToday's shift carries the routine the research found to be the strongest lever: a safety moment drawn from this week's reports, the new starter's first-shift walkthrough, the first-aid cabinet check.

RejectedLeaving the routine to a separate training system, which is where it lived when nobody could tell whether a new starter had been shown the system.

Checked against Jobber, home workflow strip

Module 03

Dashboard: the area manager, twelve restaurants

What the research found

Reporting volume varied widely between restaurants and nobody could say why. Low volume could mean a safe restaurant or a silent one, and the existing lever was a reminder email that went to everyone and told no one anything.

The area view compares restaurants on the ratio of hazards to incidents rather than on report counts, and it names the three conversations worth having this week instead of sending twelve people the same message.

This is the screen where the study's central finding becomes an operating instrument: if the manager is the variable, the area manager's job is to find the managers who need a conversation, and to have it on evidence.

Figure 3The area manager's dashboard. Twelve restaurants compared, three flagged, and the reasoning written next to the table rather than left to the reader.

Module 04

Incidents and hazards: one register, two types

What the research found

The platform's hazard categories contained events rather than conditions, so the two record types blurred at the point of entry and then had to be kept apart everywhere else. Reports also sat waiting because nothing said which ones needed a person today.

Incidents and hazards live in one register with one filter, because a manager's question is about their restaurant this week, not about a record type. The type is a column, not a navigation decision.

Status is written as the next human act. Welfare check due, action assigned, fixed and awaiting sign-off: each one names something a person does, so a list of statuses reads as a list of obligations.

Figure 4The register with the quick filters that match the manager's questions, and the type as a column rather than a menu.

The states that matter

Module 05

The incident record: welfare first, classification after

What the research found

The old form asked for the outcome before the event. Severity and the regulatory question sat at the top, answered by a person who could not yet know, and corrected later by someone above them. People learned that reporting was a way to get something wrong.

The record puts the sequence back in the order the shift actually runs. What the reporter told us, then the welfare check, then the classification drawn from what happened, then the cause and the control, then the actions, then the person who reported it being told what changed.

The status pipeline across the top is the argument in one line: the record is not finished when it is logged, it is finished when someone has been told.

Figure 5The incident record. The welfare check is the current step, and the classification below it is a manager's judgement recorded after the fact, not a reporter's guess recorded before it.

Module 06

Corrective actions: the half that was invisible

What the research found

Reporters rarely heard what happened next. Minor reports were closed without a word, which built the belief that reporting helps you with a claim but does nothing for anyone else. The exception proved it: the restaurant where a faulty piece of equipment was fixed the same day had a story people kept telling.

Actions are the part of the service where a report turns into a change, and in the old workflow they were the part nobody outside the office could see. Here they are a board with owners and due dates, and closing one asks for evidence and offers to tell the person who reported it, with the message written for them.

The proposal rejected recognition programmes that reward report counts. The safety team's view was blunt: put an incentive on hazard reports and you get reports, not fewer hazards. So the artefact this module produces is a notice of outcomes, not a leaderboard.

Figure 6The corrective actions board. Overdue is a state of the card, not a report someone runs, and the closed column is the one the monthly notice is printed from.

Module 07

Emergency information: the screen nobody reads until they must

Design decision

Emergency information was not the study's focus. The restaurant visits did look at the posters, the first-aid cabinet and the office setup, and the finding that carries over is the one about language: the people who need this are new, busy and do not know the terminology.

So the module is a picture of the restaurant rather than a list of records: where the extinguisher, the blanket, the eyewash and the spill kit actually are, with a legend in plain words and a date beside each check.

The right-hand column is the part used under pressure. Numbers big enough to read at arm's length, in the order you would need them, with the first-aider roster showing its own gap rather than hiding it.

Figure 7Emergency information for one restaurant. The floor sketch is the record; the list beneath it is how the record gets checked.

Module 08

Chemical register: an obligation that expires

Design decision

Chemicals were outside the study's scope. The principle applied here is the one the research established everywhere else: the system should answer the question the person on shift actually has, in language they already use.

A safety data sheet is a compliance document with an expiry date, so the register leads with what is expiring and what is missing rather than with an alphabetical list of products.

The panel is written for the moment it is needed: what this is, what to wear, what to do if it goes on skin or in eyes, and the three steps for a spill. The regulatory document is attached underneath, not in front.

Figure 8The chemical register. Status first, alphabet second, and PPE as a column so a new starter can read the requirement without opening anything.

Module 09

Contractor management: the people the system forgets

Design decision

Contractors were outside the study's scope. The finding that carries over is that managers work through the system on behalf of everyone else, and that open hazards were held in a record nobody on the floor could see.

Signing a contractor in is where an open hazard becomes useful to someone who has never been in the building: the sign-in reads the restaurant's open hazards and tells them, on the record, before they start.

Induction and insurance are dates that expire, so they are badges with a date on the row rather than a document someone has to open to find out.

Figure 9Contractors. Who is on site now sits above the register, because that is the question a manager is asked, not who is on the list.

Module 10

Knowledge base: the threshold question, answered

What the research found

The barrier was not fear. Team members trusted their managers and nobody described feeling embarrassed to report, even when they had been doing the wrong thing. The barrier was the form, the phone and the threshold: not knowing whether a thing counted. A plain-language guide to what counts was one of the concept mockups delivered in December 2025.

So the knowledge base opens on the question people actually type, and its first article is the one that unblocks reporting: incident or hazard, with examples from a restaurant rather than definitions from a standard.

The answer to not being sure is written into the article and matches the promise the reporting flow makes: report it as whatever it looks like to you, and the manager sorts out the classification from what happened.

Figure 10The knowledge base. Search first, then the article that resolves the threshold question, with the examples in the words people used in interviews.

The design system

One set of tokens, one set of components, no framework

Every screen here is hand-written HTML on two stylesheets: a token file and a component file. Nothing is generated by a UI kit and nothing is a picture of a component. The tokens below are read out of the live token file when this page is built, so what you see is what the screens use.

The type scale has seven steps from a 14px base. Spacing is a 4px grid. There are three radii and two elevations. Colour is one neutral ramp plus four status pairs, each pair a foreground and a ground; a contrast script checks every text pair at 4.5 to 1 and every border at 3 to 1 before a screen is captured, and the muted ink and the strong line were both darkened after it failed them.

Numbers are set in tabular figures so a column of counts aligns. Status is never carried by colour alone: every badge has a dot and a word. The sidebar is the one dark surface, which keeps the working area white and lets a status colour mean something.
