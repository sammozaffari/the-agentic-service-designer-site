Pilots, Scaling & Organizational Change at Aurelia Insurance

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Aurelia Insurance is a fictional composite of several mid-size European insurers; every name, figure, and dataset in this article is illustrative and anonymized. This phase-2 case study continues the claims redesign first documented in article 03: the pilot outcomes are preserved, but all rollout details here are reconstructed for teaching purposes. The point is to share implementation mechanics, governance, and AI-first working methods, not client specifics. Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were used as the primary working tools; the human designer kept responsibility for framing, politics, ethics, and every go or no-go decision.

1. Executive summary

Article 03 proved that Aurelia Insurance could redesign claims as a service: a two-region pilot across 11,200 claims cut cycle time by 52%, reduced status-chase calls by 81%, and lifted First-Contact Resolution from 61% to 83%. The hard part started the week after that gate. Scaling the service nationally meant moving from two champion-heavy urban regions to a 900,000-policy insurer with four regional centres, eight satellite claims desks, legacy batch architecture, rural phone-first claimants, skeptical middle managers, and dozens of locally invented workarounds. Over 14 months Aurelia treated rollout as design continued: it audited what the pilot had not tested, built a six-wave rollout play, formed a cross-site change network, versioned runbooks and readiness gates in GitHub, and used AI agents to author training packs, maintain a site-by-wave tracker, stress-test the business case, and generate readiness dashboards. Nationally, the scaled service settled all claims in 15.8 days versus a 28.9-day baseline, lifted FCR to 79%, improved claims NPS from −12 to +18, and verified a €5.9M conservative annual run-rate benefit after the 13-week hardening review.

What you will learn:

  • How to close the pilot-rollout gap: what a successful pilot proves, what it hides, and how to convert pilot evidence into a six-wave national rollout plan.
  • How to run organizational change as service design: manager incentive resets, frontline enablement, journey governance, and explicit choices about which legacy workarounds to kill versus keep.
  • How to use AI agents in implementation without AI-washing the work: runbook authoring, readiness dashboards, risk-register review, and rollout governance as versioned operational artifacts.

2. The brief

Client portrait. Aurelia Insurance is the same fictional Benelux insurer introduced in article 03: roughly 900,000 active policies, about 96,000 claims a year, and four regional claims centres supported by eight smaller satellite desks that handle broker queries, phone overflow, and complex-document intake. The insurer writes motor, home, and small commercial lines, operates with two legacy PAS environments plus a shared claims module, and still carries the cultural shape of a traditional insurer: strong regional autonomy, manager scorecards tilted toward handle time and file-age metrics, and high local pride in "how we do claims here." The phase-1 pilot ran in the east and west regions, which together represented about 47% of annual claim volume and had the strongest local managers.

The challenge. The pilot succeeded, but scaling exposed three gaps immediately. First, the pilot skewed urban and digitally capable: it proved the self-service assistant, proactive milestone messaging, and concierge model under good staffing conditions, but it barely touched rural phone-heavy behaviour or broker-heavy home claims. Second, east and west had managers who co-designed the service; north and south had to inherit it. Middle-manager resistance appeared within two weeks of the board approval: "our claims are different," "the pilot had extra people," and "this will hurt my queue numbers." Third, local workarounds sat everywhere: handler-owned spreadsheets, manager-only approval inboxes, a broker fax queue kept alive for one commercial desk, and shadow SMS lists maintained by supervisors because the old telephony platform could not reliably push confirmations before 09:00. The real problem was no longer whether the service concept worked. It was whether Aurelia could replace vertical local habits with one national service system without breaking claims performance in the process.

Constraints. Scale budget capped at €1.9M including change, training, and vendor work; no PAS replacement; works-council consultation required for scorecard changes; broker contracts could not be rewritten midyear; storm season would spike motor and home claims in waves 4 and 5; and the board wanted rollout complete inside 14 months so the next renewal cycle could benefit. The service also had to stay audit-safe: fraud review thresholds, complaint handling rules, and payout authorities could evolve only with compliance sign-off.

Why service design. This was implementation in the exact sense described by the literature: design carried out under live organizational constraints, where the organization itself is the delivery system (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/07-implementation.md). Aurelia did not need a project-management overlay alone. It needed a journey governance model, frontline enablement, capability transfer, incentive redesign, and a disciplined distinction between what the pilot had proven and what the rollout still had to learn (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md). It also needed protection from corporate antibodies — demands for false certainty, local budget protection, and the reliability trap that punishes experimentation once the project leaves the innovation halo (See: 15-change-by-design/07-design-thinking-meets-the-corporation.md).

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

Aurelia treated scaling as a second design programme, not a mechanical deployment of article 03. The double diamond still applied, but the emphasis shifted: Discover meant auditing pilot evidence and local resistance; Define meant building the rollout architecture and governance; Develop meant creating runbooks, training, trackers, and gate logic; Deliver meant six operational waves plus a hardening loop. The same future-state service blueprint from article 03 became a binding national operating contract, translated into readiness gates, site scorecards, manager standards, and GitHub-controlled runbooks.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Discover what the pilot did not prove

Goal. Convert article 03's successful pilot into a realistic national rollout thesis by identifying missing conditions, hidden dependencies, and change risks.

Method & SOP. The team ran a structured pilot-to-rollout review using three inputs: the phase-1 pilot scorecard, site-by-site operating diagnostics, and stakeholder incentive mapping (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md, 04-service-design-for-business/06-organizational-change.md). The SOP was simple and strict. For every pilot success, ask: under what operating conditions did this occur; which of those conditions disappear at national scale; and which site types, managers, or claimant segments were absent? The team then layered on a corporate-antibody diagnostic: who would rationally resist, why, and what metrics or budget logic would make that resistance predictable rather than personal (See: 15-change-by-design/07-design-thinking-meets-the-corporation.md).

AI execution. Claude Code was used as the first-pass synthesis engine. One agent compared pilot and control metrics from article 03 against non-pilot regional baselines; another extracted every unresolved exception from pilot steering notes; a third built a "not yet proven" register with evidence links back to the pilot readout. OpenCode ran the operational comparison pipeline over six months of post-pilot claims data, segmenting by site type, line of business, and intake channel. That is where the first major scale risk surfaced: outside the pilot regions, 63% of rural home claims still entered through phone or broker, and those claimants had 1.9 times the document resubmission rate of urban digital claimants. Codex then challenged the national business case before any rollout wave was locked:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read article 03 metrics, rollout assumptions,
and the site segmentation table. Attack the scale case. Identify where pilot
adoption, manager coverage, training intensity, or legacy-system throughput are
unlikely to hold at national level. Return only defects that change the rollout
plan or the economics."

Codex flagged two real defects. The first draft assumed pilot adoption levels would hold in all regions; the rural phone-heavy sites made that false. The second assumed manager coaching capacity was evenly distributed; in reality three sites had supervisors carrying individual caseloads and could not run change properly without backfill.

Human designer judgment. The designer made two politically important calls. First, the programme would stop describing non-pilot regions as "laggards" and instead describe them as operating under different constraints. Second, the programme would not promise a pure digital rollout. The service concept remained digital plus human. That choice preserved credibility with brokers, works council members, and the supervisors running phone-heavy teams.

Artifact produced. A pilot-rollout gap register with 27 items, of which five changed the rollout sequence: rural hybrid channel design, manager backfill, legacy batch-load rehearsal, broker handoff preservation, and a wave-specific training model instead of one national course.

4.2 Define the national operating model and governance

Goal. Turn the phase-1 blueprint into a national operating contract with named decision rights, explicit gate logic, and a sequencing model that fits Aurelia's real delivery system.

Method & SOP. This phase combined blueprint-to-backlog translation with journey governance design (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/07-implementation.md, 14-outside-in/07-governance-and-culture.md). The future-state claims blueprint from article 03 was re-walked column by column and lane by lane, but this time every step had to answer four implementation questions: which sites will run it first; who owns the metric; which legacy behaviour must stop; and which exception path remains valid at scale. The team also used the journey governance charter pattern from the organizational-change literature: appoint one national claims Journey Owner, create a cross-functional Claims Design Council, define escalation routes, and integrate CX review gates into every change affecting the claims journey.

AI execution. Claude Code authored the first draft of the rollout charter, KPI owner matrix, manager-standard cards, and the change-pack skeleton. GitHub became the implementation control plane: one epic per wave, issues tagged by blueprint step ID, and pull requests as the gate for any runbook or training change. OpenCode built the first version of the rollout tracker and dashboard spec from the site matrix. To keep the governance structure honest, Codex reviewed the risk register and charter language for missing decision rights, especially around disputed trade-offs between claims speed, fraud-control, and local manager autonomy.

Representative rollout tracker build:

opencode run --agent build "Read articles/21/data/rollout_sample.csv and

volume, FCR, NPS delta, adoption, and red amber green status. Output a static

Human designer judgment. The crucial decisions here were subtractive. Three workarounds were killed nationally: the handler shadow spreadsheet, the manager-only approval inbox, and the supervisor shadow SMS list. Two were intentionally retained with expiry dates: broker-assisted FNOL for commercial fleet and a phone-first severe-weather intake path for high-distress home claims. Those were not compromises of principle; they were controlled exceptions backed by claimant evidence and operational reality.

Artifact produced. The national rollout charter, phased scaling logic, current-versus-target operating model, governance matrix, and 186 implementation issues across Operations, IT, HR, Compliance, and Broker Support.

4.3 Develop the change system around frontline adoption

Goal. Equip frontline staff and middle managers with the capability, authority, and motivation to deliver the new service without coercion or blame.

Method & SOP. Aurelia used the frontline enablement triad directly: capability, authority, motivation (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/07-implementation.md). Capability meant scenario-based training, cleaner tools, and one versioned claims playbook. Authority meant explicit discretionary thresholds, clarified escalation routes, and permission to resolve routine exceptions without manager delay. Motivation meant changing the scorecards: FCR, claimant effort, and adherence to leader standards replaced pure handle-time and open-file-age measures. The change plan also followed the distributed-champion model from the organizational-change chapter: every site nominated one champion and one skeptic into the change network, so the programme did not create an innovation-club blind to local objections (See: 04-service-design-for-business/06-organizational-change.md).

AI execution. Claude Code authored the change pack in layers: site kickoff decks, FAQ sets for handlers and team leaders, scenario rehearsal scripts, and a runbook diff summary for every wave. The highest-value SOP was the change-pack authoring workflow itself: one agent drafted, one agent checked evidence against pilot and site diagnostics, and one agent applied a guardrail pass for tone, coercion, or hidden blame. GitHub PRs enforced review: no training pack merged without one operations reviewer and one site champion reviewer. OpenCode maintained the readiness checklist and certification tracker. Codex reviewed the risk register and the most sensitive comms.

You are CHANGE-PACK-AUTHOR. Input: site profile, current metrics, top three
local objections, national operating model, runbook diff from previous wave.
Output sections in order: why this wave, what changes for handlers, what changes
for team leaders, what stops, what stays, top five FAQs, three rehearsal
scenarios, site-specific risks, escalation path.
Rules: no blame language, no claim that staff resistance is irrational, no
promise of benefits without local evidence, cite at least one pilot or site
metric in every why section.

Human designer judgment. The designer rejected the first AI draft of team-leader communications because it framed low adoption as a discipline problem. It was rewritten around obstacle removal, not compliance theatre. The designer also insisted that middle managers keep agency: they could adapt staffing patterns and coaching sequences locally as long as they did not violate the service standard. That distinction mattered. It turned the design council into a governing body, not a script police unit.

Artifact produced. The change map, stakeholder map, site-persona set, go or no-go scorecard template, risk register template, leader standards, and versioned rollout-wave SOPs.

4.4 Deliver the six-wave rollout

Goal. Roll out the redesigned claims service nationally without confusing a rollout for a pilot repeat: every wave must meet readiness gates, expose real local conditions, and either scale, iterate, or pause.

Method & SOP. Each wave followed the same rollout-wave SOP-as-code. Ten working days before go-live, the site had to show certified staff, production-tested interfaces, live feedback capture, compensation guardrails, and an agreed red-issue owner. Seven days after go-live, the wave room reviewed volume, FCR, claimant sentiment, and open defects daily. At week 4 and week 13, the service review decided whether the site was green, amber, or red. This was deliberately unit-level measurement, not corporate averaging, following the Service Profit Chain warning against hiding weak sites inside enterprise means (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/06-leadership-and-implementation.md).

AI execution. GitHub structured the work. Each wave lived as an epic with linked issues for site prep, training, technical fixes, and sign-offs. A GitHub Action regenerated the readiness dashboard from data/rollout_sample.csv plus the live tracker tables and published a per-site HTML summary for the steering group. Claude Code drafted the weekly wave-review packs; OpenCode refreshed the dashboards nightly; Codex reviewed the risk register when a site remained amber beyond week 4. The programme's most useful automation was not glamorous: a runbook-diff bot that highlighted exactly what changed between waves so handlers did not have to reread a 40-page pack.

gh issue create --title "Wave 4 readiness review"   --label rollout,wave-4,claims-site   --body-file rollout/wave-4/go-no-go-pack.md

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Review rollout/wave-6/risk-register.md.
Flag risks that are understated, duplicated, or missing mitigations. Pay special
attention to local manager incentives, telephony fallback, and complaint volume."

Human designer judgment. Go-live decisions never came from the dashboard alone. In wave 4 the GRO-Rural site hit the numeric readiness threshold, but the designer and Journey Owner held go-live 72 hours because the local broker desk could not yet see the same status timestamps as the claims core. In wave 6 the NAM-Legacy site went live even though readiness was only 74 because keeping it permanently outside the new service would have institutionalized a second-class journey; instead the team paired the go-live with on-site coaching and a temporary exception budget.

Artifact produced. A six-wave rollout history, site tracker, readiness pack archive, and a national hardening backlog of 43 items, 17 of which were closed before Gate G5.

4.5 Harden, measure, and embed

Goal. Prevent the service from decaying back into local variants once the formal programme ends.

Method & SOP. Aurelia moved from programme governance to operating governance. The Claims Design Council became the monthly forum for journey health, while a rolling 13-week review loop became the hardening mechanism: every site is re-scored 13 weeks after go-live on adoption, FCR, claimant sentiment, complaint volume, and leader-standard adherence. This follows the governance-and-culture logic directly: customer-centricity survives only when decision rights, review rhythms, and incentives make it the default behaviour (See: 14-outside-in/07-governance-and-culture.md). Leadership also adopted a "walk the chain" routine: every regional director and one executive sponsor had to spend one half-day per quarter on claimant calls or handler shadowing, logging barriers for removal (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/06-leadership-and-implementation.md).

AI execution. OpenCode maintained the 13-week review dataset and produced the outlier tables. Claude Code turned the review into an actionable pack: top three wins, top three breakpoints, sites drifting from leader standards, and required runbook edits. Codex challenged the post-rollout benefit case again and found one inflation error in the first draft: a proposed call-cost saving assumed all reduced contacts were fully removable labour, which Operations rejected. The corrected readout kept only verified cost-to-serve improvements and a conservative retention effect.

Human designer judgment. The designer pushed for one more hard choice: delete the old SOP library. As long as both old and new playbooks remained accessible, local reversion stayed socially acceptable. After Gate G5 only the new national SOP library remained active, with two documented exception playbooks retained by design.

Artifact produced. The KPI tree, traceability table linking pilot learnings to scaled controls, the national claims audit, and a permanent governance cadence owned by the claims Journey Owner rather than the project team.

6. Use cases / case studies

UC1 — High-volume urban region rollout with legacy ramp

Scenario. Wave 1 opened in AMS-Central, Aurelia's largest urban claims site at roughly 1,055 claims per month. It looked like the safest place to scale because digital uptake was already high. It was not. Morning batch lag created 35 to 50 minutes of stale status during the first two days, which immediately triggered social-media complaints from motor claimants who expected the pilot's near-live visibility.

What was designed. A dual-rail urban ramp: the new claim journey went live nationally at AMS-Central, but status timestamps displayed a plain-language freshness banner until the replay job stabilized. Team leaders also ran a 10-day "urban spike" staffing pattern that shifted two handlers from afternoon backlog work into morning live-contact response.

Mini flow. incident -> digital claim -> status freshness banner if stale > 20 min -> live update -> self-serve or handler rescue

AI working session summary. Claude Code wrote the day-1 exception script and the claim-status freshness copy after reviewing the pilot messaging library from article 03. OpenCode built a one-page ops dashboard showing stale-timestamp counts by 15-minute interval. Codex reviewed the public-facing copy to ensure it was transparent without over-disclosing system weakness.

Outcome. AMS-Central landed week-4 FCR at 78%, NPS delta at +30, and adoption at 62%. More importantly, the site proved the programme could expose legacy-system limitations without hiding them from claimants. The freshness banner was later reused in waves 2 and 3, then retired after the replay job stabilized.

UC2 — Rural region where phone dominates and the service stays hybrid

Scenario. Wave 5 included HSS-Phone, a low-volume but strategically important site covering rural home claims and elderly policyholders. Here the pilot's digital-first assumptions failed fastest: 68% of eligible claimants still started by phone in the first five days, and brokers warned that a forced app path would push vulnerable customers back into complaint loops.

What was designed. A deliberately hybrid service. The site used the same national blueprint, but the front door differed: handlers followed a guided phone FNOL script, sent SMS proof-of-claim links, and offered a scheduled callback instead of insisting on live self-service completion. Team leaders listened to three calls per handler per week to coach clarity rather than speed.

AI working session summary. Claude Code authored the phone-script pack, including the plain-language phrases that explained why a claimant might receive a text after a phone report. OpenCode instrumented a call-tagging dashboard that measured how often guided calls later converted into self-serve evidence uploads. Codex reviewed the broker briefing note and removed two sentences that implied brokers were a temporary legacy channel; politically and operationally, that was false.

Outcome. HSS-Phone reached 74% adoption of the new flow only because phone-assisted claims counted as adoption when they used the same evidence and status backbone. FCR reached 80%, NPS delta +34, and complaint volume stayed flat despite the channel shift. This was the clearest proof that scale did not mean uniformity.

UC3 — Recalcitrant region where pilot learnings did not transfer

Scenario. NAM-Legacy, wave 6, was the programme's hardest site. The supervisor persona represented by Pieter in A5 was real enough in composite form: local managers believed their spreadsheet tracker and approval inbox were what made the site performant. Pre-go-live observation showed handlers printing the new runbook while still keeping the old spreadsheet open beside it.

What was designed. The site did not get a heavier training deck. It got a different change design. The team paired the skeptical supervisor with Marta, a champion from RTM-West, for two live coaching days; removed handle-time targets from the site scorecard before go-live; and turned the spreadsheet into the explicit subject of the first 13-week review rather than pretending it would disappear by decree.

AI working session summary. Claude Code generated an adoption-diagnosis pack that compared NAM-Legacy with RTM-West on leader standards, not just claimant metrics. OpenCode plotted where the spreadsheet created duplicate status work. Codex reviewed the risk register and insisted the programme name the true risk: not low enthusiasm, but management incentives still rewarding the old behaviour.

Outcome. NAM-Legacy went live at readiness 74, below the programme average, and underperformed at week 4: FCR 68%, adoption 41%, NPS delta +12. By week 13, after the scorecard change and coaching intervention, the site improved to FCR 72%, adoption 56%, and NPS delta +19. It remained the only amber site at Gate G5. That was acceptable because the service had a truthful remediation plan, not because the programme averaged it away.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

This engagement worked when methods were encoded as executable operating routines rather than vague instructions. The pattern repeated across artifacts:

Method -> step list -> output schema -> acceptance criteria -> review gate.

Meta-SOP used across the programme

1. Read prior evidence and the current site context.
2. Extract only grounded facts; quote pilot or site evidence where available.
3. Produce the requested artifact in the required schema.
4. Run a self-check against guardrails and number consistency.
5. Send to human or PR gate for sign-off before it affects a live wave.

SOP 1 — Change-pack authoring

  • Input: site profile, prior-wave defect list, pilot evidence, latest runbook diff.
  • Output schema: why this wave, what changes, what stops, what stays, FAQs, rehearsal scenarios, risks, escalation path.
  • Acceptance criteria: every why statement cites evidence; no blame language; no unsupported promise of savings; site-specific objections addressed.
  • Review gate: one operations reviewer plus one site champion reviewer in GitHub.

SOP 2 — Rollout-wave SOP as code

  • Input: readiness scorecard, site risk register, certification records, production test log.
  • Output schema: go/no-go decision pack with weighted scores, blockers, and owner actions.
  • Acceptance criteria: any failed critical gate stops go-live; metrics tied to named owners.
  • Review gate: Journey Owner, regional director, and COO delegate.

SOP 3 — Adoption-diagnosis prompt

You are ADOPTION-DIAGNOSER. Compare one weak site to one healthy site.
Inputs: readiness table, leader-standards audit, claims-volume mix, channel mix,
open defects, site notes. Output exactly five sections: (1) what differs in
context, (2) which differences are controllable, (3) which differences are not,
(4) first three actions, (5) what evidence would disconfirm your diagnosis.
Rules: never say staff resistance is the root cause without identifying the
broken tool, metric, policy, or backlog condition that makes the behavior
rational.

Guardrails

  • No coercive communications. AI-generated training packs were forbidden from using shame, urgency theatre, or "compliance mindset" language.
  • Preserve middle-manager agency. Agents could recommend leader standards, but not centralize every staffing decision.
  • Evidence over rhetoric. Change claims had to cite article-03 pilot results, local site metrics, or direct observation notes.
  • Human sign-off at all live gates. No AI-authored readiness pack could trigger a go-live without named operational approval.
  • Privacy controls. The tracker used site-level operational data only; no personal claimant data entered the article repo.

Failure modes observed and mitigated

  1. Training pack blames users. First Claude Code draft framed low adoption as poor discipline. Mitigation: tone guardrail pass plus human rewrite.
  2. Business-case optimism leakage. First Codex challenge found removable labour overstated. Mitigation: only verified cost-to-serve savings survive.
  3. Generic rollout advice. Agents initially produced one-size-fits-all wave plans. Mitigation: site profile required input and site-type segmentation.
  4. Diagram drift from operations. Early blueprint updates simplified away broker and phone exceptions. Mitigation: keep controlled exceptions visible in the blueprint and runbook.

Causal logic

The Service Profit Chain logic became more convincing at scale than in the pilot because Aurelia could see the middle links move, not just the endpoints. Internal service quality improved when shadow tools were removed, site dashboards made work visible, and leader standards forced supervisors to coach rather than inspect. That raised handler eNPS and reduced local firefighting. Capability then rose: FCR improved, complex-claim routing stabilized, and adoption held even outside the pilot halo. External value followed — lower effort, faster cycle time, fewer status chases — which lifted NPS and renewal confidence. Only after those links held at site level did the financial result become credible. That sequence matters. It is the main reason the programme resisted board pressure to judge rollout success on cost alone (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/06-leadership-and-implementation.md).

9. AI tool playbook

Claude Code

Role on this engagement. Primary artifact author, synthesis partner, and runbook maintainer.

Concrete usage. Claude Code produced the rollout charter, change packs, FAQ sets, leader-standard cards, and weekly wave-review packs; it also generated the traceability table connecting article 03 pilot learnings to article 21 controls.

What it excelled at. Turning structured evidence and templates into usable operational documents fast, especially when the schema was explicit.

What it failed at. Tone drift under change pressure. Left unguarded, it over-framed resistance as a people problem.

When the human took over. Any communication to middle managers, works-council-sensitive text, and any go-live recommendation.

OpenCode

Role on this engagement. Data pipeline and dashboard builder.

Concrete usage. Built the site-by-wave tracker, readiness dashboards, and adoption comparison views from rollout_sample.csv and the live operational feeds.

What it excelled at. Mechanical data transformation and dashboard scaffolding with deterministic outputs.

What it failed at. It treated the store column literally until the brief clarified that it meant claims site; context mapping still needed a human.

When the human took over. Metric-definition disputes, site segmentation logic, and any interpretation of why a site was weak.

Codex

Role on this engagement. Adversarial reviewer for economics, governance gaps, and change risk.

Concrete usage. Stress-tested national-scale assumptions, challenged the risk register, and reviewed claimant-facing or staff-facing copy that carried political or regulatory risk.

What it excelled at. Finding over-optimistic assumptions and missing control logic.

What it failed at. It could recommend over-conservative pauses when live operational nuance mattered more than abstract risk containment.

When the human took over. Final business-case posture, deciding whether an amber site should still go live, and interpreting resistance in organizational context.

GitHub

Role on this engagement. System of record and operating gate.

Concrete usage. Rollout epics, site issues, PR review gates for runbooks and training packs, and Actions generating per-site readiness dashboards and diff summaries.

What it excelled at. Making implementation artifacts inspectable, diffable, and owned.

What it failed at. GitHub does not create adoption. Without live leadership behaviour, a perfectly versioned rollout can still die locally.

When the human took over. Steering conversations, escalation decisions, and reconciling dashboard truth with frontline reality.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • A successful pilot can hide managerial heroics. If rollout assumes those heroics are normal operating conditions, scale will underperform.
  • The middle-manager layer is where service design becomes real organizational change. Aurelia's hardest problems were scorecards and authority, not UI.
  • Killing a workaround is a design act. Some workarounds are pathological; some are value-preserving exceptions that should stay until a better replacement exists.
  • National consistency does not require identical channel mixes. Rural phone-assisted claims still counted as adoption because they used the same service backbone.
  • AI-generated training content is high-leverage and high-risk. Without tone guardrails it can produce polished blame.
  • Versioning runbooks in GitHub mattered more than prettier slides. Diff visibility reduced training fatigue across waves.
  • Corporate averages are dangerous in rollout. NAM-Legacy would have disappeared inside the national mean; the 13-week review loop prevented that.
  • Deleting the old SOP library was necessary. Coexistence between old and new instructions is usually disguised non-adoption.
