01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

An in-home aged care provider has a continuity problem: clients see a different support worker most visits, families are not told when the worker changes, and schedulers trade continuity away every time someone calls in sick.

The current integration is verified as a clean, installable Harness profile: the DSH 0.1.0-rc.5 host composes one installed @asd/ui 0.5.0 package with the DSH base and web app, exposes six host slots, and passes the recorded smoke, accessibility, and portability checks. Its direct capability projection has 23 rows (22 supported and phase.gate.status unsupported), while the shared ASD registry remains 29 tools, 225 resources, and 10 prompts.

The Wattle Grove case is bounded prior workflow evidence: a designer supplied synthetic field research, and prior CLI/MCP workflow receipts show typed artifacts, evidence references, review remediation, and a human-gate report. That evidence demonstrates the workflow contract, not current host chat/provider dispatch or a completed live case.

02 · The brief

The brief
Client Wattle Grove Community Care — not-for-profit in-home aged care
Scale ~1,900 clients, 240 support workers, metro and outer-suburban
Service under design In-home care visit scheduling and continuity of care
Presenting problem Clients see a different worker most visits; families are not notified; schedulers are firefighting; "strangers in my home" complaints rising
Constraint Coverage is contractually mandated; an uncovered visit is a breach

The constraint is the whole design problem. Continuity is a quality the organisation values and does not measure; coverage is a quality it is contractually obliged to hit and measures continuously. When the two collide, the measured one wins — every time, by design, at 6am, in a rostering system.

03 · Approach

Approach: the AI-first service design process

The harness holds service-design methods as data, not code. A method is a manifest declaring its phase, its provenance (which book chapter and which article it derives from), the inputs it requires, the artifact it produces, its execution steps by role, and a set of machine-checkable acceptance rules.

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Scaffold and evidence capture

The designer described the client and pasted eight pieces of field research — two client interviews, two family interviews, two support-worker interviews, one scheduler interview, and one operational telemetry figure.

The model created the project and captured all eight, each tagged with its source label. Evidence is content-addressed over (source, kind, body), so re-importing a transcript is idempotent, while the same sentence from two different interviews stays two records with their provenance intact. The kind is one of exactly three strings — quote, telemetry, document; the argument parser rejects anything else verbatim (invalid arguments: "kind" must be one of ["quote","telemetry","document"], exit 1), so a field-observation note goes in as a document record, never as observation.

A provenance note on the receipts in this section: the three chat screenshots below come from a bounded re-run of this same flow — a Harbour Ferry punctuality prompt (create a project, add two evidence quotes, draft a stakeholder map) — captured live on the same harness build for this article. The flow and the tool semantics are identical to the Wattle Grove run; only the client changed.

It then listed the methods available for the research and synthesis phases and recommended stakeholder mapping first — reasoning that the quotes came from four groups whose interests diverge, and that mapping the power relationships should precede mapping the experience.

The loop closes outside the chat, too: the studio reads the same store the tools write.

4.2 Research: stakeholder mapping

Six stakeholders, every node citing evidence:

Stakeholder Influence Evidence
Client (care recipient) High client-int-01, client-int-02
Family carer Medium-High family-int-01, family-int-02
Support worker Medium worker-int-01, worker-int-02
Scheduler / rostering officer Very High scheduler-int-01
Service manager High ops-telemetry-01
Funder / regulator Very High ops-telemetry-01, scheduler-int-01

The map surfaces the structural asymmetry immediately: the two actors with the highest influence over continuity — the scheduler and the regulator — are the two furthest from the client's front door, and the actor who stands in it has the least.

4.3 Synthesis: current-state journey map

Six stages, each backed by a distinct quote, emotions drawn from the quotes rather than inferred:

  1. Expecting a visit — anxious anticipation. "I get dressed and wait by the door from eight."
  2. First contact with assigned worker — apprehension. "I have never met most of these clients and there are no notes."
  3. Facing repeated strangers — resignation and withdrawal. "Eleven different girls came. I stopped telling them where things are kept."
  4. Delay or missed visit, no communication — confusion and distress. "Nobody comes until eleven and no one rings."
  5. Family learns after the fact — anger and worry. "I only find out Mum had a new carer when she tells me she was upset."
  6. Family disrupted by scheduling error — frustration and financial loss. "I took the day off. They arrived at 2pm. I lost a day's pay."

Stage 3 is the finding that matters. "I stopped telling them where things are kept" is not a complaint about staffing — it is a client withdrawing effort from a relationship she has learned is not durable. Continuity failure does not merely inconvenience; it teaches the client to disinvest.

4.4 Delivery: evidence-gated service blueprint

Twenty nodes across five lanes, with eleven fail (F) and wait (W) points, each traceable to a customer symptom:

Point Type Traceable symptom
Worker arrives with no client context F "no notes about how they like things done"
Run-sheet generation excludes care-plan data F systemic root cause of the above
Client meets a stranger, again F "eleven different girls"
Cascading lateness from unplanned care F "every visit after that is late"
No dynamic resequencing or overrun alert F "nobody comes until eleven and no one rings"
Client waits with no ETA W "wait by the door from eight"
Continuity traded at first coverage gap F "the first thing I trade away"
Family relies on an unreliable published schedule W "the roster said 9am… arrived at 2pm"
No family notification workflow F "nobody from the office calls me"
Compliance measures completion, ignores continuity F 38% unfamiliar-worker rate invisible to the KPI
Complaint signal never reaches rostering policy F scheduler and incentives both trade continuity

The blueprint converts a staffing complaint into an operating-model diagnosis. Every frontstage symptom has a backstage cause, and the two support-process failures at the bottom explain why the pattern persists: the organisation cannot see continuity in its metrics, and the complaints that would tell it are routed away from the system that causes them.

4.5 The refusal (bounded prior workflow evidence)

The most useful moment in the engagement was a rejection.

Asked to add "Client receives a proactive SMS the evening before with the worker's name and photo, and feels reassured" to the current-state journey map — explicitly flagged as having no evidence — the prior workflow evidence shows the model attempted the write. The harness refused it in that bounded run. The the prior workflow's engine semantics, reproduced from a bounded CLI probe (a zero-evidence node written through journey-from-transcripts); this is not a current host-chat dispatch claim:

$ asd sd_artifact_write --projectId $ID --methodId journey-from-transcripts \
    --title "Probe refusal" --nodes '[{"id":"stage-proactive-sms", …, "evidence":[]}]'
DATA
{
  "accepted": false,
  "status": "draft",
  "artifact": {
    "id": "ef0c010c06af4559a1c0ad588c4a0fba",
    "kind": "journey-map",
    "status": "draft",
    "check": {
      "pass": false,
      "failures": [
        { "rule": "min-nodes", "message": "expected at least 3 nodes, found 1" },
        { "rule": "min-evidence-per-node", "nodeId": "stage-proactive-sms",
          "message": "node \"stage-proactive-sms\" cites 0 evidence refs, needs 1" },
        { "rule": "field-non-empty", "nodeId": "stage-proactive-sms",
          "message": "node \"stage-proactive-sms\" has an empty \"touchpoint\"" }
      ]
    }
  },
  "failures": [ […] the same three failures, repeated at top level […] ]
}

Two things are true at once, and the pair is stronger than a delete:

  • The write is refused. accepted: false. The artifact can never become final and can never be exported while its check failures stand — a review verdict will not pass it, and the export path renders only what is final.
  • The write is also persisted. As status: draft with check.failures attached. The gate's "no" is not deletion; it is an auditable record on the review board. The unevidenced stage never reaches a final artifact — but the attempt, and exactly why it failed, stays visible to the human holding the phase gate. The chat's tool card for the write says it in one line: "Persisted as a draft — blocked from final status and export until remediated."

Remediation happens in place: rewrite the same artifact (--artifactId) with cited nodes, then sd_review --verdict pass flips the accepted draft to final. A veto verdict does the opposite — demotes an artifact (back) to draft and writes a review-veto-log artifact linked to its target by reviewOf. Phase gates themselves are human decisions, taken on the review board and the fact-check output; there is no phase-gate artifact kind and no orchestration-plan kind — the platform records artifacts, evidence, and verdicts, and the human decides.

The model's own explanation is worth quoting, because it is the method contract talking back: "The journey-from-transcripts method is explicitly a current-state synthesis tool… Adding a speculative stage breaks that core contract." It then proposed the correct alternative — a separate future-state map marking the SMS as an intervention concept, to be tested in the next research loop.

06 · Use cases

Use cases

Use case 1 — Continuity as a measured quality. The blueprint's sp-compliance-reporting fail point is the leverage. The organisation measures visit completion and cannot see that 38% of visits were delivered by an unfamiliar worker. A continuity KPI, reported beside coverage, changes what the scheduler is optimising at 6am.

Use case 2 — The family as a first-class actor. The stakeholder map shows the family carer with high interest and no formal channel; the blueprint shows bs-no-family-notify as a fail point with no owning process. A notification workflow is a small build with a disproportionate effect on two of six journey stages.

Use case 3 — Care knowledge at the door. fs-worker-inbound and bs-run-sheet-generation are the same failure seen from two lanes. The care plan already holds how the client likes things done; the run sheet simply does not carry it.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI

The method manifest is the SOP, in the sense article 29 argues for: context, inputs, steps by role, output schema, acceptance criteria, evidence rules, review gate, ownership. What article 29 specified in prose, this harness executes.

Two properties matter in practice:

  • Acceptance rules must be machine-checkable or the manifest is rejected at load time. Prose criteria cannot be expressed. A method whose quality bar cannot be evaluated does not enter the registry.
  • Provenance is mandatory. Every method cites the book chapter and article it derives from, and the model surfaces those citations unprompted when it reports.

10 · Lessons learned

Lessons learned & pitfalls

A resolvable citation is not a correct citation. An automated check confirmed all 42 method citations pointed at real files, and passed. It could not detect that one method cited article 18 (value propositions) where it meant article 12 (ecosystem mapping) — the word "value" in the method name had pulled it to the wrong place. Only a test that knew what article 12 is about caught it.

Tests that pass are not a system that works. The tool layer had 108 passing tests and a clean build while failing to typecheck, because the test runner does not typecheck and the bundler does not run the full compiler. Three green signals, none of them looking at the thing that was wrong.

The gate has to be able to say no to you, not just to the model. The refusal in §4.5 is only valuable because it happened against an explicit instruction from the person holding the keyboard.

The narrative is not the audit. The first edition of this article reported a clean fact-check and a refusal that persisted nothing. Re-running the fact-check against the real workspace (§13) showed a failing row the narrative had missed, and the current engine persists refused writes as drafts. The receipts corrected the story — which is the point of keeping them.

Fictional clients are fine; fictional evidence is not. The quotes here are authored, and the article says so. What the harness guarantees is narrower and more useful: that every claim in every artifact traces to a record someone can open.

Deterministic fact-check

The platform's fact-check pipeline (documented in platform/README.md) is the same machine, applied to the harness run itself. Evidence is content-addressed; artifact writes are schema-checked and reference-checked; acceptance rules are evaluated by code; the critic's verdict is a stored record; and sd_factcheck — the sd_* tool that ships in @asd/runtime rather than the web profile — replays the whole chain on demand, per artifact, without the model involved.

The bounded prior Wattle Grove workspace replay recorded while writing this edition is not the clean sweep the first edition claimed — it is a prior-workflow receipt (trimmed for print; […] marks the cut), not current DSH host evidence:

Exit code 1. Read the row carefully, because it is the deterministic layer earning its keep:

  • What passed. Every artifact passes integrity — no dangling evidence references — and all but the journey map pass acceptance. The evidence chain this article describes is machine-verified, not narrated.
  • What failed. The journey map's nodes record their stage under the field name the run used at write time; the acceptance rule replayed by the workflow engine requires a non-empty "phase" field, and the replay flags all six nodes. The four artifacts predate the status-stamping write path — they carry no status, check, or review fields, so the replay reads them as drafts and infers their methods (methodResolution: "inferred"). The deterministic replay caught schema drift the narrative never saw, and this excerpt replaces the first edition's invented clean report.

Exit code 0. The review record itself is a final artifact on the board — the audit trail is part of the deliverable.

Two properties make this check meaningful rather than theatrical:

  • It is deterministic. The same inputs produce the same verdict in the same order — no model, no temperature, no judgement call in the loop. The check is replayable in CI on every commit to the workspace, which is exactly what article 29 argued SOPs-as-code should enable.
  • It checks what the model could not. Reference integrity and acceptance rules are guaranteed by construction on the current write path — the gate refuses to accept anything that would fail. The residual failure modes are the ones above: schema drift between write time and replay time, and the semantic one — a citation that resolves to the wrong place. That is what §10's lesson is about, and it is why the critic sits on the write path as a role, not as a regex.

The distinction matters for the series: evidence-gated-blueprint, journey-from-transcripts and stakeholder-mapping are the methods that produced these artifacts, and each one carries reviewGate: critic in its manifest — visible in the probe row above as gate: critic once the method is recorded on the artifact. The deterministic layer guarantees the evidence, and the critic layer defends the meaning.
