40 — Copperline 2000 Retrospective: One Brief, Three Agent Stacks

One brief, three agent stacks, one question: when you change everything about how a multi-agent engagement is run — the collaboration surface, the dispatch mechanism, the gates, the audit trail — how much of the deliverable actually moves? This is the bridge piece of the Copperline 2000 series. Article 41 ran the engagement as a BUZZ channel with role runtimes replying under their own identities; article 42 ran it from the terminal with four coding-agent CLIs and our OMP plugin library; article 43 ran it inside DeepSeek Harness, where the workflow itself shipped as plugins. Here the three lanes sit in one frame, compared honestly, defects included.

00 · Provenance

Provenance

Copperline Chicken — 2,000 charcoal-grill stores, 41,300 crew, a support centre in Port Meridian — is a fictional composite. Every person, quote, figure, store and outcome in this series is synthetic. No real company, store, or person is described. That boundary is stated first in all fourteen artifacts, and restated here because a retrospective multiplies the surfaces a reader might mistake for evidence.

The second boundary concerns the lanes. Lane 1's BUZZ transcript is a simulated channel log following real relay conventions; lane 2's terminal sessions are simulated run logs following each CLI's real command conventions; lane 3's harness session is simulated, its five UI panels illustrative renderings, not captures. Not simulated: the project canon every lane read, and the fourteen artifacts all three lanes share, byte for byte. This article adds no new artifacts.

Third, a note about numbers. The engagement-level facts — store counts, metric baselines, note bands, gate timestamps, critic verdicts — are canon-bound and checked in section 08. The comparative estimates (owner hours, setup effort) are synthetic judgments, marked as estimates everywhere they appear: no invented number gets to wear the costume of a measured one.

01 · Why a retrospective

Why a retrospective

The three lanes were published back to back, each written from inside its own substrate: article 41 argues the channel case, article 42 the terminal case, article 43 the harness case. A reader could finish all three and still not know what to do on Monday. A retrospective exists to fix that — to move from three advocacy documents to one decision document.

The method is the standard retrospective discipline, and this replay is unusually clean: all three lanes consumed the same canon and produced the same fourteen artifacts, so variance between them isolates almost perfectly to the substrate — a control most retrospectives never get. Every difference in coordination, gating, audit and effort is attributable to the stack, not the work.

One framing note. The lanes are not three competing recommendations; they are three points on a graph whose axes are visibility, reproducibility and enforcement. Making those axes explicit turns the choice conditional — pick a lane for a reason, not a tool for a loyalty.

02 · Three lanes

The three lanes at a glance

One six-week engagement, one brief, one canon, one 14-artifact contract — run across three substrates:

Dimension Lane 1 — BUZZ channel (41) Lane 2 — terminal CLIs (42) Lane 3 — DSH plugins (43)
Coordination One private channel, sd/copperline-2000 on the tm relay; threaded, visible to every member Five owner-typed terminal sessions; no conversation surface at all Six harness sessions routed to role agents; sessions list for cross-visibility
Dispatch Owner-posted scoped mentions (@role + bounded instruction + named artifact target) Shell invocations per artifact; AGY pipelines chain artifact-to-artifact dependencies asd-roles plugin routes @role dispatch inside sessions
Gates Human receipts posted in-channel, recorded in the store; runtimes trusted not to advance phases Manual — the owner reads output and types the next command; shell history is the only trace gatekeeper plugin holds the phase lock; no agent path can advance a phase
Audit trail Relay event IDs, channel receipts, Canvas index over durable records Run logs, git diff on artifacts, exit codes, sealed ledger hash Write-gates in code; ledger, reviews and receipts are enforceable records
Artifact fidelity 14/14 against the template catalog; blueprint PASS after 2 revisions Identical files — same 14 artifacts, same note bands, verified by a fail-closed exit-code gate Identical files; note-ID drift rejected at write time before review
Best for Shared visibility, multi-party gating, sponsor-watchable work Batch-shaped work, reproducible pipelines, CI and quarterly re-baselines Repeat engagement shapes, enforceable workflow, versioned governance

Read column by column and the table says something quietly radical: the deliverable row barely changes. Coordination, dispatch, gates and audit are substrate decisions; the artifact contract is not. That observation is the spine of everything below.

03 · What held

What was identical

Holding the deliverable constant was the design of the exercise, and it held. Four things were identical across all three lanes:

  1. The canon. All three lanes read the same canon.md first — the 8.1 KB single source of truth fixing the client (2,000 stores, 41,300 crew, 2,140 salaried managers), the five store formats (68 flagship · 1,214 standard · 389 food-court · 251 drive-thru express · 78 travel-hub kiosk), the seven named systems from CopperPOS v7 to Route56, and the metric baselines every artifact reports against. Canon over artifact, artifact over chat, in every lane.
  2. The note ledger. One continuous sequence, N001–N560, 560 numbered notes, never renumbered. The blueprint owns N001–N220; research owns N221–N320; synthesis and delivery artifacts hold N321–N500; the brief N501–N540; the channel log N541–N560. A citation made in week 4 resolves identically in all three lanes at delivery.
  3. The 14 artifacts. Same files, same sizes, same kinds: brief (01), research pack (03/04), stakeholder map (05), ecosystem map (06), insight register (07), persona set (09), journey map (11), the full-operation service blueprint (12), analysis report (28+32), ten proposals (31), 16-slide deck (33), channel transcript (26). Lanes 2 and 3 linked rather than forked — the DSH lane's version control is the session log, not a second artifact set.
  4. The critic record. 13 verdicts, 3 revision verdicts carrying 4 defect rows, 0 unresolved at close (N560). The same two blueprint defects — the unnamed crew→courier handover lane and the uncited 246s drive-thru figure — surfaced in lanes 1 and 3 verbatim, and lane 2's critique pass caught two visual-contract defects in the same artifact. Findings replicated across substrates; the substrate did not steer the work.

Underneath, the research base is equally shared: 8 flagship interviews (412 minutes, 148 verbatims), surveys of 412 managers and 1,893 crew, five channel extracts on one 90-day window — 214 evidence IDs (62 interview + 58 survey + 94 channel). The MCP surface was one surface throughout: six ASD bundles, 29 sd_* tools, 225 resources, 10 prompts — consumed by channel runtimes, by CLIs from the shell, and by the harness through a bridge plugin.

04 · What differed

What differed

Everything else moved. The honest deltas, with estimates marked synthetic:

Reproducibility Re-run means re-staging a channel Re-run means agy run on a revised canon; pipeline wall clock 41 min (illustrative) Re-run means booting the profile; workflow versioned next to the code
Stakeholder visibility High — work is watchable as it happens None — stakeholders see artifacts, not process A window — live artifact preview inside the web UI

Two observations. First, defect character followed the substrate: the channel lane surfaced evidence-discipline defects, the terminal lane surfaced visual-contract defects the generators were blind to, and the harness lane moved enforcement left of review entirely. Second, no lane dominated the effort columns — setup cost ran inversely to per-run owner time, which is the actual economic trade the three stacks offer.

One drift to disclose rather than smooth over: the lane-2 session log's band table records the analysis report at N421–N460 and proposals at N461–N500, while the artifact set's own ledger carries analysis at N421–N455 and proposals at N456–N490, the deck owning N491–N500. Both close the same 560-note ledger with zero collisions. Per the canon rule — artifact over chat — the artifact set is authoritative, and the disagreement is itself evidence that shell history is not a receipt.

05 · Decision guide

Choosing a lane

The decision guide that falls out of the three runs, expressed as conditions rather than preferences:

  • Run the channel lane (41) when the engagement has an audience — a sponsor like Priya Nair and functional heads who need to watch the thinking, not just receive the deck — or when gating is multi-party and receipts need to be posts, not files. Visibility is the product.
  • Run the terminal lane (42) when the work is batch-shaped, already specified, or destined for repetition — the quarterly re-baseline in proposal 10 is exactly this: nobody needs to watch, everybody needs it identical.
  • Run the harness lane (43) when you will run the same engagement shape more than once and want the workflow itself — roles, evidence rules, renderers, gates — versioned and enforced rather than remembered. Pay the setup once, compound it every rerun.

Three conditions cut across the choice. If gates must be unforgeable, the harness's phase lock is the only in-code guarantee; the channel's receipts are strong but conventional, the terminal's manual. If CI matters, only the terminal lane hangs off a nightly job without ceremony. If the engagement is a one-off with two people involved, the channel wins on setup alone — building a plugin suite for a single run is buying a factory to bake one loaf.

And the honest anti-guide: if you cannot say which of visibility, reproducibility or enforcement you need, you are not ready to choose — you will judge the lane you picked by the axis you did not need.

06 · What broke

What broke in each lane

Each lane earned its lessons by failing in a characteristic way.

Lane 1 — the channel. The simulated transcript's shorthand drifted from the artifact set at least four times — 26 vs 24 insights, 18 vs 16 slides, 5 vs 6 personas, 4 vs 2 journeys — and every drift was resolved by the artifacts-win rule, not by the channel's own memory. Mention scoping was real coordination overhead: a bounded instruction plus a named artifact target every time, or replies arrive as essays. And the surveys' weighting defect (flagship over-represented 7.3% vs 3.4% of the store base, N548) survived to critic review — the channel catches drift at the gate, not at the write.

Lane 2 — the terminal. The gates. Manual session boundaries left the owner's phase decisions in shell history — if you need gated governance, this lane quietly doesn't have it unless you script receipts. The two blueprint defects were visual-contract failures (a wait node missing its hatched-gray marker; the Route56 handoff drawn solid where canon §6.3 requires dashed), both proving the lane's own rule: generators do not notice this class of error in their own output. Sequential pipelines also under-used the hardware — steps ran concurrent only where note bands allowed.

Lane 3 — the harness. The substrate churned: two plugin updates mid-lane changed registry APIs, because DSH is a developer preview with announced compatibility-breaking changes — a plugin suite is a maintenance commitment, not an install. Permission prompts interrupted long research dispatches badly enough that approval fatigue became a failure mode, resolved only by pre-authorizing read-only tool classes. An early draft renumbered two adjacent blueprint annotations and was rejected at write time. And the critic carried no per-role memory between sessions.

The pattern across lanes: nothing broke in the artifacts. Everything that broke, broke in the coordination layer — the retrospective's summary finding about where the risk actually lives.

07 · The confession

The numbering-gap confession

A numbering confession, because a series that audits note IDs should audit its own sequence. The Copperline series was planned as three lanes published as articles 41, 42 and 43 — and the comparative piece, the one that should logically sit before all three, was never assigned a number. Article 40 was simply skipped: the gap between 39 (the live BUZZ tutorial) and 41 (the first Copperline lane) stayed empty, and the site's own test suite grew a comment explaining that "deliberate numbering gaps (e.g. the skipped 40 between 39 and 41)" are valid archive structure. The infrastructure politely documented the omission.

This slot is the natural occupant. A retrospective is written last and read as the bridge — it presumes all three lanes exist, and exists to connect them — so it belongs exactly where the gap was: after the tutorial that proved the pattern live, before the lane that scaled it. Nothing was renumbered to make room. Articles keep their published identities, the same way note IDs do, and the gap closes the only way a never-renumber sequence can: by filling the empty slot, not by shifting the filled ones.

The lesson generalizes: in a series, a ledger or a note sequence, gaps you leave "temporarily" become structure, and others build around them. Fill them deliberately or document them loudly.

09 · Lessons learned

Lessons

  • The contract is the practice; the substrate is a setting. Change the channel for a shell for a harness and the deliverable set does not move, because the canon, the note bands and the template kinds did the load-bearing. Choose substrates for tempo, not identity.
  • Defect classes migrate with enforcement point. At the gate (lane 1), evidence gaps; after generation (lane 2), visual-contract failures; at write time (lane 3), numbering drift. Every step left you move enforcement, you buy earlier, cheaper catches — and a new class of false alarm to tune out.
  • Gates are only as strong as their weakest receipt. A channel receipt is a post; a terminal gate is shell history; a plugin phase lock is code. Match the receipt's durability to the governance you promised the sponsor.
  • Estimates must wear labels. A retrospective's tempting failure mode is letting hours and judgments impersonate ledger numbers. This article's estimates are marked synthetic on every appearance — the fact-check refuses them, by design.
  • Chat is not the record — in any lane. The channel transcript drifted from the artifacts; the terminal log's band table disagreed with the ledger; the harness session log is itself a reconstruction. The artifact set is where the engagement is true.
  • Setup cost and run cost trade inversely. The channel was cheapest to start and heaviest to run; the harness the reverse; the terminal cheapest to rerun. Price the reruns before you price the pilot.
  • Variance between lanes is a quality signal. When the same defects surface under three substrates, the substrate is not steering the work — and that replication is the strongest evidence the method survives its tools.
