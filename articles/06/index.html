Research Ops at Scale: 200 Interviews, One Agent Team

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Calder Community Bank is a fictional composite client, assembled from patterns observed across several European regional and cooperative banks; no real bank, customer, or employee is depicted. All names, figures, datasets, and quotes are illustrative and anonymized; participant quotes are fictional reconstructions written to the register of real fieldwork, not transcriptions of real people. The article shares knowledge and process — the research operations architecture, SOPs, prompts, and review gates — never client specifics. The engagement ran AI-first, with the human designer retaining consent, rapport, framing, and every participant-facing decision.

1. Executive summary

Calder Community Bank, a fictional regional bank with 120 branches and 1.1 million customers, commissioned a service reset and faced the discovery problem from hell: four service domains (SME onboarding, branch service, digital banking, complaints), twelve distinct customer situations, and a board burned twice by research it did not trust. A traditional program at the required depth — 200 semi-structured interviews, 45 frontline shadow days, 800 diary entries — was quoted by an agency at 16 weeks and €285,000, and that quote silently assumed the corpus would be cut to 40 interviews. Instead, one lead service designer with an AI agent team ran the full corpus in 10 weeks for €94,000: an OpenCode ingestion and pseudonymization pipeline, a three-role Claude Code research team (researcher, synthesizer, librarian) producing per-interview summaries and a 4,826-item evidence register, a Codex adversarial synthesis pass, and GitHub as research-ops system of record — one issue per interview, PR gates on evidence quality, and CI blocking any personal data from the evidence store. First insights landed in week 4; human–LLM agreement on a 20-interview double-coding study reached kappa 0.78; every one of the 12 insight statements traces to verbatim quotes with character offsets into pseudonymized transcripts.

What you will learn:

  • How to structure research operations so an agent team can process 200 interviews without a single quote being fabricated, leaked, or orphaned from its evidence.
  • How to encode the interview-to-insight SOP — extraction with character-offset verification, a versioned codebook, affinity clustering, adversarial synthesis — as executable agent work with human gates.
  • How to use GitHub as a research ops platform: issue-per-interview tracking, consent state machines, PR review gates, and CI that treats PII as a build failure.

2. The brief

Client portrait

Calder Community Bank is a fictional regional bank serving a single eurozone region: 120 branches across three sub-regions, 1.1 million customers (880,000 retail, 220,000 micro and small-business), roughly 2,400 employees, and a mutual-adjacent ownership culture that prizes its local reputation. Digital maturity is mid-table: 61% of customers are monthly active on the app or web, but the core banking platform dates from 2009, branch staffing follows a rota written in 2017, and the complaints function reports into operations rather than to anyone who owns a journey. Two conventional research programs in the previous four years had produced decks that branch and digital leadership disputed within a month, because nobody could trace a claim back to a customer.

The challenge

The trigger was strategic: a new retail banking director inherited an NPS of +4 flat for three years, complaint volume of 18,400 cases per year rising 9% annually, SME onboarding that dashboards claimed took 8 days but every relationship manager privately called "three weeks on a good day," and a branch cost review putting 22 rural branches under closure consideration. Her diagnosis was blunt: "We do not have a service problem we can see. We have fifty service problems we argue about anonymously." She commissioned a discovery research program across four domains — SME onboarding, branch service, digital banking, and complaints — with one hard requirement: every insight must be traceable to evidence the organization can inspect.

The research problem was scale and trust simultaneously. Adequate segment spread (SME owners and finance managers, branch-loyal seniors, digital-first commuters, recent complainants, frontline advisors, complaints handlers) demanded roughly 200 interviews — five times what the budget line assumed. And Grönroos's warning applied: perceived quality is a judgment of experienced service against expected service (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md), so the research had to capture expectation–experience gaps in customers' own words, not satisfaction scores the bank already had.

Success criteria (agreed with the client)

Constraints

  • Budget: €100,000 for the research phase, all-in, including participant incentives.
  • Timeline: insights had to land before the branch network decision in week 14.
  • Regulation: GDPR explicit consent for recording; interviews in Dutch and French; the DPO required that no raw audio or direct identifiers leave the bank-controlled environment; 30-day withdrawal right.
  • Works council: shadowing required agreement that observation notes would never be used in performance management.
  • Trust history: two prior research decks had been publicly disputed by division heads; any new program would be audited by skeptics.

Why service design

The bank's symptoms — customers re-sending documents, complaints dying in handoffs, queues blamed on demand — were cross-silo phenomena no single department's data could explain. Service design research exists for exactly this: generative, ecology-wide inquiry across customers, frontline, and backstage, because contradictions between those accounts are where insights live (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md). The methods were standard — semi-structured interviews, shadowing, diary studies, affinity synthesis (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md; 18-universal-methods-of-design/02-foundational-research-methods.md) — the innovation was operational: running them at five times the usual corpus without diluting rigor, by treating research operations itself as a service staffed by an agent team (See: ../01/README.md).

3. Approach: the AI-first research operations architecture

The program ran as a five-stage pipeline under Kumar's Mode 2 (Know Context) and Mode 3 (Know People), converging in Mode 4 (Frame Insights) (See: 17-101-design-methods/03-mode-2-know-context.md; 04-mode-3-know-people.md; 05-mode-4-frame-insights.md). The operating rule: humans do everything participant-facing; agents do everything transcript-facing; GitHub arbitrates the boundary.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research design, ethics, and sampling (week 1)

Goal. A research plan the DPO, works council, and four domain owners could all sign — and a sampling plan that made 200 interviews mean something.

Method & SOP. The Research Plan Canvas (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md) plus a stratified sampling plan built as Kumar's Mode 3 prescribes: include extreme users, not just the statistical middle (See: 17-101-design-methods/04-mode-3-know-people.md). The 200 interviews stratified across four domains and their extremes: SME onboarding (50: owners, finance managers, relationship managers, including 8 who abandoned onboarding and 6 declined for credit), branch service (60: branch-loyal seniors, rural customers near the 22 at-risk branches, branch advisors), digital (50: digital-first commuters, app abandoners, branch-free customers), complaints (40: recent complainants by outcome, complaints handlers).

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the plan, guides, and consent pack as version-controlled documents in a fresh repository (calder-research/), from a prompt that fixed the constraints as acceptance criteria:

Draft the research plan for Project Compass. Hard constraints: 200 interviews
across four domains (SME 50, branch 60, digital 50, complaints 40); 45 shadow
days; 40 diarists x 4 weeks; GDPR explicit consent, 30-day withdrawal, Dutch
and French fieldwork; works-council clause that observation notes are never
used in performance management; every insight must be evidence-traceable.
Output: research/PLAN.md following the Research Plan Canvas fields, plus
research/sampling-plan.md with quotas per stratum and recruitment fallout
assumptions (40% response, 77% consent, 84% completion).

The consent pack went through the bank's DPO as PR #3 — the first review gate of the engagement — which requested two changes (explicit 18-month retention period; incentive disclosure on the invitation letter) applied before any recruitment. The codebook started at v1 with 41 deductive codes in 9 families (access, effort, emotion, fees, handoffs, trust, staff capability, status visibility, life events), seeded from the complaint taxonomy and the expectation–experience quality framing (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md), with an explicit rule that inductive codes would be added only at fixed versioning points — v2 at interview 60, v3 at interview 140 — so the scheme could not drift mid-corpus.

Human designer judgment. The stratum quotas, the decision to oversample abandonment and decline cases (structurally invisible to the bank's dashboards), and every ethics trade-off. The designer also insisted on episode-based interviewing — "walk me through the last time you opened the business account" — over attitude surveying, on the say-versus-do principle (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md).

Artifacts produced. Research plan and interview guide templates (A6), sampling plan, consent and data-flow pack (below), codebook v1.

The load-bearing decision: the identity mapping vault (participant code to real identity) lived only in the bank's own restricted storage, held by the client coordinator. The repository and every agent context saw pseudonyms only — INT-041, P-041a, branch codes, segment labels. Agents never processed raw audio and never saw a real name.

4.2 Fieldwork operations: the scheduling and consent pipeline (weeks 1–7)

Goal. Move 640 invitations to 200 completed interviews without losing a consent state, a reschedule, or a withdrawal request.

Method & SOP. Research operations as a state machine. Every interview was a GitHub issue from screener to deletion-eligibility date, with lifecycle screened → invited → consented → scheduled → completed → processed → verified → closed (with withdrawn reachable from any state). The issue template carried the consent checklist, stratum quota tags, language, incentive status, and artifact links; a Projects board gave the client coordinator the same operational picture as the designer — no side spreadsheets.

AI execution. The designer ran fieldwork; agents ran the logistics data. Each evening an OpenCode job reconciled the tracker: new screener responses became issues, arriving consent PDFs were checked off, and quota burn-down per stratum was recomputed and posted as a board comment:

opencode run --agent build "Read research/ops/screener-responses.csv and the
GitHub project board state (gh api). Create one issue per new respondent using
issue templates/interview.md with stratum labels. Flag strata within 3 seats
of quota. Post a quota burn-down table as a comment on issue #2. Never store
email addresses in issue bodies; reference the vault ID only."

Waves were planned against quota gaps the board exposed: week 3 showed branch-loyal seniors at 40% of quota while digital-first was full, so wave 3 invitations were re-weighted toward branch referrals and the at-risk rural clusters. 17 no-shows were rebooked within 72 hours because the board surfaced them the same evening. Three participants withdrew after their interviews; each withdrawn label triggered a deletion SOP — transcript, derived evidence items, and summary removed from the register in the same PR — which is how the analyzed corpus settled at exactly 200.

Human designer judgment. All interviewing (the designer conducted 132 interviews; two trained client researchers conducted 68 under the same guide), all shadow days, all rapport, and the call to accept a slightly under-quota stratum (credit-declined SMEs, 6 of 8) rather than pressure reluctant participants.

Artifacts produced. 200 interview records, 45 shadow logs, 800 diary entries, a complete consent audit trail, and the quota burn-down history that made coverage defensible at the readout.

4.3 Per-interview processing: ingestion, pseudonymization, QA (weeks 2–8, rolling)

Goal. Every interview converted, within 36 hours of fieldwork, into a structured summary plus a set of verified evidence items — at 200-interview scale without quality collapse.

Method & SOP. Kumar's Structured User Observations Database: every observation as an atomic record, tagged by participant, POEMS category, human factor, and journey phase, in a searchable repository (See: 17-101-design-methods/04-mode-3-know-people.md) — plus one non-negotiable addition: no evidence item exists unless its quote verifies against the transcript. This is the interview-to-insight SOP's core guardrail, developed in Section 7.

AI execution. Three pipeline stages, each with the tool where it was strongest:

  1. Ingestion (OpenCode, agent mode). Nightly batch: transcribe and diarize new audio, deduplicate (one diarist's uploads had synced twice), then pseudonymize — person names, business names, account numbers, towns under 5,000 population replaced with stable codes, the mapping written only to the client vault. A PII lint pass scanned output for residual identifiers; failures quarantined the file and opened an issue. Two genuine leaks were caught: a participant's business name surviving inside a quoted phrase (INT-088), and a branch manager's first name in a shadow log. Both were fixed before any synthesis agent touched the files.

  2. Extraction (Claude Code researcher agent). One invocation per interview produced the structured summary (context, episodes, emotions, workarounds, POEMS tags) plus candidate evidence items, each carrying quote, transcript_start, transcript_end character offsets, journey phase, human factor, and provisional codes:

You are the researcher agent for Project Compass. Read
transcripts/pseudo/INT-041.txt. Produce: (1) a structured summary using the
schema in sop/interview-summary.md; (2) evidence items as JSONL per
sop/evidence-schema.json. Rules: every quote MUST be copied character-exact
from the transcript with start and end offsets; never paraphrase into the
quote field; tag each item with journey_phase, human_factor, and up to 3
codebook v3 codes; if a passage is ambiguous, set confidence: low rather than
interpreting. Output to evidence/candidates/INT-041.jsonl.
  1. Verification (Claude Code, second pass). A separate agent re-checked every candidate against the transcript span — a mechanical substring assertion plus a semantic check that the span supports the tag. Across the corpus, 5,214 candidate items were extracted; 388 (7.4%) failed verification — mostly offset drift, 41 outright fabricated or merged quotes — and were re-extracted or dropped. The accepted 4,826 evidence items (3,962 interviews, 512 diaries, 352 shadow logs) constitute the evidence register, schema in A6.

Human designer judgment. The designer spot-read one full interview summary per day against the transcript (a 10% audit of the audit) and reviewed every confidence: low item weekly. The 36-hour SLA was a human decision too: fast enough that the designer still remembered the interview when reading its summary.

Artifacts produced. 200 structured summaries, the evidence register (4,826 items), pseudonymization and PII-quarantine logs, and the interview register dataset (A7).

4.4 Synthesis at scale: register, codebook, affinity (weeks 4–9, rolling)

Goal. Climb the ladder from data to themes to insights (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md) without the synthesis decay that killed the bank's previous research: claims nobody could trace.

Method & SOP. Bottom-up affinity clustering with active-sentence theme names (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md), executed as rolling synthesis: from week 4, each Friday the synthesizer agent re-clustered the growing register, so themes stabilized progressively instead of arriving in one big-bang workshop. The human gate was a half-day synthesis wall session in week 8 with six client stakeholders, where clusters were merged, renamed, and killed by hand.

AI execution. Two Claude Code subagents with separated roles. The synthesizer proposed clusterings: it read the register in domain batches, grouped items by behavioral similarity (not topic keywords), and wrote cluster cards with draft active-sentence names and member item IDs. The librarian enforced register hygiene: near-duplicate detection across batches, theme-membership stability between weekly runs (theme IDs are join keys — see A7), and rebuilding the digital twin of the physical wall after the stakeholder session so human merges propagated to the data. The pipeline as executed:

flowchart TB T["233 hours audio + 800 diary entries + 45 shadow logs"] --> TR["Transcribe and diarize"] TR --> PS["Pseudonymize - identifiers to codes"] PS --> EX["Extract - candidate evidence items with char offsets"] EX --> VF["Verify - quote must match transcript span exactly"] VF -->|accept 4826| ER["Evidence register"] VF -->|reject 388| RB["Re-extract or drop - logged"] ER --> CB["Codebook v3 application - 64 codes"] CB --> CL["Affinity clustering - 96 clusters"] CL --> TH["Theme consolidation - 22 themes"] TH --> HG{"Human gate - synthesis wall review"} HG -->|re-cluster| CL HG --> IN["12 insight statements"] IN --> CX["Codex adversarial check"] CX --> FN["Validated insight register + journey drafts"]

Convergence: 96 clusters → 22 themes → 12 insight statements in the three-part syntax — observed behavior, underlying tension, design implication (See: 17-101-design-methods/05-mode-4-frame-insights.md). The acid test was applied literally: two candidates that changed no design decision ("customers value friendliness") were demoted to findings.

Human designer judgment. Theme naming is framing, and framing is political: the designer renamed the agent's "Branch capacity shortfall" to "Branch queues are a rostering failure, not a demand problem" because the evidence pointed at the 2017 rota, and that naming determined which executive owned the fix. The designer chaired the wall session and made every merge/kill call.

Artifacts produced. The 22-theme register with prevalence counts, 12 insight cards, and the theme-frequency and prevalence-versus-impact charts (A8).

4.5 Validation: reliability, adversarial review, journey drafting (weeks 8–10)

Goal. Prove the synthesis to the skeptics before asking the board to act on it.

Method & SOP. Three independent validation moves. First, an inter-rater reliability study: 20 interviews (10% of corpus, stratified) double-coded blind — once by the pipeline, once by a senior human researcher unconnected to the engagement. Second, triangulation accounting: every theme cites its method mix, honoring the rule that valid findings combine observation, inquiry, and secondary data (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md). Third, an adversarial synthesis pass by Codex as an independent model family (See: ../01/README.md).

AI execution. The IRR comparison was computed by an OpenCode script from the two coding exports; no human eyeballed agreement. Result: code-level agreement 79% (kappa 0.71); theme-level agreement 86% (kappa 0.78) — above the 0.70 gate. Cohen's kappa was used instead of simple percentage agreement because both coders were drawing from the same bounded codebook, so some overlap would happen by chance; the sponsor needed a reliability statistic that discounted that chance agreement before trusting the pipeline. Disagreements clustered where they should: the human coded fees where the pipeline coded status visibility in 11 cases, and review showed both were defensible readings of ambiguous passages; the codebook gained a disambiguation note rather than a forced winner.

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read research/evidence-register.jsonl and
synthesis/themes-v3.md without reading synthesis/insight-cards.md. Independently
propose themes from the register. Then read the 12 insight cards and attack
them: for each, (1) verify every cited quote exists at its stated offsets,
(2) argue the strongest alternative explanation, (3) flag any insight whose
implication is not entailed by its evidence. Output a verdict per card."

Codex's independent theme list overlapped 19 of the 22 pipeline themes; it killed one insight card (a "digital trust deficit" rested on 4 items from a single stratum) and forced evidence upgrades on two others. All 12 surviving insights cite at least 8 evidence items from at least 2 methods and 2 participant strata. Finally, Claude Code drafted current-state journey maps from the tagged evidence — phases by customer goal, pains separated from opportunities (See: 17-101-design-methods/05-mode-4-frame-insights.md) — with the SME draft (A1) seeding the ideation engagement that followed (See: ../16/README.md).

Human designer judgment. The designer accepted the killed card without defending it, presented the kappa results at the readout unprompted (showing the audit before being audited), and held the line that journey phases come from customer goals, not the bank's process map.

Artifacts produced. The IRR study report, the Codex verdict log, 12 validated insight cards, and two current-state journey drafts.

6. Use cases

6.1 Use case A — The SME onboarding sprint that fed the prototype

Scenario. Weeks 2–4 delivered the first 22 SME interviews, 10 credit-operations shadow days, and 212 diary entries. Rolling synthesis meant the SME cluster reached stability in week 4 — six weeks before the full program closed.

What was produced. The SME journey draft (A1), themes T01/T02/T10, and insights INS-01 and INS-02, with the headline reframing: dashboards said onboarding took 8 days; the evidence showed a median of 19, with 68% of applicants re-requested for documents (median 2.6 re-requests per affected applicant). The gap between believed and experienced service — Grönroos's expectation–experience gap, measured in the bank's own house (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/02-perceived-service-quality.md) — became the ideation brief's spine.

The AI working session. The synthesizer produced the SME cluster set on a Friday; the designer reviewed Monday; the insight cards passed verification Tuesday (every quote re-checked against offsets) and shipped to the ideation team as PR #87 with a machine-readable evidence bundle:

Assemble the SME evidence bundle for handoff. Input: evidence register +
themes T01 T02 T10 + insight cards INS-01 INS-02. Output:
handoff/sme-bundle/ with (1) insight cards, (2) the 25 strongest quotes with
offsets and segment tags, (3) journey draft mermaid, (4) open questions the
evidence does not answer. Every quote must pass the offset check before commit.

Outcome. The ideation engagement started from validated evidence rather than a briefing deck; its winning concept — a status-heartbeat onboarding tracker with document receipt confirmation — cites INS-01 and INS-02 directly (See: ../16/README.md). First SME interview to ideation-ready evidence: 17 days.

6.2 Use case B — Complaint interviews catch a systemic rostering failure

Scenario. The complaints stratum was designed to study complaint handling. The evidence had a different idea: 29 of the 40 complaint interviews mentioned branch wait times unprompted — queue complaints arriving through the complaints channel because there was nowhere else to put them. Cross-joining with the branch stratum (theme T03, 61 interviews across 31 branches) and 15 complaint-centre shadow days produced INS-03: the queues were not demand; the 2017 rota allocated full staffing to mornings while footfall had shifted to the 11:00–14:00 window.

The AI working session. The pattern was found by the librarian, not a human: its cross-theme dedup pass flagged that T03 and T06 items shared participant and branch codes at 4 times the base rate, and opened a synthesis note proposing the join. The designer then commissioned triangulation — an OpenCode job pulled queue telemetry for 14 instrumented branches and tested the roster hypothesis:

opencode run --agent build "Read branch-telemetry/queue-samples.csv. Compute
median wait by 30-minute slot and weekday, split by branch. Compare against
roster/rota-2017.csv staffing levels. Output: a table and a short memo testing
the hypothesis that peak waits coincide with roster troughs, 11:00-14:00 Tue-Thu."

Telemetry agreed with testimony: median midday waits of 23 minutes against 7 off-peak, concentrated Tuesday to Thursday, precisely where the rota ran two advisors short.

Outcome. The bank ran a roster redesign pilot in 6 branches (projected 38% wait reduction at readout), and — the detail the board repeated — complaints coded as "branch wait" stopped being treated as a complaint-handling problem at all. One evidence join, two departments re-aimed: the ecology-wide dividend of researching across the customer–frontline boundary (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md).

6.3 Use case C — AI versus human synthesis, A/B on 20 interviews

Scenario. The client's research manager asked the question every client asks: would a human team find the same things? Rather than argue, we ran the comparison as a study. The 20 IRR interviews were synthesized independently by (a) the pipeline and (b) two senior human researchers blind to its output, both working from the same pseudonymized transcripts and codebook v3.

What was compared. Theme recall, precision, insight ranking, and effort.

Measure (illustrative) Human team Agent pipeline
Themes proposed 19 23
Themes validated on joint review 19 19 (2 of the 4 extras adopted; 2 rejected as over-splits)
Recall against the joint gold set (21 themes) 19/21 (90%) 19/21 (90%), rising to 21/21 with adopted extras
Insight ranking correlation (Spearman) — 0.81 vs human ranking
Code-level agreement between the two kappa 0.71 —
Effort 34 researcher-hours 6.5 hours including human verification
Cost at €95/h loaded €3,230 €620

Outcome. The two approaches converged on the same core structure, which de-risked the method politically more than any methodology memo could have. The pipeline's failure mode was over-splitting (two clusters where humans saw one with a modifier); the humans' failure mode was missing two low-prevalence themes the pipeline caught via exhaustive register coverage (bereavement handling; closure anxiety among rural customers). The synthesis rule was updated accordingly: agents exhaust, humans consolidate, Codex attacks, and nobody synthesizes alone.

7. SOPs for AI — how research SOPs are best used by agents

The meta-SOP

The interview-to-insight SOP was encoded as: method → step list → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate. Five stages, each gated so the next cannot bypass a failure:

sop: interview-to-insight
version: 3.1
stages:
  - id: ingest
    owner: opencode-pipeline
    steps: [transcribe, diarize, dedup, pseudonymize]
    output_schema: transcripts/pseudo/INT-NNN.txt
    acceptance: [pii_lint_green, diarization_speakers_plausible]
    gate: quarantine_on_failure
  - id: extract
    owner: claude-researcher-agent
    steps: [structured_summary, candidate_evidence_items]
    output_schema: sop/evidence-schema.json
    acceptance: [schema_valid, codes_from_current_codebook_only]
    gate: none_if_schema_fails
  - id: verify
    owner: claude-verifier-agent
    steps: [offset_substring_check, span_supports_tag_check]
    acceptance: [quote_exact_match, tag_supported]
    gate: reject_item_else_register
  - id: synthesize
    owner: claude-synthesizer + claude-librarian
    steps: [cluster, name_active_sentence, dedup, version]
    acceptance: [theme_ids_stable, prevalence_recomputed_from_register]
    gate: human_wall_review
  - id: validate
    owner: codex-adversarial + human-designer
    steps: [independent_themes, quote_audit, alternative_explanations]
    acceptance: [all_quotes_resolve, kills_resolved, min_evidence_per_insight]
    gate: sponsor_readout

The codebook application prompt template

Apply codebook v3 (research/codebook-v3.md) to evidence/candidates/INT-NNN.jsonl.
Rules:
- Assign 1-3 codes per item from the 64 defined codes ONLY; never invent codes.
- If no code fits, use UNCODED and write a one-line candidate-code proposal.
- Do not re-read the transcript to reinterpret the quote; code what the span says.
- If the span is ambiguous between two codes, prefer the one whose definition
  requires fewer inferences, and set confidence: low.
Output: same JSONL with codes[] and confidence fields; append a code-application
log line per item. Reproducibility: temperature 0, codebook version pinned.

Guardrails (what kept 200 interviews safe)

  1. Verbatim verification kills hallucination structurally. Agents do fabricate quotes — 41 of 5,214 candidates (0.8%) contained merged or invented quotations. None reached the register, because "the quote field must resolve to a transcript span by character offsets" is a mechanical CI assertion, not a prompt instruction. Prompts request honesty; pipelines enforce it.
  2. Consent as a state machine. Withdrawal is a label triggering deletion of transcript, summary, and every derived evidence item in one PR, auditable by the DPO. Three withdrawals executed, zero residue.
  3. Pseudonymization before agents, vault outside the repo. No agent context ever contained a direct identifier; the identity map lived in client-held storage. PII lint ran as a blocking Action on every commit.
  4. No biometrics, no inference. The extraction schema forbids inferring health, ethnicity, or political/religious content even when volunteered; such passages are summarized topically with the sensitive span excluded.
  5. Human sign-off points. Consent pack (DPO PR), codebook versions, theme names (wall session), insight cards (designer + sponsor). Agents propose; named humans dispose.

Failure modes observed and mitigations

Failure mode Frequency Mitigation
Fabricated or merged quotes 41 items (0.8%) Offset verification gate; re-extract or drop
Offset drift (quote real, span wrong) 347 items (6.7%) Same gate; auto-repair by re-locating the span

9. AI tool playbook

Claude Code — the research team. Three subagents with separated duties: researcher (per-interview summaries and evidence extraction, one invocation per interview, temperature 0, schema-pinned), synthesizer (weekly clustering over the growing register), librarian (dedup, theme-ID stability, join-key hygiene). Excelled at: tireless per-interview structured work at 36-hour SLA; keeping cluster names in active sentences because the prompt demanded it. Failed at: cross-interview pattern-spotting outside its batch (the librarian's cross-theme join in use case B needed a human-commissioned follow-up to become an insight); emotional register in translation. Human took over: every theme name carrying political weight.

OpenCode — the pipeline. Agent-mode nightly jobs: transcription orchestration, dedup, pseudonymization, PII lint, telemetry joins, mock dataset generation:

opencode run --agent build "Generate data/interviews_register_sample.csv: 30
rows matching the register schema, plausible segments/dates/themes consistent
with synthesis/themes-v3.md prevalence counts, fictional quotes, seed fixed."

Excelled at: deterministic file-munging with a fixed seed; the PII quarantine. Failed at: silent mangling of French diacritics in week 2 — caught because the verifier reported offset mismatches on accented spans, fixed by pinning UTF-8 end-to-end and re-processing 14 files.

Codex — the independent voice. Two uses: the independent synthesis pass (themes proposed blind, then compared) and the adversarial insight check (Section 4.5). Excelled at: killing the weak insight card the designer was fond of, and finding that one insight's design implication outran its evidence. Failed at: over-flagging — 3 of its 9 challenges dissolved on inspection because it lacked wall-session context; verdicts need a human adjudicator, not automatic adoption.

GitHub — research ops system of record. Issue-per-interview with the consent state machine (200 issues, zero lost states); Projects board as the fieldwork picture; PRs as review gates (DPO on the consent pack, designer on codebook versions, sponsor on insight cards); Actions enforcing the two mechanical rules — PII lint and quote-offset verification — as blocking CI. The discipline that made it work: no artifact exists outside a PR, so every artifact has a reviewer, a diff, and a revert path.

10. Lessons learned and pitfalls

  1. The pipeline found trust before it found insights. The most valuable decision was making quote verification mechanical. Everything else — kappa, adversarial passes — was layered credibility on top.
  2. Rolling synthesis beats big-bang synthesis at scale. Weekly re-clustering from week 4 meant the week-8 wall session was a confirmation exercise, not a rescue. The cost — librarian overhead keeping theme IDs stable — was worth it.
  3. Agents over-split, humans over-merge. The A/B study showed the failure modes are complementary; the durable design is agents exhaust, humans consolidate, a second model attacks.
  4. Pseudonymization is a pipeline, not a promise. Both PII leaks came from quoted phrases, not metadata. Lint the content, not the headers.
  5. Consent state machines surface no-shows and withdrawals the same day. 17 rebooks within 72 hours kept every stratum at quota; the previous program lost a month to silent scheduling decay.
  6. The DPO and works council are design partners, not gates. Engaging both in week 1 turned the two highest-risk constraints into two PRs and zero stalls.
  7. Translated quotes lose emotional texture. Six quotes read as neutral in English that were raw in Dutch; retaining original-language spans with a translation flag became standard.
  8. What I would do differently: instrument branch telemetry before fieldwork, not after (the roster triangulation cost a week waiting on data pulls); and run the A/B comparison in week 2, not week 8 — its political value peaked before the first readout, not after.
