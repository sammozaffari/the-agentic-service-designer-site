01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

Aurelia Insurance, a fictional mid-size EU insurer with roughly 900,000 active policies, was losing customers at the one moment insurance actually matters: the claim. Internal analysis showed that 68% of claimants who rated their claims experience as poor switched provider at renewal, and the claims phone channel carried failure-demand costs that the operations team had stopped trying to justify. Over 16 weeks of design plus a 12-week, two-region A/B pilot, we rebuilt the claims journey around three interventions — a self-service claims assistant, proactive status communication, and a claims concierge for complex cases — co-designed with the claims handlers who run the frontline. The pilot cohort settled claims in half the calendar time, cut status-chase calls by 62%, and moved Claim Effort Score from 5.0 to 2.6, against a control group on the legacy journey.

02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait. Aurelia Insurance is a fictional composite insurer headquartered in a mid-sized Dutch city, writing roughly 520,000 motor, 260,000 home, and 120,000 small commercial and fleet policies across the Benelux. About 1,400 employees; the claims organization is 340 people, of whom 210 are claims handlers in four regional centers. Digital maturity is low: first notification of loss (FNOL) is 71% phone, 17% broker-assisted, 12% a static web form. Two legacy policy administration systems (PAS) — a mainframe platform for motor and a 2008-era system for home — are joined to the claims module by batch jobs that run overnight. The telephony platform is 14 years old. There is no claimant-facing status capability of any kind.

The challenge. Claims is the moment of truth in general insurance: the product is a promise, and the claim is where the promise is kept or broken (See: 04-service-design-for-business/01-why-service-design-for-business.md). Aurelia's own retention data showed that 68% of claimants rating the experience as poor switched at renewal, versus a 94% retention rate among claimants rating it good. Meanwhile the claims contact center was drowning: 58% of its 34,000 monthly inbound contacts were failure demand — status chases, missing-document re-contacts, and complaint handling — at a fully loaded €8.50 per call. The sponsor's original brief was "cut claims call volume by 30%." We reframed it using the business-problem reframing method: from reduce calls to make it unnecessary to call by giving claimants transparency, certainty, and control (See: 04-service-design-for-business/04-solving-problems-and-creating-value.md).

Success criteria, agreed with the sponsor:

  1. Claim Effort Score (CES, 1 = very easy, 7 = very difficult): from 5.0 to ≤ 3.0 in the pilot cohort.
  2. Status-chase contacts per claim: −50% in the pilot cohort.
  3. Simple motor claim cycle time (FNOL to settlement): from ~22 days to ≤ 12 days.
  4. A costed, finance-reviewed business case with a control-group pilot readout, not a design aspiration.

Constraints. Budget €1.5M cap for year one. No replacement of either PAS within the horizon — all integrations read-only against the core. Regulatory obligations under EU insurance distribution and GDPR, plus internal fraud-policy constraints on automated settlement. Claims handlers working to a collective labor agreement; any change to their workflow required consultation. The pilot had to run inside normal operations — no protected sandbox teams.

Why service design. Aurelia's proposed internal fix was a bigger IVR and two extra phone queues — a textbook goods-logic trap: answering a service failure by adding capacity to absorb it instead of removing its cause (See: 04-service-design-for-business/04-solving-problems-and-creating-value.md). The claimants were not calling because they wanted to talk; they were calling because the service gave them no other way to know what was happening. That is a service design problem — Movement, Structures, and Behavior misaligned across the journey — not a telephony problem (See: 04-service-design-for-business/02-the-frontline.md). The Service Profit Chain gave us the causal language the CFO would accept: internal service quality drives handler capability, which drives external service value, which drives claimant satisfaction, loyalty, and retained premium (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/05-measuring-the-chain.md).

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

Phase 1 — Discover: claims-journey mining and the frontline

Goal. Establish what actually happens to the 96,000 claims a year, and what it costs.

Method & SOP. Two SOPs ran in parallel: (1) failure-demand and cost-to-serve quantification (See: 04-service-design-for-business/07-measuring-and-evaluating.md) over 15,000 sampled claim records from the last 12 months; (2) frontline shadowing and workaround mapping with 12 claims handlers across two regions (See: 04-service-design-for-business/02-the-frontline.md).

AI execution. Claims-journey mining was delegated to Claude Code research agents running the mining SOP (Section 7): records were normalized by an OpenCode agent-mode pipeline into a clean parquet-like intermediate, then themed. The 60 call transcripts and complaint letters were pseudonymized by OpenCode before any LLM saw them — names, policy numbers, plates, and IBANs replaced with stable tokens — then analyzed by Claude Code agents into pain themes with verbatim evidence quotes linked back to claim IDs. Every theme in the register carries at least two evidence quotes; any theme an agent could not ground was marked unverified and excluded from synthesis. GitHub issues were opened per theme (theme/status-chase, theme/document-ping-pong), so the synthesis phase had trackable work items.

Human designer judgment. The designer chose the taxonomy, ran the shadowing (agents cannot sit beside a handler), and made the call to exclude three transcripts that contained active fraud investigations even after pseudonymization — a judgment about investigative sensitivity, not data hygiene.

Artifact. Evidence register (212 themed rows), frontline friction map, and the failure-demand baseline: 58% of 34,000 monthly contacts were failure demand ≈ 19,700 contacts/month × €8.50 ≈ €2.0M/year of avoidable handling cost.

Phase 2 — Define: current-state journey map and value at stake

Goal. One shared, evidence-backed picture of the claims journey and its economics.

Method & SOP. Current-state journey mapping with fail points (F) and waits (W) per the template in the case-studies chapter (See: 04-service-design-for-business/08-case-studies-and-templates.md), validated line-by-line by the handler panel. Then value-at-stake framing: premium at risk from claims-driven churn plus failure-demand cost (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/08-service-design-and-business.md, 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md).

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the mermaid journey map directly from the evidence register — every F and W annotation carries an evidence-register reference. Codex ran adversarial review on the value-at-stake model:

Artifacts. The current-state journey map (A1, Section 5) and the value-at-stake worksheet (A6): 36,500 claimants/year report a poor experience (38%); 68% of them switch ≈ 24,800 policies at risk × €590 average premium ≈ €14.6M premium at risk, plus €2.0M failure-demand cost ≈ €16.6M total value at stake per year.

Phase 3 — Develop: ideation, concepts, and the business case

Goal. Convert the opportunity map into a fundable, deliverable service concept.

Method & SOP. Service problem reframing and proposition development with triple-lens evaluation — desirability, feasibility, viability (See: 04-service-design-for-business/04-solving-problems-and-creating-value.md); concept briefs in the service-innovation-handbook style (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/07-the-toolkit-templates.md). Two co-design workshops with the handler panel used the frontline co-design method: handlers role-played the proposed exception flows against their real caseloads and killed two ideas the design team liked (auto-approval of windscreen claims under €400 — rejected on accumulated fraud-pattern grounds; a claimant chat channel — rejected because handlers already could not keep up with email).

AI execution. Claude Code ran the ideation session as an agent partner: it generated 40 candidate interventions from the opportunity matrix, the handler panel and designer scored them, and the surviving nine went to triple-lens scoring. OpenCode generated the mock claims dataset used for prototyping and cost modeling (data/claims_sample.csv). The business case was assembled as a PR; GitHub Actions ran a mermaid/markdown lint on every artifact push; Codex re-reviewed the final math before the gate.

Human designer judgment. Concept selection, workshop facilitation, and the decision to scope the concierge to complex claims only (12% of volume) — a scope cut the agents argued against on coverage grounds and the designer made on operability grounds.

Artifact. Three concept briefs (one shown as A6 in Section 5): C1 Self-service claims assistant (digital FNOL + guided photo evidence + real-time status for simple claims, 61% of volume), C2 Proactive status communications (milestone push messages at six blueprint events, all channels), C3 Claims concierge (named handler + bounded discretion for complex claims, 12% of volume).

Phase 4 — Prototype: test before we build

Goal. De-risk desirability and usability of C1+C2 before any PAS integration.

Method & SOP. Walkthrough prototyping plus a clickable status-page prototype tested with 40 claimants (recent claimants recruited from the client research panel, screened to exclude active claims), following the pilot-experimentation template structure (See: 04-service-design-for-business/08-case-studies-and-templates.md).

AI execution. OpenCode built the prototype in agent mode: a static status page fed by the mock dataset, simulating a claim moving through six milestones, with the proactive message copy embedded. It also generated 40 synthetic claimant profiles so each test session walked through a different claim.

Illustrative OpenCode host command — emitted pack; live case execution unverified.

TERMINAL
opencode run "Build a static HTML prototype of the Aurelia claim status page. \
  Data: data/claims_sample.csv (pilot rows only). Six milestone states: FNOL \
  received, triage complete, assessor assigned, estimate approved, repair \
  scheduled, payment released. Each milestone shows: what happened, what \

Claude Code coded the test observations into an issue log (19 usability issues; 11 fixed same-week; the largest: claimants read "estimate approved" as "payment approved" — copy changed to "repair cost approved").

Human designer judgment. Moderating the sessions, reading distress in two participants with recent real claims, and deciding the fraud-related copy ("your claim is under routine review") needed human review by compliance before any claimant ever saw it.

Artifact. Working prototype + test report: 38/40 participants correctly stated what happens next at every milestone (target 85%); 34/40 said they would not have called. That last number became the pilot hypothesis.

Phase 5 — Deliver: pilot and measurement

Goal. Prove contribution, not correlation, with a controlled pilot.

Method & SOP. Before/after pilot evaluation with control grouping and the three-tier measurement stack (See: 04-service-design-for-business/07-measuring-and-evaluating.md; unit-level linkage from 20-the-service-profit-chain/05-measuring-the-chain.md). Pilot: east and west regions (11,200 claims over 12 weeks), control: north and south on the legacy journey. Eligible simple claims offered the digital assistant; all pilot claims received proactive milestone messages; complex claims routed to the concierge.

AI execution. GitHub Actions regenerated the KPI dashboard from weekly claims extracts; an OpenCode agent normalized each weekly extract and flagged schema drift (the PAS export changed column order in week 6 — caught in CI, not in the readout). Codex reviewed the evaluation script and the contribution claim language before the gate deck went to the sponsor. PR review was the human sign-off gate: the claims ops lead and digital product owner had to approve every measurement readout PR before it merged.

Human designer judgment. Weekly steering with the region directors, handling the week-4 incident (milestone messages firing twice after a duplicate batch job — designer paused messages for 48h, handlers briefed claimants manually, trust preserved), and the go/no-go recommendation.

Artifact. Pilot scorecard (Section 8): every primary metric passed its gate threshold.

06 · Use cases

Use cases

UC1 — Roadside breakdown claim (persona P1)

Scenario. Sofia, 34, rear-ended on a wet ring road at 08:10, car undriveable, first claim ever. What was designed: the roadside moment became the FNOL moment — the tow-truck partner's arrival SMS contains the digital-FNOL link; photos of the damage are the claim evidence; by the time the tow leaves, the claim is triaged and the status page shows six milestones with dates.

AI working session. OpenCode generated the prototype flow and the mock tow-partner SMS payload; Claude Code drafted the six milestone messages, then ran a reading-level pass (B1 Dutch) and flagged "estimate approved" as ambiguous — the copy fix that later scored in testing. Sample agent instruction: "Rewrite each milestone message at B1 reading level, max 160 characters, each ending with what happens next and when." Outcome (illustrative, pilot digital rows): settlement in 7–9 days vs 22-day baseline, zero status calls, CES 1.8–2.1, renewal intent 93–95%.

UC2 — Multi-party property claim (complex, concierge)

Scenario. A washing-machine leak damages two apartments and a shared ceiling: two insurers, one landlord, one contractor, liability unclear. This is the 12% complex tail where self-service fails and where Aurelia previously produced its worst experiences (baseline complex: 61 days, CES 6.2, CSAT 1.9). What was designed: concierge routing — one named senior handler owns the case end-to-end, calls all parties within 24 hours, holds bounded discretion to €5,000 for emergency mitigation, and drives a shared milestone view each party sees in their own terms.

AI working session. Claude Code mapped the multi-party dependency graph from nine historical complex claims; Codex adversarially reviewed the concierge playbook for liability language risk (it flagged two template sentences that could be read as an admission of liability — rewritten with compliance); the designer ran the tabletop walkthrough with three senior handlers. Outcome (illustrative, pilot complex rows): settlement 34–37 days vs 60-day control, status calls 2–3 vs 8, CES 3.4–3.6, CSAT ~4.0. Complex claims stay human-led by design; the AI-first tooling served the designers, not the claimant.

UC3 — Fleet manager B2B claims portal (persona P2)

Scenario. Dennis runs a 46-van courier fleet; a van off the road costs him roughly €380/day in lost routes. His old journey: email the broker, wait, phone for status per vehicle, maintain his own spreadsheet. What was designed: a fleet portal — batch FNOL upload, per-vehicle status board, downtime-day counter per claim, and a concierge escalation when any claim exceeds its expected milestone date by 48 hours.

AI working session. OpenCode built the portal prototype fed by data/claims_sample.csv (fleet rows) including the downtime counter; Claude Code wrote the CSV batch-upload validator spec and the exception rules; GitHub issues tracked the seven validator edge cases (duplicate claim, wrong policy year, missing plate). Outcome (illustrative, pilot fleet rows): settlement 10–13 days vs 29-day control, CES 2.2–2.5, renewal intent 91–92%, and Dennis's spreadsheet deleted — the strongest qualitative signal of the pilot.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs on this engagement:

  1. Claims-journey mining SOP (records → themes → evidence register). Encoding pattern: closed taxonomy → per-file extraction agent → verbatim-quote grounding rule → JSON output schema → acceptance criterion (≥2 quotes per theme) → human review gate. The register, not the prose summary, is the deliverable; prose is regenerated from the register so the map can never drift from the evidence.
  2. Failure-demand quantification SOP. Contact categorization rules codified as a decision table an agent applies uniformly to 34,000 contacts/month — consistency a human coder cannot sustain at that volume, with a 2% human audit sample each month.
  3. Transcript analysis prompt (the extraction agent prompt in Section 4) — the workhorse template: closed taxonomy, verbatim evidence, ≤25-word quotes, fraud-context flag, unverified state.
  4. Good Services audit SOP. Principle-by-principle scoring with an evidence-required rule: no score without a cited observation. Agents propose scores; the designer and handler panel confirm them in a review session.

Fraud-sensitivity guardrails (insurance-specific):

  • Pseudonymization before any LLM sees claimant data; PII redaction is verified by regex re-scan, not trusted.
  • Any record touching an active fraud investigation is excluded from analysis and from model context entirely — the flag_fraud_context rule above. Agents never see, summarize, or pattern-match across fraud files.
  • Automated settlement is bounded (≤ €2,500, fraud-score threshold, 5% human audit). The assistant never communicates suspicion: claimants under review see neutral "routine review" copy approved by compliance — protecting both the investigation and the innocent majority (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/06-service-recovery.md on procedural and interactional justice).
  • Human sign-off gates: G1 insight (claims ops lead), G2 funding (sponsor + CFO), G3 pilot go (compliance + works council), G4 rollout (steering). Every gate is a PR review with named approvers; no agent output crosses a gate unmerged.

Failure modes observed: (1) the theme agent initially over-coded "empathy failure" from polite-formulaic transcripts — fixed by requiring a distress marker plus quote; (2) Codex's first business-case review invented a regulatory citation — caught because citations are verified against the books library or marked [unverified]; (3) the mermaid-drafting agent produced an edge-labeled quadrantChart that mermaid rejects — the Actions lint caught it on the PR, and the fix (plain point syntax) is now in the diagram SOP.

10 · Lessons learned

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  1. The evidence register is the single source of truth. Everything regenerates from it. The one week we let a summary drift from the register, the handler panel caught three ungrounded claims and trust in the map cost two sessions to rebuild.
  2. Codex earns its keep on math, not prose. Its business-case review found a real double-count; its copy suggestions were mostly noise. Route tools to their strengths.
  3. Frontline co-design killed our two favorite ideas — correctly. The auto-approval and chat-channel concepts would have failed in production. Handler rejection in week 9 is far cheaper than pilot failure in week 22 (See: 04-service-design-for-business/02-the-frontline.md).
  4. Fraud guardrails must be designed, not bolted on. The neutral-copy and exclusion rules took a full week with compliance; starting them in Discover would have saved a rework cycle in Develop.
  5. Proactive messaging is a trust liability when it breaks. The week-4 duplicate-message incident taught us: a broken promise is worse than silence. The fallback protocol (pause, human-brief, resume) is now part of the blueprint, not an ops runbook.
  6. Agents accelerate synthesis; they cannot create the mandate. The CFO signed the conservative case because a human chose to present the defensible number. No agent can make that call for you.
  7. Lint your diagrams in CI from day one. Mermaid failures are cheap in a PR and embarrassing in a steering deck.
