Journey Mapping from Transcripts: The Disruption Recovery Map at Albatross Airways

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Albatross Airways is a fictional composite client, assembled from patterns observed across several European network and leisure carriers; no real airline, passenger, or employee is depicted. All names, figures, datasets, and quotes are illustrative and anonymized. The article shares knowledge and process — the mapping SOPs, tagging schemas, prompts, and review gates — never client specifics. The engagement ran AI-first: Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were the primary working tools; the human designer retained framing, facilitation, and every consequential judgment call.

1. Executive summary

Albatross Airways, a fictional EU network carrier flying 28 million passengers a year across 74 routes, knew its worst moments were disruption days but could not say what those days felt like for passengers. Internal folklore ("passengers want compensation faster") had survived three improvement programmes and two app redesigns. Over eight weeks, a single service designer with an AI agent team built the airline's first validated, evidence-per-row, end-to-end journey map of disruption recovery, from 140 passenger interviews, 9,000 disruption tickets, 1.1 million app and site sessions across three disruption events, 45 staff debriefs, and 2,300 social posts. The map proved the folklore wrong: sentiment bottomed out not at the cancellation and not at the compensation claim, but during the roughly 90 minutes of information blackout at the airport and inside a rebooking engine that failed 66% of self-serve attempts. The validated map, versioned as code in Git, drove a redesigned recovery service whose autumn pilot moved self-serve rebooking containment from 34% to 78%, cut disruption-day call volume by 62%, and lifted post-disruption NPS from −34 to −9.

What you will learn:

  • How to build a journey map that is evidence-per-row traceable: every phase, emotion score, and pain point cites tagged evidence, enforced in CI rather than by discipline.
  • How to run emotion scoring with agents without inventing sentiment — a rubric with anchor quotes, distribution checks against a human calibration set, and a verbatim-only rule.
  • How map-as-code works in practice: the journey map as markdown plus mermaid plus rows.csv in Git, with validation workshops generating issues and PRs against the map itself.

2. The brief

Client portrait

Albatross Airways is a fictional mid-size EU network carrier headquartered near its primary hub at Vliethaven International (VLH), with focus cities in Barcelona, Porto, and Copenhagen. It carries 28 million passengers a year on 74 routes, roughly 380 departures a day, 61% of them leisure travellers. Digital maturity is mid-table: a competent app (54% of passengers app-registered), a modern passenger service system, but ground handling outsourced at 60% of stations and a rebooking engine bought as part of a 2016 distribution stack. EU Regulation (EC) No 261/2004 obligations — care, re-routing, and fixed compensation of €250/€400/€600 by distance — shape the recovery service.

The challenge

Annually, 2.1% of Albatross flights are cancelled and 11% delayed over two hours — about 3.6 million passengers touched. Disruption days generate failure demand at industrial scale: 380 calls per 1,000 disrupted passengers into a 340-seat contact centre spiking to 4.2× normal volume. Three earlier improvement programmes produced a static "irregular operations journey" poster in 2023 (two days of stakeholder workshop, never validated, never updated) and two app redesigns that moved nothing; post-disruption NPS sat at −34. The head of customer care's diagnosis was blunt: "We keep redesigning the compensation form. Nobody can tell me what actually happens to a passenger between the cancellation decision and the hotel bed."

Constraints

Budget €120k for the mapping programme; an 8-week window before the summer schedule locked; GDPR plus passenger-data rules (no passenger PII in any artifact, booking references pseudonymized at ingestion); the works council required notice before any staff debrief recordings; and the rebooking engine vendor contract froze functional changes until Q4 — the pilot had to route around it, not through it.

Why service design

The disruption experience is produced by a system the passenger reads as one actor: operations control, the app, gate agents, the outsourced ground handler, hotel inventory, the claims desk, travel agents. Failures live at the seams, below the line of visibility — exactly what journey mapping is built to expose: an outside-in, chronological account of what it is like to be the passenger, aligned against the organization's machinery (See: 09-mapping-experiences/04-customer-journey-maps.md, 08-orchestrating-experiences/04-understanding-the-journey.md). The 2023 poster failed Kalbach's anatomy audit on four of five elements — no lens, unbounded context, internal-stages spine, no takeaways lane (See: 09-mapping-experiences/02-the-anatomy-of-experience-maps.md). The wager: a validated map built from transcripts and telemetry would end three years of designing against folklore.

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

The programme followed the mapping lifecycle — frame, assemble evidence, extract, illustrate, validate, govern — mapped onto the Design Council Double Diamond (Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver). Inside that macro-structure, the TISDD loop ran as Research → Ideation → Prototyping → Implementation. The designer ran every phase with named AI collaborators: Claude Code extraction agents tagged the corpus; OpenCode parsed the ticket and session pipelines; Codex audited extraction accuracy adversarially; GitHub held the map as code, with workshops producing issues and PRs against the artifact.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Frame (week 1)

Goal. Bound the map before touching data: one lens, one context, customer-defined phases.

Method & SOP. The five-element alignment-diagram brief (See: 09-mapping-experiences/02-the-anatomy-of-experience-maps.md): Lens — the disrupted passenger, with two primary persona lenses carried through (leisure family; time-critical business traveller); Context — start trigger is the operations control centre logging a cancellation or >2h delay decision, end trigger is compensation resolved or journey completed, out of scope is schedule-change merchandising; Spine — chronological phases defined strictly by passenger goals, never internal stages. Phase definitions were the load-bearing decision of the whole programme, written with decision rules so agents could apply them consistently: Pre-flight (passenger learns of the disruption before reaching the airport; goal: know early), At-airport (passenger is in the terminal when certainty collapses; goal: know what happens next), New arrangements (passenger secures care — food, shelter, information; goal: basic needs tonight), Rebook (passenger changes the booking itself; goal: a seat that works), Recovery/compensation (passenger seeks redress after the fact; goal: what I am owed). The boundary rule that later mattered most: if the passenger is securing basic needs it is New arrangements; if the booking record changes it is Rebook — even when both happen in the same queue.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the anatomy brief and phase decision rules from the intake notes; the designer rewrote the phase names into passenger language. GitHub issue #1 held the context statement; the head of customer care's sign-off is PR #2.

Human designer judgment. Choosing "the disrupted passenger" as one lens rather than five segment maps — disruption compresses differences; the family and the consultant suffer at the same seams. Also refusing operations' request for a "diversion handling" phase: an internal stage, not a passenger goal.

Artifact produced. The context statement and phase definitions, versioned as phases.yaml (A6), unchanged for the rest of the programme except one sharpened boundary rule after the Codex audit (§4.3).

4.2 Assemble (weeks 1–4)

Goal. Build a corpus that triangulates what passengers say, what they do, and what the system records (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md).

Method & SOP. Five sources in parallel. (1) 140 episodic interviews of 60–90 minutes using the chronological reconstruction protocol (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/04-understanding-the-journey.md): anchor on the concrete disruption episode, walk it step by step, probe channel choices and emotional highs and lows, surface the shadow journey — the family WhatsApp group, the rail app, the Reddit thread. Recruitment oversampled the failures the client wanted to ignore: 22 of the 140 were no-fly or route-switch incidents. (2) 9,000 disruption tickets across three studied events (Storm Yara in January, ATC industrial action in March, a rebooking-engine IT outage in May: 412 cancellations, 1,940 delays over two hours, 118,000 passengers affected). (3) 1.1M app and site sessions during those events, for dwell times and abandonment funnels. (4) 45 staff debriefs: gate agents, OCC duty managers, ground-handler supervisors, contact-centre team leads. (5) 2,300 social posts and verbatim survey comments as netnography.

AI execution. Fieldwork stayed human. Everything downstream was agent-built. OpenCode ran the two data pipelines in agent mode:

opencode run --agent build "Ingest raw/disruption_tickets.csv (9000 rows).
Parse each ticket into a structured event: {ticket_id, event, phase_guess,
channel, timestamps, issue_text, resolution}. Pseudonymize booking refs to
TKT-xxxx. Output research/ticket_events.jsonl. Then compute dwell-time and
abandonment funnels from raw/session_events.parquet for the 3 event windows."

Claude Code prepared the interview corpus: transcription, pseudonymization (INT-001…INT-140), and a corpus store layout (research/corpus/) that every downstream agent cited against. All processing EU-resident; the PII rule was absolute — no passenger names, booking references, or seat numbers in any artifact, enforced by a CI scan (§7).

Human designer judgment. Interview conduct, consent, and the recruitment decision to chase the 22 no-fly incidents — the client's working theory was that route-switchers were "price-driven"; the interviews showed 17 of 22 switched because of recovery handling, not fares.

Artifact produced. The corpus: 140 pseudonymized transcripts, 9,000 structured ticket events, session funnels, 45 debrief notes, 1,900 usable social items — the raw material for 17,200 tagged evidence items.

4.3 Extract (weeks 3–5)

Goal. Convert the corpus into tagged evidence items — every quote and fact carrying phase, channel, emotion, and pain code — with independently audited accuracy.

Method & SOP. Evidence tagging against the schema in A6: each item carries source, journey phase (per the decision rules), channel, emotion score 1–5 against the anchor rubric, and pain code. Emotion scoring followed the emotional-curve calibration method (See: 09-mapping-experiences/04-customer-journey-maps.md): score the evidence, never the vibe; average per phase; annotate the deepest valleys with trigger events.

AI execution. Claude Code ran a tagging fan-out — one extraction agent per source, one schema, a verbatim-only rule — with a verifier agent grep-checking every quoted string against the corpus store before merge. Representative prompt:

You are JOURNEY-TAGGER. Input: one pseudonymized disruption transcript.
Extract evidence items. For each item output EXACTLY:
- evidence_id: EV-<source>-<seq>
- source_ref: INT-xxx | TKT-xxxx | SOC-xxx | DEB-xx
- phase: pre_flight | at_airport | new_arrangements | rebook | recovery
  (apply phases.yaml decision rules; if the booking record changed -> rebook)
- channel: app | sms | email | phone | desk | gate_board | kiosk | social | web_form
- type: verbatim_quote | observed_behavior | operational_fact | workaround
- text: exact verbatim (never paraphrase a quote)
- emotion_1_5: score against rubric anchors in emotion.yaml; if no emotion
  evidence in the text, output null — never infer
- pain_code: INFO | REB | CARE | COMP | QUEUE | BAG | OTHER
- source_lines: line range
RULES: no quote, no claim. No emotion without an anchor. Output JSONL.

The first full pass hit the engagement's signature failure mode: the agents flattened emotion to the mid-range — mean 2.9 with 68% of items scored exactly 3, a sentiment smoothie with no valleys to prioritize. The mitigation (detailed in §7): anchor quotes per score, a 300-item human calibration set, per-phase standard deviation checks, and re-scoring of the flattened batches. After re-scoring: corpus mean 2.2, 14% scored 3, and per-phase means separated into the curve the workshops later validated (A1).

Codex then audited a random 400-item sample (2.3%) for extraction accuracy:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Audit research/evidence-register.jsonl
sample400.jsonl against research/corpus/. For each item: (1) does the quote
exist verbatim at source_lines; (2) is the phase correct under phases.yaml;
(3) is emotion_1_5 supported by an anchor or is it inferred; (4) is pain_code
defensible. Report agreement rate and the dominant confusion pairs."

Result: 94% exact agreement, Cohen's kappa 0.81 against the human calibration set. The dominant confusion was the New arrangements ↔ Rebook boundary (6% of items) — passengers at the service desk were securing a hotel and changing a booking in the same conversation. The fix was not re-tagging; it was sharpening the decision rule in phases.yaml ("the booking record changes → rebook") and re-running the affected batches. Without written rules, agents and humans would have disagreed silently.

Human designer judgment. Building the calibration set personally (300 items scored by the designer and the two client stakeholders in one sitting), and the call to treat Codex's 6% as a definition problem rather than an agent-quality problem.

Artifact produced. The tagged evidence register — 17,200 items, each traceable to source lines — at research/evidence-register.jsonl (PR #31), plus the audit memo.

4.4 Map (week 5)

Goal. Assemble the draft map so that every row is traceable — the map is an index over the evidence register, not a drawing.

Method & SOP. Current-state journey map construction per Kalbach's lane anatomy: phases as headers, then actions, touchpoints and channels, thoughts and verbatims, the emotion curve, pain points — with pain points strictly separated from opportunities (See: 09-mapping-experiences/04-customer-journey-maps.md). Information design followed the three-reading-depths discipline: a 10-second executive strip (the curve and three takeaways), a 3-minute phase read, a 30-minute practitioner dive into rows and quotes (See: 09-mapping-experiences/07-designing-the-map.md). The emotional narrative was cross-checked against the disruption arc: cancellation as inciting incident, the airport blackout as crisis, rebooking as climax, compensation as resolution (See: 10-the-users-journey/01-why-story.md) — the client's 2023 poster had no crisis beat, because it had no evidence.

AI execution. This is where map-as-code was born. The map lived in Git as maps/disruption-recovery/v0.9/: map.md (the mermaid rendering), rows.csv (one row per map cell with mandatory evidence refs), phases.yaml, emotion.yaml. Claude Code authored the first draft by rolling up the register per phase; journey lint rejected the first two commits because 9 rows had fewer than two evidence items from two sources — the rows were strengthened or dropped. The emotion line came from phase-level means of scored items, not from a drawn curve: Pre-flight 2.2, At-airport 1.4, New arrangements 2.2, Rebook 1.6, Recovery 1.8.

Human designer judgment. Choosing the three headline takeaways for the executive strip — the information blackout, the rebooking engine failure, the 63-day compensation tail — and writing the one-sentence version the COO repeated in the pilot steering: "Passengers forgive the storm; they do not forgive the silence."

Artifact produced. Draft map v0.9 (A1), emotion table, pain-code distribution (A8), all with full row-level traceability.

4.5 Validate (week 6)

Goal. Convert the draft into an organizational truth: validated by the people who run the journey, in facilitated sessions, with repairs tracked as code changes.

Method & SOP. Three co-facilitated validation workshops (34 participants total: gate agents, OCC, ground handler, contact centre, claims, digital, legal, and four passengers from the advisory panel), running a condensed version of Kalbach's seven-phase mapping arc — Frame, Immerse, Build, Walk the Map, Ideate, Prioritize, Commit (See: 09-mapping-experiences/08-facilitating-mapping-sessions.md). The designer co-facilitated with the head of customer care; presenting a finished map would have made critics, so the workshops worked from evidence packs and were licensed to break the map.

AI execution. The map's code form changed the workshop mechanics. During Build, participants turned row-level objections into issues on the spot — 41 across three sessions, each tagged to a map row ID. During Ideate and Prioritize, the strongest fixes became PRs against rows.csv and map.md, merged live in the room with the proposer watching: 29 merged, 12 parked. Examples: gate agents corrected the At-airport row "passengers check the app" to "passengers check the departures board first, then the app, then distrust both" — supported by three interviews the agents had under-weighted; the claims lead killed a draft pain point the evidence could not support at two-source minimum. Claude Code acted as scribe, drafting issue text and projecting the diff after each merge. The shadow journey lane (family WhatsApp, rail apps, Reddit) survived scrutiny intact — the ground-handler supervisor confirmed it from the desk side: "They know about the 19:20 rail option before we do."

Human designer judgment. Facilitation, power management (a passenger panelist outranks a vendor manager in a validation room — the designer enforced it), and the decision to park rather than resolve 12 issues that needed data the corpus did not have.

Artifact produced. Validated map v1.0 with a public repair log (41 issues, 29 merged PRs), the moment-that-matters scoring (A8 quadrant), and the workshop facilitation plan template (A6).

4.6 Handoff and govern (weeks 7–8)

Goal. Hand the validated journey to the blueprint work with zero information loss, and leave the map as a living artifact.

Method & SOP. The journey-to-blueprint transition: the journey map stays the passenger-facing truth; the blueprint takes its rows as requirements. The gate before blueprinting: every blueprint step must cite the journey row IDs it answers, and every Tier-1 journey moment must have at least one blueprint step — enforced as a CI check on the traceability table (A9). Governance followed the living-map programme: a named map owner (head of customer care), a quarterly refresh cadence, and versioned releases in Git (See: 09-mapping-experiences/08-facilitating-mapping-sessions.md).

AI execution. Claude Code generated the traceability table from the map row IDs and the blueprint draft, and Codex ran the gate review — catching two blueprint steps that answered no journey pain (vendor wish-list items that had crept in) and one Tier-1 moment (the family reaccommodation queue) with no blueprint coverage. GitHub milestones closed the programme: v1.0 map, v1.0 blueprint seed, pilot charter.

Human designer judgment. The scope call to exclude the rebooking engine rebuild from the pilot (vendor contract freeze) and design around it with an offer-orchestration layer instead — a constraint turned into the pilot's central design move.

Artifact produced. The future-state map and emotion projection, the blueprint seed (A2), the traceability table (A9), and the governance plan. The 12-week autumn pilot of the redesigned recovery service (two disruption events, 41,000 affected passengers) produced the value figures in §8.

T3: Emotion scoring rubric (emotion.yaml, abridged)

1 Distressed.  Passenger reports crisis language, tears, panic, giving up.
  Anchor: "The children slept on our coats." (INT-041)
2 Negative.    Frustration, distrust, complaint intent; workaround forming.
  Anchor: "Nobody could tell me anything, so I stopped asking." (INT-074)
3 Neutral.     Transactional, no valence language, task completed flatly.
  Anchor: "I filled in the form and waited." (INT-118)
4 Positive.    Relief, gratitude, effort acknowledged.
  Anchor: "The agent actually fixed it while I watched." (INT-096)
5 Delighted.   Advocacy language, unprompted praise, story retold.
  Anchor: "They rebooked me before I reached the terminal." (SOC-402)
RULES: score only explicit evidence; null when absent; never average nulls;
every batch must show per-phase standard deviation >= 0.8 or be re-scored.

T4: Map validation workshop facilitation plan (2.5 hours, per session)

1. FRAME (15 min)       : sponsor states intent; ground rule "the evidence is the
                          passenger, not the room"; lens and scope restated
2. IMMERSE (25 min)     : evidence gallery — quote wall, two audio clips, the
                          session-funnel screen recordings; silent reading
3. BUILD (30 min)       : participants assemble and correct rows in small groups;
                          row-level objections become GitHub issues tagged live
4. WALK THE MAP (20 min): one participant role-plays the passenger phase by
                          phase; group confirms emotion scores against anchors
5. IDEATE (20 min)      : turn validated pain points into "How might we" options
                          and draft fixes against the map
6. PRIORITIZE (25 min)  : dot-vote fixes by impact and feasibility; top ideas
                          become PR candidates, low-confidence items get parked
7. COMMIT (15 min)      : confirm version bump; assign owners; set the next
                          review date; no vague enthusiasm allowed
LOGISTICS: max 12 per session; evidence packs circulated 48h ahead; printed
map plus live repo projected; decisions logged in the repair log, not minutes

6. Use cases

Use case A — Same-day rebooking self-serve (F-04)

Scenario. EVT-02, the March ATC action: Kathrin Brandt's 18:05 Frankfurt–Vliethaven is cancelled at 15:40. Baseline behavior: she joins the desk queue, phones the contact centre from the queue, and her assistant rebooks through the travel agent — three parallel bets, one wasted seat. The rebooking engine fails her self-serve attempt because her ticket was agent-issued; 41% of disrupted passengers defaulted to the phone.

What was designed. The offer orchestration layer sits over the frozen vendor engine: when the OCC logs the reason code, each disrupted passenger receives a push with two or three ranked, bookable options — a same-day remaining seat, next-morning plus hotel, or refund plus rail voucher on short-haul — each with a 20-minute soft hold: "This seat is yours until 16:05." Agent-issued tickets get parity through a PNR lookup bridge. Rovers carry the same offers for non-digital passengers.

AI working session. OpenCode built the offer-ranking mock against replayed seat inventory from the three studied events, tuning the soft-hold window (10, 20, and 30 minutes simulated; 20 balanced seat spoilage against decision comfort). Claude Code drafted the notification copy at CEFR B1 and red-teamed variants for panic-inducing wording. Codex reviewed the ranking logic and caught a bias: v1 ranked the airline's cheapest option first under seat pressure; the principle "rank by passenger outcome" was enforced and re-tested.

Outcome (illustrative, pilot). Self-serve containment 34% → 78%; phone default 41% → 12%; median time-to-rebooked 96 → 9 minutes; soft-hold conversion 83%; parallel-betting (passengers holding two active channels at once) down 69%.

Use case B — Family-with-minors reaccommodation (F-03)

Scenario. Storm Yara cancels the Vermeulens' 19:40 to Faro at 21:15. Baseline: one parent queues 80 minutes at the ground-handler desk while the other beds the children on coats near Gate D; the hotel desk closes at 23:00; the family ends up in two non-adjacent rooms across town.

What was designed. Care becomes automatic: the disruption wallet issues meal vouchers for four (child-priced, not adult-capped) the moment the cancellation is reason-coded "overnight"; the accommodation pool allocates family blocks — adjacent rooms held per station — ranked by distance and family suitability; rovers with tablets sweep family seating zones first; a care pack (blankets, children's snacks, activity cards) sits at every rover station. Families with minors are flagged from the booking record, so nobody has to declare "I have children" in a queue.

AI working session. Claude Code generated the rover sweep pattern and the family-block allocation rules as SOP-as-code; OpenCode stress-tested the hotel pool against the storm's actual demand curve (family rooms ran out at 23:40 in simulation — the buffer was raised from 6% to 12% of block inventory); Codex reviewed the under-18 flagging for GDPR data minimization and required the flag be computed, never displayed. The first copy draft said "your comfort is our priority"; the designer replaced it with what the voucher actually buys, per the workshop ground rule.

Outcome (illustrative, pilot). Voucher issue time 34 → 3 minutes; care complaints 210 → 58 per major event; family-segment post-disruption NPS −41 → −12; zero children-accommodated-off-site incidents across the two pilot events.

Use case C — Compensation claims after disruption (F-05)

Scenario. Three weeks after her disruption, a passenger starts the EU261 claim: an 11-field form asking for booking reference, flight number, bank details, and uploaded receipts — most of which the airline already holds. 18% abandon mid-claim; the median resolved claim takes 63 days; 7.8 per 1,000 claims escalate to the national enforcement body.

What was designed. Compensation goes proactive: the eligibility engine pre-scores every disrupted booking against Regulation (EC) No 261/2004 within 72 hours; eligible passengers receive a pre-filled claim in the disruption wallet — amount stated (€250/€400/€600 by distance), two taps to confirm, SEPA payment inside a 14-day service standard. Extraordinary-circumstance refusals state the specific reasoning in plain language, with the enforcement route acknowledged honestly rather than hidden.

AI working session. OpenCode replayed the three events' 118,000 bookings through the draft eligibility rules and found 4% false-eligible (extraordinary-circumstance misclassification) — the rules were hardened before pilot. Claude Code drafted the refusal-letter template; Codex reviewed it against the regulation text and caught wording implying weather automatically exempts compensation — it does not; the airline must show all reasonable measures were taken. GitHub tracked the template as a legal-reviewed artifact with CODEOWNERS on the claims folder.

Outcome (illustrative, pilot). Claim form abandonment 18% → 6%; median resolution 63 → 19 days; claim initiation among eligible passengers 22% → 46%; NEB escalations 7.8 → 2.1 per 1,000 claims. Proactive payment raised gross compensation cost by 11% while cutting handling and escalation cost by 38% per claim — a net saving the CFO signed off.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Four SOPs were encoded for agent execution, each following the same pattern: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Journey-mapping SOP (mapping-sop.yaml): phase definitions from phases.yaml; evidence minimum two items from two sources per row; emotion only from rubric anchors; pain and opportunity lanes strictly separated; journey lint as the CI gate; human sign-off at v1.0. Every acceptance criterion is a check, not a hope.
  2. Evidence-tagging SOP: the JOURNEY-TAGGER prompt (§4.3) plus the verbatim verifier — a quoted string not found in the corpus fails the merge.
  3. Emotion-scoring SOP: rubric anchors per score; null when no evidence; a batch is rejected if per-phase standard deviation < 0.8 or if the score-3 share exceeds 30% — the flattening tripwire; 10% of every batch re-scored against the human calibration set.
  4. Quote-verification SOP: every quote in any artifact must grep-match the corpus store at its cited line range. One check on every PR; it caught 11 fabricated or misattributed quotes across the programme — every one plausible, every one wrong.

The meta-SOP. Frame → assemble → extract → map → validate → hand off, with gates: no extraction before phase definitions are signed; no map before the extraction audit passes ≥ 90%; no workshop before journey lint is green; no blueprint before the traceability gate passes (every Tier-1 moment covered, every blueprint step citing journey rows). Gates are GitHub milestones with required reviews — process enforced by tooling, not memory.

Guardrails.

  • Emotion is never invented. Scores come only from evidence-coded text against anchors; session and ticket aggregates inform dwell times and abandonment, never emotion. The rubric's null option is load-bearing.
  • No passenger PII in the map. Pseudonymization at ingestion; a CI scan rejects names, booking references, and seat numbers in any artifact; all processing EU-resident.
  • Verbatim or nothing. Agents may compress analysis; they may never compress quotes.
  • Human sign-off points. Phase definitions; the calibration set; workshop facilitation; persona naming; every version bump; the blueprint gate.

Failure modes observed. (1) Emotion flattening: the first pass scored 68% of items exactly 3 — sentiment smoothed into uselessness; anchors plus distribution checks (stdev ≥ 0.8, score-3 share ≤ 30%) brought the mean to 2.2 with 14% at 3. (2) Phase-boundary confusion: New arrangements ↔ Rebook at the service desk (6% of audited items) — fixed by sharpening the decision rule, not by blaming extraction. (3) Eloquence bias: early synthesis over-weighted articulate interviewees; mitigated by quote-count normalization. (4) Mermaid drift: two early renders broke on unquoted labels; journey lint killed the class of error. (5) A confident wrong funnel: the session pipeline counted app opens during the outage as rebooking intent; caught because funnel claims must cite the session-event taxonomy, which did not support it.

Causal logic. Lagging: calls per 1,000, claim resolution days, post-disruption NPS, NEB escalations, care complaints per event. 10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • The map's value was the repair log, not the poster. Twenty-nine merged PRs from three workshops is organizational memory with an audit trail; the 2023 poster had neither evidence nor history, so it persuaded no one and expired quietly.
  • Folklore dies when emotion is computed, not drawn. The 1.4 blackout valley ended three years of "compensation is the problem" in one steering meeting. A hand-drawn curve would have been negotiated upward; corpus means could not be.
  • Emotion flattening is the default failure of LLM sentiment. Without anchors and distribution checks, agents regress every score to the mean and the curve loses its valleys — the very thing the map exists to show. Budget the calibration set from day one.
  • Phase definitions with decision rules are the highest-leverage page in the repository. Every downstream disagreement — agents vs humans, agents vs Codex, workshop vs draft — resolved to a rule lookup. Write them before extraction, sharpen them after audit.
  • Verbatim verification is the highest-ROI hallucination control in AI-assisted mapping. Eleven fabricated or misattributed quotes, all plausible, all caught by one grep-style CI check.
  • Codex is an auditor, not an oracle. Its 6% phase-confusion finding was real but its framing (agent quality) was wrong; the fix was a definition change. Verify the diagnosis, not just the finding.
  • Map-as-code made validation mechanical. Build surfaced issues, ideation and prioritization turned the strongest fixes into PRs, and the room watched its objections merge. The facilitation energy went into judgment instead of note-taking (See: 09-mapping-experiences/08-facilitating-mapping-sessions.md).
  • Done differently next time: baseline the Good Services scorecard in week 1; invite travel agents into the workshops (their 12% of bookings surfaced late, in use case A's parity bridge); and run event-triggered diary prompts during live disruptions rather than relying on retrospective interviews alone (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/04-understanding-the-journey.md).
