01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

A global quick-service restaurant chain ran the certification of tens of thousands of crew members and restaurant managers on a learning platform that could not do the one thing a safety-critical learning system must do: control who learns what, when, and with proof. A new hire saw every course the network owned on day one — station training they were not yet certified for, equipment training for machines their restaurant did not have, age-restricted content they could not touch. Completions could not be trusted: training was marked complete on one system and failed to land on the other. Badges, once awarded, could never be revoked. Support tickets — about 155 a month, roughly 1,860 a year — were the visible price of this design. The invisible price was regulatory: age-restricted training ungated, certification on the wrong equipment, no audit trail for post-incident recertification, no expiry tracking for legislative certifications in food safety and first aid.

$25.8Kannual support cost — all of it avoidable

1,000+completions at 100% marking accuracy

Over roughly twelve months, a small team — a service designer leading, two analysts, the vendor's product team and the global support organisation as dependencies — replaced the legacy setup with a single, mobile-first learning platform. The work ran as a closed loop: a business case built from support-ticket data (about 600 avoidable support hours a year); a full-day scoping workshop mapping the learner's journey one system at a time across six systems; a rebuild of the learning experience as linear, role-based journeys with age gates, lock sequencing, and prerequisite badges; and a two-market pilot that produced the evidence to accelerate the national rollout.

The pilot numbers were the point: zero tickets raised against the new platform, more than 1,000 training completions, and 100% of completed learning objects marked complete — the guarantee the old dual-system setup could not give. In a short agree-scale survey, every respondent agreed they could complete their training without asking a manager what to do next. On that evidence the chain accelerated the national rollout, and the annual recertification cycle — the moment the old platform failed every year — ran cleanly on the new one.

What you will learn:

  • How to build a business case from support-ticket data — with the exclusions stated honestly, because an understated burden is more credible than an overstated one.
  • How to run a multi-system scoping workshop in which every system gets a swim lane and every fail point gets an owner.
  • How to design compliance into the learner journey itself: age gates, lock sequencing, prerequisite badges, progressive disclosure.
  • How to design pilot metrics that mirror the business case, so the evidence closes the loop from quantified problem to quantified proof.
02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

The client is a global quick-service restaurant chain running several hundred franchised restaurants across two markets. The learning system serves everyone in a restaurant: crew on front-counter, drive-thru, and kitchen stations; shift supervisors and assistant managers on certification paths; restaurant general managers responsible for their team's training; and the franchisee groups who own the restaurants. The workforce is young, high-turnover, and linguistically diverse — English is a second language for a significant share of the crew. Two legislative certification lines, food safety and first aid, carry hard expiry dates that the legacy system was failing to track.

The challenge

The surface ask was simple: replace the legacy learning platform. The real problem was the architecture underneath it: two learning systems at once — a legacy front-end sitting on a paid back-end platform in place for years. That dual setup produced three failure clusters, each stated in the business case:

  • Incorrect training completions — employees completing training they should not, not completing training they need, and no way to restrict training by age.
  • Poor user experience — not mobile-friendly, complex navigation, an unclear learning journey.
  • Complex training management — no workflow control or pacing; training could be skipped, done in any order, everything available on day one. Standards were duplicated, comments and feedback could not be exported, and there was no analytics — no time taken, no progression detail.

The reframe was that this was not a user-interface problem. The legacy front-end was a door that opened onto an uncontrolled warehouse: the failure was in the ecosystem — six systems hand-shaking beneath the learner — not in any single screen (See: 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md).

Success criteria (agreed with the client)

Constraints

  • No new licence cost in the model. The back-end platform licence was already paid, so the business case was purely a support-burden and compliance-risk release — unusually clean economics.
  • Roughly 12 months to land before the annual recertification cycle — the period when the old platform's failure modes did the most damage.
  • Franchisee autonomy. Content is franchisee-specific and franchisee groups run their own restaurants; a centrally imposed platform still had to be sold to them, not dictated.
  • Age data was not fed into any system. True age-based restriction required a data-level fix that could not wait for this release — an interim self-confirmation gate was the design answer (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/07-implementation.md).
  • A peak mid-year promotional trading period sat directly across the intended pilot window, forcing a phased, market-by-market start.
  • External dependencies. The platform vendor's product team, and a sandbox environment whose admin access was initially too limited to build in independently.

Why service design

This was not a conversion problem on one screen; it was an experience produced by a socio-technical system — six systems, dozens of hand-offs, certification data flowing below the line of visibility. That is precisely what service design exists for. The governing structure came from Polaine, Løvlie, and Reason's arc — research to insight, ideas, prototyping, implementation (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md) — and the economic frame from the Service Profit Chain: restaurant managers were spending their scarcest resource, time on the floor, absorbing a system failure. And because a learning platform in a quick-service restaurant is a safety system — it controls who may touch which equipment, and who can prove they may — the design had to treat compliance as the primary experience, not a bolt-on (See: 05-good-services/02-the-fifteen-principles.md).

03 · Approach

Approach: the AI-first service design process

The engagement followed the Double Diamond with the insight-to-implementation arc as its spine, run by one lead designer with two analysts and disciplined tooling: the ticketing system as evidence base, the spreadsheet as single source of truth, the sandbox platform as prototyping surface, and the slide deck as system of record — every gate a review against a named audience. The arc: quantify the problem (business case), align on the target state (scoping workshop), rebuild the journeys (phased build), and prove the outcome (pilot and rollout) (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/08-service-design-and-business.md).

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research — the business case

Goal. Put a number on the legacy platform's failure load and a shape on its compliance risk — before anyone proposed spending anything.

Method. The ticketing system recorded 155 tickets a month for the legacy platform: about 50 hours of support effort and roughly $2,150 a month. Annualized: about 1,860 tickets, 600 hours, and $25,800 a year. The model's stated exclusions — manager time spent logging and chasing tickets, escalation time, manager cost, multi-issue tickets counted once — all pushed the true burden higher, never lower. Corroborating evidence: about 2,700 tickets in the trailing 18 months, roughly 1,100 escalated to the global support team, and a full-time role consumed by learning-platform support.

Compliance-risk register. The business case carried a six-row risk table — the reason this was a safety project, not an upgrade:

Risk What the legacy platform did Impact
Irreversible badges Training modules could be removed, but a certification badge, once awarded, could never be revoked Certification integrity undermined
No audit trail No trace of post-incident recertification Legal and reputational exposure
No expiry tracking Legislative certifications (food safety, first aid) not tracked to expiry Legislative gap
Wrong-equipment certification Staff certified on equipment the restaurant does not have, or operating equipment without certification Safety risk and liability
Restricted reporting Advanced reporting limited to a single person per restaurant No oversight; concentrated control
Dual-system sync failures Completions not consistently recorded between the two systems Certification gaps, progression delays, support load

Two registers completed the picture: a feature-loss register of twelve capabilities stripped from the last migration (proxy access for managers, franchisee admin access, viewing one's own training, notifications, cross-portal completion marking, and more), and a future-fit register of the capabilities the network needed to be a leading QSR learning experience (content pacing, age restriction, mobile, detailed analytics, language translation). Every claim was validated line-by-line with the platform vendor's product owner — so feasibility was agreed before the design depended on it.

Human designer judgment. The model was built to understate: stating the exclusions made the $25,800 figure defensible under challenge, and it survived the funding gate exactly because it did.

Artifacts produced. Business case (cost model, compliance-risk register, feature registers, options analysis), validated line-by-line with the vendor product owner.

4.2 Synthesis — decide and reframe

Goal. Turn the quantified problem into a decision and a framing that the whole network could accept.

Method. Options analysis: do nothing, a minor upgrade, or full replacement. The recommendation — full replacement — was approved on the economics: the platform licence was already paid, so the release was pure cost reduction plus risk reduction. This was never just a migration of content: the team consolidated content into portals and mapped progression paths that made sense to the learner before cutover.

Human designer judgment. The load-bearing move was the continuity frame: "What's changing is the learning experience. What's staying the same is the learning itself." Separating platform change from standards change defused the restart-from-scratch fear before it formed. Five design principles followed: D1 A single mobile-first front door. D2 Only assigned learning is visible. D3 Nothing unlocks before its prerequisite. D4 Compliance is provable, not asserted. D5 Widen visibility; never hoard it in one role.

Artifacts produced. Options analysis, capability validation, design principles.

4.3 Mapping — the scoping workshop

Goal. Map the current state with one swim lane per system, and design the target state the ecosystem should become.

Method & SOP. A full-day facilitated workshop on a timed agenda. Current-state mapping: one lane per system — the HR system, identity and single sign-on, the legacy front-end, the learning back-end, the recruiting platform, and the people platform — traversing the complete learner lifecycle from user creation, through login, navigation, enrolment, launch, and completion, to reporting. Every hand-off between systems is a potential fail point, and the exercise forced each one onto the table.

The signature exercise was target-state visioning: "if the legacy front-end disappeared tomorrow…" — what would the learner experience become? Requirements and user stories were captured in three groups — learners; managers and administrators; platform and compliance — rather than one per system, which kept the artefact whole. Dependencies, constraints, and risks were named under an explicit norm of brutal honesty. Every exercise fed one spreadsheet — as-is and to-be flows, risks, an assumptions log, owners, due dates — the spreadsheet, not the room, was the single source of truth.

Fail points surfaced fast: account links at user creation, generating tickets before the first login; dual sign-in paths; the unfiltered content wall at navigation; no sequencing at enrolment; completions recorded on one system and not the other; reporting restricted to a single role per restaurant, with no audit trail. The facilitator prompts that did the work: "Where does this decision actually happen?" — "Is that enforced, or just hidden?" — "What breaks if this step fails?"

Human designer judgment. Keeping the blueprint whole — refusing the request to split it per system, which would have re-siloed the artefact — and choosing where the line of visibility sits: learner-facing surfaces are visible; sync and certification plumbing stay backstage until they break.

Capture schema — the spreadsheet as system of record.

4.4 Ideation — the journey redesign

Goal. Design the compliance controls as a learner experience, not an administrative feature.

Method. A content audit of everything the network owned — "massive" was the working adjective — then cleanup of outdated content, mapping of franchisee-specific content into portals, and consolidation into a formalised learning architecture: learning experiences within role-based focus areas. The pivotal decision: linear journeys. Team members are guided through the correct sequence; induction must complete before station training unlocks. Lock sequencing added pacing: a 24-hour wait before day-2 induction modules, and supervisor assessments barred from the same day as web-based training.

Progressive disclosure became a compliance tool, not a cosmetic one: only assigned focus areas appear; due and overdue indicators, mandatory-training markers, completion dates, and a "show completed" toggle keep the dashboard honest. Age-restricted learning sits behind a confirmation gate — interim, because age data is not fed into any system; a custom field and learner self-confirmation were the stopgap while a data-level fix was committed. Equipment training was split into fundamentals and open-procedures; learners still select equipment, but the experience is simplified and sequential — interim again, until a live equipment registry auto-targets training to the equipment that actually exists in each restaurant. Badge engineering closed the integrity loophole: a new open-procedures certification badge was inserted as a prerequisite, because without it crew could earn the supervisor-trained badge without ever being certified on open procedures.

Human designer judgment. Consistency over novelty at launch — reuse existing learning objects wherever possible to reduce migration risk; redesign the pathways, not the standards; nail the functionality before the look. The redesign changed the experience, not what defines a certified team member at any station.

Journey-gate configuration — config.

CONFIG
focus_area: onboarding_path
audience: new_crew
sequence:
  - induction                     # locked until: role assigned
  - induction_day2                # locked until: induction + 24h wait
  - station_training: front       # locked until: induction_day2
  - station_training: kitchen     # locked until: induction_day2
  - age_restricted_training       # gate: learner confirms minimum age
  - equipment_certification       # locked until: fundamentals complete
prerequisite_badges:
  - open_procedures_cert          # required before supervisor-trained badge

  - show_only_assigned: true
  - due_overdue_indicators: true

labeled interim deliberately; the permanent fix is a live equipment registry
that targets training from each store's real equipment list.

Artifacts produced. Learning architecture, journey specifications with gates, dashboard specification, new badge definition.

4.5 Prototyping — sandbox and acceptance testing

Goal. Prove the journeys in a realistic environment before anything touched production (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/06-prototyping-services.md).

Method. Most of the network's learning experiences were copied from production into the sandbox for redesign and testing. The learning architecture was built; user testing ran with the global team; franchisee-specific test groups validated cross-portal completion marking — completing in one portal and marked complete in another, the failure the legacy setup could not fix. Acceptance testing audited the content transition from sandbox to production, validated completion and progression, and verified reporting.

Constraint surfaced. Limited administrator access in the sandbox slowed independent building, testing, and development — resolved only when the vendor created per-market administrator levels. The lesson, logged then and learned loud: external dependencies must be surfaced at the start, not discovered mid-build.

Human designer judgment. Function before aesthetics, held on principle: the dashboard spec — tabs for my learning, my team, history, accomplishments, live events, feedback, and help — iterated only after the flows worked. The UI was honest enough to say "work in progress."

Artifacts produced. Sandbox journeys, dashboard specification, acceptance-test results.

4.6 Implementation — the pilot

Goal. Prove the closed loop — zero tickets, reliable marking, self-directed learners — in live restaurants (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md).

Method. A pilot in two markets, phased around a peak mid-year promotional trading period: market A first, then market B. Participants were the five largest franchisee groups plus independents, recruited through an expression-of-interest mechanism after demos at leadership forums, coach check-ins, and roadshows — buy-in built before the test. Pilot metrics deliberately mirrored the business-case metrics: tickets in versus tickets out, completion-marking verification, a short agree-scale survey, and a running "what's not working" feedback channel.

Pilot results. Zero tickets raised against the new platform. More than 1,000 training completions. 100% of completed learning objects marked complete — the guarantee the dual-system setup could not give. The survey: navigation ease, 85% strongly agree and 15% agree; completing training without asking a manager, 44% strongly and 56% agree; finding all required training, 58% strongly, 28% agree, 14% disagree. And 100% answered yes when asked whether everything they completed had actually marked complete. The open text carried the design verdict in paraphrase: "clear and well structured," "much better and more user friendly," "easy to see what I need to do," "less time for managers telling team members what to do," "love the locking."

Human designer judgment. The pilot was measured hard on purpose: evidence, not opinion, was going to move the rollout date.

Artifacts produced. Pilot results, survey dataset (Section 5, A7), completion-marking verification.

4.7 Measurement — the scale decision

Goal. Convert pilot evidence into a rollout decision.

Method. The pilot results went to the franchisee education forum as a closed loop: the same numbers that built the business case now proved the design. The expedite request for the market-A rollout was approved on their strength — recorded internally as a decisive win. National go-live followed at the start of the next quarter, with rollout calls across the network, the annual recertification launched on the dashboard, and an extended support window across business hours absorbing teething issues. The roadmap committed beyond launch: a live equipment registry, next-generation skills assessments, live events — and on the horizon, AI-powered search, smarter learning tools, improved mobile, coaching tools.

06 · Use cases

Use cases

Use case A — New crew onboarding through the age gate

Scenario. A young crew member joins a restaurant. Before the redesign, day one showed every course the network owned — including equipment and age-restricted training they were not allowed to touch — and a manager had to tell them what to do next. After the redesign, only the assigned onboarding path is visible; induction is locked until the role is assigned; day-2 modules unlock after a 24-hour wait; station training unlocks only after induction; age-restricted learning sits behind a confirmation gate; equipment certification unlocks only after fundamentals.

Outcome. The locking was the most-praised feature in the open text. Every surveyed learner could complete training without asking a manager what to do next (44% strongly), and 100% agreed the platform was easier to navigate.

Use case B — Equipment certification matched to the store

Scenario. Kitchen crew were offered training on all three cooker types and all three fryer types regardless of what their restaurant actually had. The result, stated plainly in the business case: staff were certified on equipment the store did not have, or operating equipment without certification at all.

What was designed. A split of the kitchen path into fundamentals and open-procedures; simplified, sequential equipment selection as an interim; and a new open-procedures certification badge inserted as a prerequisite — closing the loophole where crew could earn the supervisor-trained badge without open-procedures certification. The committed permanent fix is a live equipment registry: training auto-targeted from the store's real equipment list, so there is no more selecting equipment at all.

Outcome. Certification integrity restored; the wrong-equipment class of ticket designed out of existence.

Use case C — Certification lifecycle and the annual recertification

Scenario. Badges were irrevocable once awarded; there was no audit trail for post-incident recertification; food-safety and first-aid certifications were not tracked to expiry; and the annual recertification cycle crashed the system every year — a dependable ticket spike.

What was designed. The certification lifecycle as a designed object: issue, evidence, recertification, and audit. Expiry tracking for legislative certifications; evidence uploads supported within the platform; and the recertification cycle launched cleanly on top of the new dashboard the moment it went live.

Outcome. The annual recertification ran on schedule without a system failure, and every badge now carries an audit trail.

Use case D — Manager oversight without the manager as the system

Scenario. Advanced reporting was limited to a single person per restaurant, so managers ran their teams on memory. The redesign treated oversight as a first-class capability: a team view for all managers, advanced reporting extended to restaurant general managers and area leaders, due and overdue indicators with notifications ("only 14 days to complete"), and cross-portal completion marking validated with franchisee-specific test groups.

Outcome. The open-text verdict, in paraphrase: less time for managers telling team members what to do — management time returned to the floor.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. This engagement ran on conventional tooling, but every method in it is encodable for agent execution — which is why the work survived as SOPs rather than tribal knowledge. Three SOPs follow the pattern used across this site: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Support-cost model from ticket data (business case). Output: a monthly cost model with annualized totals. Acceptance: every figure traces to a ticket count; exclusions stated as a block; the model understates. Gate: sponsor sign-off before options analysis. The prompt template:
NOTE
You are SUPPORT-MODELER. Input: a ticketing-system export (trailing 12 months)
and an hourly cost basis. Output a cost model with monthly rows:
- month, tickets, escalated_share, hours, cost
RULES:
- hours = tickets x hours_per_ticket_avg; state the hours basis in the model
- escalated tickets sit on a separate line, never merged into the base rate
- list exclusions explicitly (manager time, escalation time, multi-issue
  tickets) as a "stated exclusions" block; the model must UNDERSTATE
- no fabricated rows: every figure traces to a ticket count
ACCEPTANCE: annualized totals; exclusions block present; source column
on every row. GATE: sponsor sign-off before options analysis.
  1. Multi-system scoping workshop (mapping). Steps: name every system that touches the journey; give each a swim lane; walk the lifecycle stage by stage; flag fail points with evidence; run the "disappeared tomorrow" target-state exercise; capture requirements in three groups (learners; managers and administrators; platform and compliance); write every output to one spreadsheet by end of day; close with next-two-weeks owners. Norms: alignment over perfection; brutal honesty; momentum over perfection. Gate: the blueprint is rehearsed with zero open gaps before build.
  2. Journey-gate configuration (design). The linear journey spec as code — sequence, locks, age gates, prerequisite badges, progressive disclosure — exactly as shown in Section 4.4. Acceptance: every gate traces to a risk row or a lost feature in the registers; nothing unlocks before its prerequisite. Gate: human approval for every gate that touches compliance.

The meta-SOP. The engagement ran business case → workshop → build → pilot → rollout, with hard gates between stages: no build before the workshop blueprint; no pilot before acceptance testing; no rollout before pilot thresholds were met; pilot metrics mirror business-case metrics. Process was enforced by named owners and due dates in the spreadsheet, not by memory.

Guardrails.

  • No real employee data in prompts. Agents work on counts, aggregated ticket data, and sample datasets; the sample CSV ships fixed-seed.
  • Figure traceability. Every number in the pilot readout is generated from the same dataset — the survey percentages and ticket counts come from the tracked sample, never hand-typed.
  • Human sign-off points. Sponsor approval of the cost model; the workshop blueprint; pilot go/no-go; the scale decision; every compliance gate.
  • Hallucination control. A ticket count that does not trace to a source row fails acceptance; a survey percentage not in the dataset fails the readout.

Failure modes observed. (1) Sandbox administrator access blocked independent build, test, and development — an external dependency that should have been escalated at week one. (2) Linear-progression limits inside the platform forced splitting one academy across multiple learning experiences — a platform constraint that shaped the content architecture. (3) Age data absent from every system forced a custom field plus learner self-confirmation — an interim gate owned openly until the data fix lands. (4) Timing pressure: the pilot sat over a peak promotional period, and the annual recertification overlapped the rollout — change stacked on change, absorbed by the extended support window. (5) The prior migration had lost evidence uploads; the mitigation was reusing existing learning experiences rather than re-authoring from scratch.

Causal logic (Service Profit Chain). The chain ran as Heskett, Sasser, and Schlesinger describe (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md): internal service quality — one platform, one data source, reliable sign-in — raised frontline capability; managers began coaching instead of administering, and learners began self-directing; external service value rose through the denominator of the value equation, with access costs collapsing (no more hunting through the content wall, no more waiting for a manager to say what's next); and the economics followed: support burden removed, failure demand gone, faster certification, better recertification compliance.

KPI tree (abridged). North star: every learner certified on the right learning at the right time, provably. Drivers: gating correctness (age gates, lock sequencing, prerequisite badges), completion-marking accuracy, journey completion, self-direction. Lagging: support tickets, certification integrity, audit readiness, management time on learning administration.

Roadmap value. The strategic asset is the platform's improvable spine: phase 2 (live equipment registry, next-generation skills assessments, live events) and the ideas horizon (AI-powered search, smarter learning tools, improved mobile, coaching tools) — a system that keeps improving rather than standing still.

09 · AI tools

AI tool playbook

Honest framing first. No AI tools were deployed in this engagement; the platform's AI-powered search sits on the post-launch roadmap, and claiming otherwise would be fabrication. What follows is the method playbook the work produced, written in the form this site uses for agent execution: each method, what an agent version would do, and what stays human. The commands are examples for agent execution.

Multi-system swimlane blueprinting. The engagement's signature visual — one lane per system across the learner lifecycle. An agent version reads the ticketing export and proposes the lane-by-stage matrix; the human chooses the line of visibility and adjudicates evidence: the agent's value is exhaustive coverage, the human's judgment about what the matrix means.

TERMINAL
agent run --agent blueprint-auditor "Read support-tickets.csv. Group tickets
by system lane (HR, identity, front-end, back-end, recruiting, people) and by
lifecycle stage (user created, login, navigate, enrol, launch, complete,
report). Output a lane x stage matrix with ticket counts and the top failure
mode per cell. Flag cells with no tickets - those may be hidden failures,
not successes."

Target-state visioning. The "if the legacy front-end disappeared tomorrow" exercise is human-run in the room; an agent can prepare the inventory of what the exercise must not lose (every capability with a legal or safety consequence) and challenge the result for gaps.

Requirements by user group. Three groups — learners; managers and administrators; platform and compliance. An agent clusters requirements by group and role; the human adjudicates conflicts, because learner-group and compliance-group requirements frequently collide — that collision is the design problem, not a data problem.

Linear-journey progressive disclosure. Lock sequencing, assigned-only focus areas, due and overdue indicators, the show-completed toggle. An agent proposes sequences from the prerequisite graph; the human approves every gate that touches compliance, because a gate that fails closed costs certification throughput and one that fails open costs safety.

Prerequisite badging. The open-procedures badge closed a real loophole: the supervisor-trained badge was earnable without its intended prerequisite. This is a graph-audit problem an agent excels at:

TERMINAL
agent run --agent badge-graph "Load the badge and prerequisite definitions.
For each badge, compute the set of certifications achievable without completing
each named prerequisite. List every badge whose prerequisite can be bypassed
(for example, supervisor-trained awarded without open-procedures
certification). Be adversarial: include paths through partial credit."

Interim gates while the data fix lands. The age-confirmation gate and simplified equipment selection were deliberate stopgaps, owned openly until the permanent fix (a data-fed age field; a live equipment registry). An agent's job is to flag when an interim becomes permanent — the quiet failure this method exists to prevent.

Extended support as change absorption. The rollout's extended support window covered business hours and routed through leader, coach, and manager calls. An agent version triages and routes during the window; the human holds the escalation line.

Continuity framing. "What's changing is the learning experience; what's staying the same is the learning itself." An agent drafts the what's-changing-versus-staying-the-same comparison from the plan; the human owns the message — framing is political and cannot be delegated.

Journey gates as configuration. The full gate spec from Section 4.4 is the config file an agent would load, validate, and diff across markets.

10 · Lessons

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • Frame change as continuity. "What's changing is the learning experience; what's staying the same is the learning itself" defused the restart-from-scratch fear across the franchise network.
  • Fix the job-to-be-done, not the tool. This was never just a migration of content: consolidation and pathway mapping happened before cutover, which is why the launch landed on redesigned journeys rather than a faster warehouse.
  • Progressive disclosure is a compliance tool, not a usability nicety. Lock sequencing, assigned-only focus areas, and the show-completed toggle resolved safety problems — wrong-order training, wrong-equipment certification, age-restricted access — not just confusion.
  • Design badges from certification outcomes, not module completions. The open-procedures badge closed a loophole where the supervisor-trained badge was earnable without its prerequisite. Badge graphs deserve an adversarial audit before launch.
  • Staged compliance honesty. Interim gates delivered now, permanent fixes committed publicly: age confirmation while the data field lands; simplified equipment selection while the live registry is built.
  • Measure the pilot hard, so evidence — not opinion — moves the date. Tickets, marking accuracy, and a short agree-scale survey produced the board-level evidence that accelerated the national rollout — the closed loop from quantified problem to quantified proof.
  • Sequence the engagement ladder. Leadership-forum demos → coach check-ins → roadshow demos → expression-of-interest recruitment → governance updates; momentum over perfection, the workshop norm held for the whole program.
  • Nail function before aesthetics. The dashboard spec iterated only after flows worked; the UI was honestly labeled work in progress until it wasn't.
  • Pitfall — external dependencies surfaced late. Limited sandbox administrator access slowed independent build and test for weeks. Escalate external blockers at week one, not when they bite.
  • Pitfall — platform constraints shape architecture. Linear-progression limits forced splitting one academy across multiple learning experiences; design the content architecture around the platform's real capabilities, validated with the vendor.
  • Pitfall — change stacked on change. The pilot sat over a peak promotional period, and the annual recertification overlapped the rollout. The extended support window absorbed it; plan for overlap, don't pray it away.
  • Pitfall — prior migrations lose data. Evidence uploads had been lost in an earlier migration; reusing existing learning experiences mitigated the risk, and the audit trail was rebuilt forward from launch.
