SOPs as Code: The AI Execution Playbook

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

This is a framework article, not a single-client case. The proof points come from fictional composite engagements already published in this series; names, figures, quotes, datasets, and operational details remain illustrative and anonymized. What is real is the method: an AI-first service design practice in which Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub are used as the primary execution environment, while humans keep ownership of ethics, framing, and final judgment.

1. Executive summary

Service design methods were written for people, not for machines. That is why the same prompt can yield one excellent journey map and one persuasive hallucination: the method lives partly in the book, partly in the facilitator's habits, and partly in unspoken review norms. SOPs as code close that gap. They turn method prose into an executable contract: context and principles, numbered steps, output schema, acceptance criteria, evidence rules, review gates, and named ownership. Across the proof points in this series — telehealth onboarding, research ops at 200-interview scale, disruption journey mapping, public-sector blueprinting, and behaviour-change services — the pattern is consistent: once the SOP is machine-checkable, AI becomes reliable enough to accelerate the work without flattening its rigor. This article defines the playbook, the reusable SOP catalogue, the Git-and-CI governance model, and the reviewer loop required to make service-design methods safely executable by agents.

2. The brief

Client portrait

The "client" in this article is the service-design practice itself: a small cross-sector team delivering research, synthesis, mapping, prototyping, and operating-model work for public, health, financial, and B2B services. The practice had already proven that AI could accelerate individual tasks. What it had not yet solved was reproducibility. One designer could run a transcript-to-evidence routine brilliantly; another would over-prompt and produce elegant but unsafe summaries. One map reviewer would reject orphan evidence; another would let plausible rows slide. The operating question became institutional rather than technical: how does a service-design team make good execution repeatable across people, clients, and models?

The challenge

Method books describe what to do, when to do it, and what artifact should result. They do not define what an AI agent must refuse to invent, what fields are mandatory, what evidence is sufficient, or what gate must block a merge. In practice, that gap creates five recurring failure modes. First, prose-only methods produce stylistically polished but structurally unreliable outputs. Second, hidden facilitator judgment means the artifact quality depends on which human happened to brief the agent. Third, weak handoffs break traceability from observation to insight to blueprint step, exactly the problem Kumar warns against when he argues for end-to-end decision traceability (See: 17-101-design-methods/09-using-the-methods.md). Fourth, over-specified prompts become brittle when the service context changes. Fifth, governance lag means teams discover defects in steering meetings rather than in CI.

Success criteria

Criterion Target
Reusable SOP coverage 25+ named SOPs spanning research to report pack

Constraints

  • Cross-sector portability: the same library had to work across public services, telehealth, banking, and B2B operations without becoming vague.
  • Low design-ops overhead: one designer and one part-time design-ops owner had to maintain it.
  • Regulation-aware: health, public-sector, and financial cases require explicit evidence and review boundaries.
  • Tool realism: the playbook had to work with plain GitHub, markdown, YAML, JSON Schema, CSV, and Mermaid — boring tools by design.
  • Series continuity: proof points had to remain consistent with prior articles, especially the anchor system in ../01/README.md, the transcript discipline in ../02/README.md, the research pipeline in ../06/README.md, the journey-map guards in ../09/README.md, the blueprint factory in ../11/README.md, and the behaviour-change governance pattern in ../19/README.md.

Why service design

Service design is already a method system: iterative, non-linear, and artifact-driven. The four process activities — research, ideation, prototyping, implementation — recur as loops, not a waterfall (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/03-the-service-design-process.md). Research quality depends on triangulation and ethics (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md). Prototype quality depends on explicit learning questions, progressive fidelity, and simultaneous frontstage/backstage rehearsal (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/06-prototyping-methods.md). Kimbell's toolkit clarifies that templates are boundary objects, not bureaucracy: they let different disciplines reason together over one artifact (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/07-the-toolkit-templates.md). The move here is simply to finish the job for AI: if a template is already a cognitive scaffold for humans, make it executable for agents too.

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

The meta-problem was treated as a service-design engagement on the practice itself. We studied where previous articles succeeded or drifted, encoded the recurring method patterns into SOPs, tested them on fixed fixtures and live pilot runs, then governed them as a library. The process kept article 01's 4D spine, but added a deeper five-gate control model.

Mapped explicitly: Discover = TISDD Research + Kumar Modes 2 and 3, Define = reframing and method selection around Mode 4, Develop = TISDD Ideation plus Prototyping across Modes 5 and 6, and Deliver = TISDD Implementation in Mode 7 with governance feedback into the next loop. The diagram reads left to right, but the gate model keeps the process non-linear in practice.

The five gates were more prescriptive than the ones in article 01:

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Discover — prove where prose fails and structure helps

Goal. Identify the recurring method moves, the recurring failure modes, and the minimum executable contract needed to make them reliable.

Method & SOP. We built a Decision Traceability Log across the series, exactly in Kumar's sense: pick the delivered artifact, walk backward to the method, then backward again to the observation and rule that justified it (See: 17-101-design-methods/09-using-the-methods.md). The log revealed that high-performing articles already behaved like SOP systems. Article 02's transcript-to-evidence flow enforced verbatim quotes. Article 06 converted per-interview processing into a five-stage pipeline. Article 09 defined journey phases before tagging. Article 11 refused a blueprint row without evidence. Article 19 split content generation from claim approval.

AI execution. Claude Code acted as the pattern miner across the article corpus, returning one structured record per candidate SOP: method name, proof points, repeated prompt pattern, output form, failure class, and candidate tests. Representative prompt:

Read articles/01, 02, 06, 09, 11, 19. Extract any repeated service-design
execution pattern that appears in at least two articles. For each pattern,
return: name, triggering condition, required inputs, output artifact, explicit
guardrails, failure mode observed, and article proof citations. Output JSON.

Codex then challenged the result, specifically hunting fake generalization:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read sop_candidates.json. For each candidate,
check whether the cited articles truly share the same method contract or merely
surface language. Reject any candidate whose steps or output artifact differ in
kind. Return accepted candidates, merges, and false merges with reasons."

GitHub captured each accepted SOP as an issue with labels discover, candidate-sop, and the future owning phase. This mattered: the library was never "written later"; it was assembled as auditable work from day one.

Human designer judgment. The designer decided which parts of service design must remain non-delegable. Interviewing, consent handling, stakeholder diplomacy, politically sensitive reframing, naming the final insight, and go/no-go decisions stayed human. The SOP library was designed to support those decisions, not replace them.

Artifact produced. The first-pass SOP inventory and failure log: 31 candidates, 26 accepted as reusable, 5 kept local because they were too context-bound.

4.2 Define — encode the anatomy of an AI-executable SOP

Goal. Turn repeated good practice into one library contract: readable by humans, strict enough for machines.

Method & SOP. Kimbell's field-by-field template discipline was the direct model here: every field exists because a downstream team needs it, and unverified claims must be marked rather than smuggled through (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/07-the-toolkit-templates.md). The resulting anatomy had eight required parts: context and principles, trigger, inputs, steps, output schema, acceptance criteria, evidence rules, review gate, plus ownership metadata.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the initial YAML contract. OpenCode generated JSON Schemas and fixture skeletons so each SOP could be tested mechanically. Representative command:

opencode run --agent build "Read sop_catalogue.csv. For each SOP row, scaffold
schemas/<sop_id>.schema.json and tests/fixtures/<sop_id>/README.md. Use output
field names from the catalogue exactly. Mark unknown constraints TODO_FOR_HUMAN."

Codex was used narrowly: not to write the SOP, but to challenge whether the fields truly controlled risk. It repeatedly found two omissions in early drafts: missing provenance rules for numerical claims, and missing ownership fields for change approval.

Human designer judgment. The crucial judgment was resisting two opposite errors. One is the prose SOP — easy to read, impossible to enforce. The other is the brittle SOP — every sentence locked so tightly that the agent cannot adapt to a new service context. The final structure fixed the non-negotiables and left room only in evidence-backed interpretation.

Artifact produced. The v1 annotated SOP schema, ownership model, and naming convention.

4.3 Develop — test the library like a service prototype

Goal. Prove that the SOPs can survive both fixed fixtures and real project conditions.

Method & SOP. We treated the library as a prototype, in the exact sense of This Is Service Design Doing: hypothesis first, low-cost test, explicit failure signals, simultaneous frontstage and backstage rehearsal (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/06-prototyping-methods.md). Fixed fixtures answered, "Does the SOP behave deterministically?" Live runs answered, "Does it behave usefully in real project mess?"

AI execution. Claude Code ran the live SOP invocations; OpenCode generated synthetic-but-structured fixture inputs; GitHub Actions executed schema checks, quote checks, and Mermaid lint; Codex reviewed deltas when a test broke after a prompt or schema revision. The flagship worked example was the BlueprintBuild SOP from article 11. Representative orchestration prompt:

Execute SOP-BP-CORE exactly. Inputs: validated journey spine, evidence register,
lane dictionary, and cost file. Produce blueprint rows JSON and markdown render.
Run self-check against acceptance criteria. If a node lacks evidence, output GAP.
Do not invent filler to satisfy lane minimums.

Human designer judgment. The designer interpreted failures by class. If a fixture failed because the prompt encouraged smooth language, the prompt was tightened. If it failed because the agent needed information not present in the schema, the schema changed. If it failed because the method itself depended on local judgment that had not been formalized, the SOP gained a human gate instead of more instructions.

Artifact produced. The BlueprintBuild worked example, prompt-regression fixtures, acceptance tests, and the sample telemetry set in A7.

4.4 Deliver — publish, govern, and institutionalize

Goal. Make the library durable enough to outlast the original author.

Method & SOP. This phase borrowed directly from governance-and-culture work: a central practice office supplies standards and coaching, journey owners or method owners hold decision rights, and proactive review gates stop bad changes before release (See: 14-outside-in/07-governance-and-culture.md; 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/08-organizing-for-service-innovation.md). In other words, the SOP library became a service with owners, release rituals, support, and telemetry.

AI execution. GitHub was the operating backbone. Every SOP changed through PRs. CODEOWNERS routed review. Actions ran lint, schema validation, fixture tests, and Mermaid parse checks. Claude Code drafted release notes from merged changes. Representative workflow excerpt:

name: sop-library-ci
on: [pull_request]
jobs:
  validate:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - run: node scripts/validate-mermaid.mjs articles/29
      - run: python scripts/validate_schemas.py sop/
      - run: pytest tests/sop_regression -q

Human designer judgment. Two governance decisions mattered most. First, every SOP received three named roles: creator (authors content), reviewer (tests method integrity), approver (owns production risk). Second, some steps were explicitly declared non-runnable by AI: live interviewing, consent negotiation, politically sensitive prioritization, claims approval in health contexts, and final pilot go/no-go.

Artifact produced. SOP library v1.0, release governance, training plan, telemetry review cadence.

A6 — Templates and reusable SOP assets

A6a — Annotated SOP-as-code schema template

# Every SOP is one versioned file in sop/<sop_id>.yaml
sop_id: SOP-XXX-000                # Stable id. Never reuse after retirement.
name: Descriptive method name      # Human-readable title.
version: 1.0.0                     # Semantic version. Major means contract break.
phase: discover                    # discover | define | develop | deliver | cross_cutting
purpose: >-
  One sentence on what decision this SOP helps the team make.
source_method:
  book: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md  # Canonical basis
  article_proof_points:                                         # At least 2 if reusable
    - ../02/README.md
    - ../06/README.md
context_and_principles:
  - work from evidence before interpretation
  - mark uncertainty explicitly rather than smoothing it over
  - documented SOP is evidence of intent, not of live practice
trigger_when:
  - clear event that starts the SOP run
not_for_when:
  - live interviewing
  - executive prioritization under political sensitivity
inputs:
  - name: evidence_register
    path_pattern: research/*.jsonl
    required: true
  - name: rules_file
    path_pattern: config/*.yaml
    required: true
steps:
  - id: step_1
    actor: agent                       # agent | human | shared
    instruction: exact action to perform
    output_field: named result produced by this step
  - id: step_2
    actor: agent
    instruction: next action
output:
  format: json                         # json | markdown | csv | mermaid | mixed
  schema: schemas/example.schema.json # Machine-checkable contract
acceptance_criteria:
  - every required field validates against schema
  - every non-obvious claim cites evidence_id or source_ref
  - every quote is verbatim-verifiable when quote fields exist
  - unknowns are marked NO_EVIDENCE or UNVERIFIED, never invented
evidence_rules:
  quote_verification: exact_match_required
  numeric_provenance: source_and_denominator_required
  method_provenance: cite_book_and_rule_file
review_gate:
  creator: lead_designer
  reviewer: domain_reviewer_or_critic
  approver: journey_owner_or_risk_owner
  automated_checks:
    - schema_validate
    - evidence_gate
    - mermaid_lint_if_present
    - pii_scan_if_applicable
failure_modes:
  - prose_only_sop_causes_interpretive_drift
  - over_specified_prompt_causes_brittle_edge_case_failures
  - unlabeled_human_judgment_hides_unreviewed_risk
telemetry_fields:
  - sop_id
  - run_id
  - pass_fail
  - artifact_schema_ok
  - evidence_gate_ok
  - human_review_min
ownership:
  method_owner: service_design_practice
  review_cadence: monthly
  retirement_rule: retire after 3 superseding releases or 90 days unused

A6c — Worked example: BlueprintBuild SOP from method prose to running code

YAML

sop_id: SOP-BP-CORE
name: BlueprintBuild from validated journey spine
version: 1.2.0
phase: define
purpose: Build one service blueprint whose rows are evidence-gated and operationally reviewable.
source_method:
  book: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/07-the-toolkit-templates.md
  article_proof_points:
    - ../11/README.md
    - ../02/README.md
context_and_principles:
  - start from the customer action spine
  - one blueprint row is an evidence-backed claim about practice
  - documented SOP alone is evidence of intent, never proof of practice
trigger_when:
  - validated_journey_spine_exists
not_for_when:
  - no journey spine has been signed off
  - lane evidence is missing for more than half the columns
inputs:
  - name: journey_spine
    required: true
    shape: markdown rows with stable column ids
  - name: evidence_register
    required: true
    shape: jsonl evidence items with source refs
  - name: lane_dictionary
    required: true
    shape: markdown table of acceptable evidence per lane
  - name: cost_volume_file
    required: false
    shape: csv or markdown table
steps:
  - id: load_spine
    actor: agent
    instruction: read customer actions and treat each as a column anchor
  - id: build_lane_rows
    actor: agent
    instruction: propose one row per lane per relevant column with evidence ids
  - id: quantify_waits_and_fails
    actor: agent
    instruction: attach W durations and F frequencies only when evidence supports them
  - id: mark_crossings
    actor: agent
    instruction: identify visibility and internal interaction crossings
  - id: render_outputs
    actor: agent
    instruction: emit JSON rows and markdown mermaid render
  - id: reviewer_walkthrough
    actor: human
    instruction: walk printed blueprint line by line and dispute any unsupported row
output:
  format: mixed
  schema: schemas/blueprint_row.schema.json
acceptance_criteria:
  - every status OK row has at least one evidence_id
  - every fail point has a numeric frequency or status NEEDS_QUANT
  - every wait point has a numeric duration or status NEEDS_QUANT
  - no row contains resident identifying data
  - mermaid render parses
  - disputed rows cannot merge without comment resolution
evidence_rules:
  quote_verification: if quote field present it must resolve verbatim
  numeric_provenance: frequency and duration need source_ref and denominator
  practice_rule: documented policy cannot be sole support for status OK
review_gate:
  creator: mapper_agent
  reviewer: critic_and_domain_panel
  approver: service_owner
  automated_checks:

A10 — Good Services audit of the SOP library

Principle Before prose-led practice After SOP-as-code library Evidence
1. Is easy to find Partial Pass Registry, stable IDs, and naming convention make reusable SOPs discoverable at the moment of need
2. Clearly explains its purpose Partial Pass Mandatory purpose, trigger, and scope fields state what each SOP is for and when to use it
3. Sets a user's expectations Fail Pass Acceptance criteria, inputs, outputs, and review gates are visible before a run starts
4. Enables each user to complete the outcome they set out to do Partial Pass Each SOP is linked to a specific artifact or decision outcome rather than generic prompting
5. Works in a way that is familiar Fail Pass Shared markdown, YAML, JSON Schema, CSV, and Mermaid patterns reduce relearning between SOPs
6. Requires no prior knowledge to use Fail Partial Worked examples and field instructions help, but good use still assumes some service-design literacy
7. Is agnostic of organizational structures Fail Pass The library lets one execution path span designers, reviewers, Codex, Claude Code, OpenCode, and GitHub without exposing internal handoff complexity
8. Requires the minimum possible steps to complete Fail Pass Schemas, fixtures, and default templates cut re-prompting, duplicate review, and manual cleanup
9. Is consistent throughout Fail Pass Shared schemas, fixtures, naming rules, and CI gates keep artifact logic and review standards aligned across the library
10. Has no dead ends Fail Partial GAP states, rejection reasons, and review gates prevent silent failure, but escalation still depends on human triage quality
11. Is usable by everyone, equally Partial Partial Explicit review roles and plain-text artifacts widen access, but some SOPs still rely on expert judgment and CLI comfort
12. Responds to change quickly Partial Pass Semantic versioning, telemetry review, and fixture regression make safe updates fast
13. Clearly explains why a decision has been made Fail Pass Evidence rules, rejection reasons, and traceability fields show why a run passed, failed, or changed
14. Makes it easy to get human assistance Partial Pass Creator, reviewer, and approver roles give every SOP a named human escalation path
15. Requires the right knowledge from the user Fail Pass SOP inputs ask for service facts, evidence, and decisions the team can actually supply instead of hidden method lore

6. Use cases

Use case A — A 200-interview research programme run entirely off SOPs

Scenario. Calder Community Bank's research operation in article 06 already proved the scale point: 200 interviews, 4,826 verified evidence items, and a 36-hour SLA from interview to structured output (See: ../06/README.md). The playbook here explains why that worked: the programme was never "AI on transcripts" in the abstract. It was a chain of SOPs — consent-state machine, pseudonymization, transcript-to-evidence, codebook application, verification, synthesis, validation.

What was designed. A research-to-evidence pipeline in which humans handled recruitment, interviewing, and ethics, while agents handled the transcript-facing work only after pseudonymization. Each step produced one schema-bound artifact for the next. The crucial move was the verify stage. Without it, 41 fabricated or merged quotes would have polluted the register in article 06.

AI working session summary. Claude Code ran per-interview extraction and verification. OpenCode handled batch ingestion and PII quarantine. Codex reviewed the validation sample and challenged over-splitting in the themes. Sample dispatch:

Execute SOP-EVD-007 on INT-088. Return JSONL only. If a quote cannot be copied
exactly with offsets, drop it. Then run SOP-EVV-009 and produce accepted items
plus rejection reasons.

Outcome. Sponsor trust rose because the review conversation changed from "Do we believe the quotes?" to "What do the quotes imply?" That is the economic value of SOPs as code in research: they turn credibility from a meeting-room debate into a pipeline property.

Use case B — A blueprint factory for a public authority

Scenario. Fernwood Council's housing-repairs work in article 11 exposed the public-sector version of the same problem: blueprints had previously been murals, not diagnostics. The service handled 28,000 repair requests a year, and 41% of open jobs triggered at least one status-chase contact — about 3,100 calls a month (See: ../11/README.md).

What was designed. The BlueprintBuild SOP became a factory pattern for the council's other journeys: waste missed collections, disabled parking permits, and damp-and-mould escalation. The reusable unit was not the diagram style. It was the method contract: validated spine first; evidence-per-row; fail points quantified; policy alone never enough; workshop disputes become PR comments, not oral folklore.

AI working session summary. Claude Code ran lane builders and linker agents. Codex audited attribution and cost math. GitHub enforced the PR checklist. OpenCode generated event-sequence samples to fill quantitative gaps. The library reduced time-to-first-diagnostic blueprint because each new journey reused the same controls.

Outcome. The public authority gained a repeatable operating capability, not just one strong artifact. The visible change was faster blueprint production. The more important change was that operational managers could challenge a row by pointing to the rule they thought was broken.

Use case C — Same SOP, three teams, visible quality variance

Scenario. A cross-team pilot ran the same core SOP family across Team Alpha, Team Beta, and Team Gamma using the sample telemetry in A7. The question was not whether the SOP existed; it was whether the practice had encoded enough support for teams with different experience levels.

What was designed. One shared registry, one reviewer rubric, one telemetry schema, and one release cadence. Team Gamma received explicit fixture walkthroughs before live runs; Alpha and Beta did not.

AI working session summary. GitHub collected identical fields from all three teams. Claude Code generated weekly readouts from the telemetry file. Codex compared failure patterns rather than overall pass/fail only.

Outcome. In the illustrative sample, Gamma reached 100% blueprint-pass rate across its two recorded blueprint runs, while Alpha and Beta each reached 50% across theirs. That was not a team talent ranking; it was a library finding. Gamma had the clearest onboarding and pre-run fixture rehearsal. The SOP change that followed was simple: no team could run a live BlueprintBuild invocation before passing the minimal fixture pack once. That is the deeper point of SOP telemetry: it improves the procedure, not just the model prompt.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Why SOPs are the bridge

Service-design method prose tells a capable human how to think. AI needs to know what to emit, what to refuse, and what must be checked by something stricter than style. SOPs are the bridge because they preserve the human logic of the method while adding the machine-checkable tail. Article 02 proved this with transcript evidence. Article 09 proved it with journey-map lint. Article 11 proved it with evidence-per-row blueprinting. Article 19 proved it with claims governance in a sensitive domain. Across all four, the winning pattern is the same: context and principles → steps → schema → acceptance → evidence rules → review gate → ownership.

The anatomy of an AI-executable SOP

Part Why it exists If missing
Context and principles Stops literal but foolish execution Agent satisfies steps while violating method intent
Trigger and not-for conditions Defines scope boundary SOP runs in the wrong moment
Inputs Makes required evidence explicit Agent silently fills gaps
Numbered steps Preserves the method sequence Artifacts become style-driven
Output schema Constrains form Prose sprawl replaces structured output
Acceptance criteria Converts quality into pass/fail checks Review becomes taste
Evidence rules Controls hallucination and provenance Quotes and numbers drift
Review gate Defines who must look and when Agent output merges itself culturally
Ownership Makes the library durable SOPs decay into copied prompt snippets

The meta-SOP of the whole service-design arc

This is article 01's operating system made more prescriptive. The human designer still loops backward when a failure reveals a deeper uncertainty, exactly as Kumar's non-linear navigation demands (See: 17-101-design-methods/09-using-the-methods.md). The difference is that the reason for looping back is now explicit. If concepts feel generic, return to synthesis. If blueprint rows fight each other, return to journey or evidence rules. If a pilot looks busy but inconclusive, return to the prototype hypothesis rather than polishing the deck.

How to version and test SOPs

Treat SOPs like production assets.

  1. Version in Git. Every SOP is a file. Every change is diffable. Major versions break the contract; minor versions tighten the rules without changing fields.
  2. Run CI. Minimum checks: Mermaid parse, schema validation, evidence gate, and prompt-regression fixtures on fixed inputs.
  3. Use telemetry. A good SOP lowers human review time without raising escaped defects.
  4. Test prompts on fixtures, not vibes. Fixed fixtures stop a "clever improvement" from breaking a previously safe behavior.
  5. Retire aggressively. A duplicate or unused SOP is design debt.

Prompt-lint is the static check before regression. Each SOP prompt must name the exact schema, the allowed evidence sources, the explicit uncertainty token (NO_EVIDENCE, UNVERIFIED, or equivalent), and the stop condition for sensitive material. A prompt that says "be rigorous" fails lint; a prompt that says "if copy enters medical territory, emit CLINICAL_REVIEW_REQUIRED and stop" passes.

The reviewer-refinement loop is part of the SOP, not an afterthought. A live run should follow one cycle: agent run -> critic findings -> targeted fix to prompt, schema, or rule file -> re-run on the same fixture -> only then run on live material. The BlueprintBuild example in A6c was stabilized exactly this way: first the reviewer flagged policy-only evidence, then the practice_rule field was added, then the same fixture was re-run to prove the class of error had closed.

Representative fixed-fixture test command:

pytest tests/sop_regression/test_blueprint_core.py -q

Representative library release command:

gh pr create --title "sop: bump blueprint core to v1.2.0" \
  --body "Adds practice_rule and numeric provenance gate; fixture pack green."

Human in the loop gates and when AI should not run the step

Role Responsibility Typical moment
Creator Writes or revises the SOP contract During method encoding
Reviewer Tests the method integrity against fixtures and live evidence Before merge or release
Approver Owns production risk in the operating context Before publishing or using in a live pilot

AI should not run the step when the work depends on live consent, political trade-off, clinical or legal judgment, stakeholder relationship repair, or ethical accountability that cannot be reduced to explicit acceptance criteria. In those cases the SOP should still exist — but as a human-run checklist, not an autonomous agent run.

9. AI tool playbook

Claude Code — orchestrator and artifact author. Role: executes schema-bound SOPs, coordinates subagents, drafts artifacts and release notes, and keeps the cross-file narrative coherent. Example use: claude "Execute SOP-JRN-013 against evidence/register.jsonl and phases.yaml. Return markdown and mermaid only." Excelled at: long structured outputs under explicit schemas. Failed at: guessing when a human boundary should apply unless the SOP said so. Human took over for: framing, facilitation, and politically sensitive prioritization.

OpenCode — data and fixture engineer. Role: pseudonymization, CSV generation, fixture scaffolding, schema boilerplate, small data transforms, and telemetry summaries. Example use: opencode run --agent build "Create fixed regression fixtures for SOP-BP-CORE from the minimal reschedule case." Excelled at: repetitive pipeline work and mock-data generation. Failed at: method interpretation without tight field definitions. Human took over for: deciding what counts as evidence.

Codex — adversarial reviewer. Role: challenge false merges between methods, verify taxonomy names, question unsafe or unsupported claims, and audit whether a proposed generalization is real. Example use: codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Audit the new SOP against its cited proof points and list any unsupported abstractions." Excelled at: skeptical second-pass review. Failed at: sometimes proposing stricter abstractions than the context needed. Human took over for: deciding whether a failure required a prompt fix, schema fix, or human gate.

GitHub — the control plane. Role: issues for candidate SOPs, PRs for change review, CODEOWNERS for approvals, Actions for validation, Projects for release tracking. Example use: PR template requiring creator, reviewer, approver, version bump, telemetry note, and fixture result. Excelled at: institutional memory and boring consistency. Failed at: nothing by itself; it only works if the practice actually routes all consequential work through it.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  1. A prompt is not an SOP. Good prompts can look stable for weeks before a new context exposes the hidden assumptions.
  2. The most valuable field is often the boring one. approver, source_ref, and review_cadence did more for quality than any eloquent system prompt text.
  3. Fixture tests prevent culture from becoming folklore. Once a good reviewer judgment is encoded as a fixture, the whole practice keeps it.
  4. Over-standardization is a real risk. If the SOP dictates language style instead of evidence discipline, teams spend time fighting the library rather than using it.
  5. Telemetry beats anecdotes about AI quality. The cross-team variance case only became visible because the runs were logged the same way.
  6. Human judgment has to be named, not implied. If the method needs a person, the SOP should say which person and what decision they own.
  7. Governance is part of the design. Without owners, release rules, and review gates, the library becomes a folder of admired templates.
