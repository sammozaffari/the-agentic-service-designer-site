Ideation & Concept Development at Speed

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Calder Community Bank is a fictional composite of several European regional and cooperative banks. Every customer, employee, number, quote, and artifact in this article is illustrative and anonymized. This case continues the Calder research program documented in ../06/README.md: the evidence base, journey pains, and personas are inherited from that validated research register, then translated into concepts through an AI-first service design sprint. The knowledge is real; the client specifics are deliberately fictionalized.

1. Executive summary

Calder's SME onboarding research had already done the hard part: it proved that the bank's internal claim of an 8-day onboarding journey was fiction. The real median was 19 days, 68% of applicants were re-asked for documents, and customers bought certainty with their own time by phoning branches for status. The next task was to turn those insights into viable concepts before the Q3 investment gate. Over 15 working days, a lead service designer used Claude Code to generate and structure 200 idea seeds, OpenCode to cluster and de-duplicate them into a machine-readable concept catalog, Codex to act as an adversarial critic, and GitHub to run the evidence and decision gates. The sprint ended with three prototype-ready concepts: a one-stop digital onboarding flow, a relationship-manager-coached route for complex SMEs, and an accountant-channel concept for embedded acquisition.

2. The brief

Client portrait. Calder Community Bank serves 1.1 million customers across one eurozone region, including about 220,000 micro and small-business customers. The SME unit operates through 37 specialist relationship-manager pods, 120 branches, and a shared KYC and credit-operations function. For this article, the relevant flow is new-to-bank SME onboarding: approximately 16,800 current-account applications a year, of which 57% are simple owner-managed firms, 19% include a credit request at onboarding, and 24% are referred by accountants or bookkeepers. Digital maturity is middling: online acquisition exists, but the real journey still depends on branch staff, email attachments, and back-office document loops.

The challenge. Article 06 established the evidence base. The validated handoff bundle contained the current-state SME journey, themes T01/T02/T10, and insight cards INS-01 and INS-02 (See: ../06/README.md). The core pains were sharp enough to act on: requirements were invisible before branch contact, 68% of applicants experienced at least one document re-request, affected applicants saw a median 2.6 re-requests, and the median time to an operational account was 19 calendar days rather than the dashboard's 8. The bank did not need more research; it needed a concept portfolio strong enough to survive risk, operations, and commercial scrutiny.

Success criteria (agreed at sprint kickoff).

Criterion Target
Idea volume ≥ 180 distinct idea seeds before clustering
Traceability 100% of shortlisted concepts cite at least one validated insight and journey pain
Speed ≤ 15 working days from evidence handoff to 3 prototype-ready concepts
Portfolio coverage Final concepts address ≥ 75% of annual onboarding volume across standard and complex cases
Decision quality All staged concepts pass NUF plus desirability/feasibility/viability review
Business plausibility Each staged concept includes a value proposition, business model check, and prototype hypothesis

Constraints.

  • Timeline: the SME sponsor needed options before the next quarterly investment gate; there was no appetite for a 6-week concept phase.
  • Regulation: no concept could assume relaxed KYC, AML, or credit policy. In AMLD6-era eurozone SME banking, public UBO checks often require legitimate-interest verification, so any idea that depended on bypassing those UBO verification hurdles was invalid by default.
  • Technology reality: the core banking platform could emit events and expose read-only data, but replacement of the onboarding stack was out of scope.
  • Frontline bandwidth: relationship managers could participate in two half-day workshops and one 90-minute review, not a long co-design program.
  • Trust: the bank had been burned by innovation theater before. The sponsor wanted a design process where every concept could be walked back to evidence in under five minutes.

Why service design. The problem was not a single-screen UX issue. It was a cross-actor service breakdown spanning branch discovery, document exchange, KYC interpretation, credit visibility, and post-approval activation. Service design is the right frame when value depends on orchestrating touchpoints, people, rules, and backstage systems around a customer outcome rather than a departmental KPI (See: 04-service-design-for-business/04-solving-problems-and-creating-value.md). The sprint also needed disciplined divergence and convergence, not free-form brainstorming, because AI can create endless feature variants unless the work stays anchored in validated insight statements and ecosystem realities (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/05-ideation-methods.md; 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/05-developing-ideas.md).

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

The sprint ran as a compressed double-diamond nested inside the broader Calder program: Discover was already completed in Article 06, so this engagement began with a validated evidence handoff and focused on Define–Develop–Deliver. The operating rule was simple: agents expand the option set; humans control problem framing, risk boundaries, and kill decisions. Kumar's Mode 5 supplied the concept-generation logic, while Kimbell's concept brief and business-model artifacts kept the work from collapsing into feature lists (See: 17-101-design-methods/06-mode-5-explore-concepts.md; 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/04-ideation-and-concept-development.md).

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Define — evidence handoff and challenge framing

Goal. Convert the research register into a bounded set of generative challenge prompts.

Method & SOP. The sprint began exactly where Article 06 ended: validated insight cards, journey pains, and personas. The design task was to reframe those into How Might We prompts without losing specificity. TISDD's rule is useful here: ideation never begins with a blank page; it begins with tensions translated into generative but bounded questions (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/05-ideation-methods.md). We also used the service problem-framing logic from business-oriented service design: articulate the customer outcome and the business reason in the same sentence (See: 04-service-design-for-business/04-solving-problems-and-creating-value.md).

AI execution. Claude Code consumed the handoff bundle from ../06/README.md, then drafted six HMW prompts with a required schema: originating insight, affected persona, journey stage, tension, HMW, and prohibited assumptions. The exact prompt pattern was:

Use only the Calder SME evidence bundle from article 06.
For each validated pain point, produce one HMW prompt.
Rules:
1. Every HMW must cite an insight ID or journey pain.
2. Keep it specific enough to guide concepting.
3. Do not assume relaxed KYC, skipped branch staff, or core replacement.
4. Add two lateral sub-prompts per HMW.
Output as a markdown table with source evidence, target persona, HMW, and red lines.

GitHub held the framing gate. The HMW register lived in a pull request, with inline comments from the sponsor and KYC lead. One prompt — "How might we approve simple SMEs instantly" — was rejected because it embedded a solution and a policy breach. It was reframed to "How might we reduce uncertainty before approval without weakening controls." OpenCode generated a first-pass CSV scaffold for the concept catalog so every later idea could inherit source-insight metadata.

Human designer judgment. The designer's role was not writing better prompts; it was deciding where creativity was allowed and where it was not. Three boundary calls mattered. First, the designer kept INS-01 and INS-02 separate even though many ideas touched both; status visibility and document-loop friction were related but not identical. Second, the designer insisted that every HMW include a "red line" so workshop participants did not waste time ideating illegal shortcuts. Third, the designer chose two active personas for the sprint — owner-operator Eva and finance manager Malik — because the research showed their needs diverged operationally.

Artifact produced. HMW register, persona refresh, and sprint brief. These appear in Section 5 as A5 and A6a.

4.2 Develop — divergence at scale with agents first and humans second

Goal. Produce enough idea volume and variation to avoid incremental redesign.

Method & SOP. We enforced the classic divergence rules: no evaluation during generation, quantity before quality, and constraints used as stimuli rather than brakes (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/05-ideation-methods.md). To force non-obvious ideas, the sprint combined three mechanisms. First, brainwriting logic: multiple agents generated ideas silently against the same HMW set. Second, Kumar's concept-generating matrix: each idea had to combine a trigger, interface, trust mechanism, and human-support model (See: 17-101-design-methods/06-mode-5-explore-concepts.md). Third, analogy transfer: the team borrowed mechanisms from parcel tracking, accountant workflow software, and appointment triage rather than just copying competitor bank portals (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/04-ideation-and-concept-development.md).

AI execution. Claude Code ran the divergence engine as a small agent team. Each agent got one persona + one HMW + one provocation mode. The quota was deliberate: for each active HMW, generate 4 ideas for Eva, 4 for Malik, 2 for the relationship manager, and 2 for the accountant channel. Run the set in three modes — direct, cross-sector analogy, and severe constraint — yielding 5 HMW × 12 ideas × 3 modes = 180 raw seeds. HMW-06 was held back for partner-led exploration in the mixed workshop, which added the final 20 ideas and surfaced the accountant-channel concepts later refined into Accountant Connect.

claude-code run "Read handoff/sme-bundle/. For HMW-03 only, generate 12 ideas:
4 for Eva, 4 for Malik, 2 for the RM, 2 for the accountant channel.
Mode: cross-sector analogy. Borrow mechanisms from parcel tracking,
appointment scheduling, and document-signing software. Do not repeat
mechanisms already present in ideas/round1/HMW-03.json. Output JSON with
idea_id, source_insight, persona, mechanism, channel, value_hypothesis."

OpenCode then did the mechanical work AI is unusually good at: normalizing wording, removing near-duplicates, and clustering by mechanism rather than by surface label. GitHub issues were opened automatically for the 24 surviving clusters so stakeholders could comment on concrete idea families instead of scrolling through 200 post-its. The idea-source pie chart and divergence-round bar chart were generated from that catalog.

Human designer judgment. This was the first critical anti-hallucination gate. Agents produced many plausible ideas that violated the economics or regulatory reality of SME banking: instant limited accounts with no document verification, automatic credit explanations that revealed internal risk rules, and a WhatsApp status channel that no one in compliance would approve. Humans killed those fast. More subtly, the designer noticed an AI bias toward digital-only ideas. The accountant partner and two RMs added the breakthrough variation that became Accountant Connect: not a better portal, but a consented pre-flight channel where the accountant can prepare the case before the bank touches it.

Artifact produced. 200 idea seeds, 24 clustered concept families, and the divergence pipeline artifacts in A6/A8.

4.3 Converge — from idea families to concept briefs

Goal. Reduce a noisy idea pool into a small, comparable portfolio.

Method & SOP. The convergence stack had four steps. First, affinity clusters were pitched in the room. Second, participants dot-voted to reveal energy, not to make the decision. Third, the top clusters went through the NUF test — New, Useful, Feasible — to remove fashionable but weak directions (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/05-ideation-methods.md). Fourth, the surviving concepts were scored through the desirability/feasibility/viability lenses and written as standardized concept briefs so they could be compared as service propositions rather than favorite features (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/05-developing-ideas.md; 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/07-the-toolkit-templates.md).

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the first version of all 11 shortlisted concept sheets from the cluster catalog. OpenCode filled a structured NUF table and highlighted spread between evaluator groups. Codex was deliberately held back until after the room liked three concepts; then it was asked to try to kill them.

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read concepts/shortlist/*.md and evidence/hmw-register.md.
For each shortlisted concept: (1) verify traceability to an insight or journey pain,
(2) attack the feasibility assumption from a banking operations perspective,
(3) flag any concept that is only an incremental variant of another.
Be adversarial. Recommend kill, park, or stage with one sentence of reason."

Codex killed two crowd favorites. The first was a status chatbot: useful, but not conceptually distinct from the status-heartbeat family and risky for a regulated flow. The second was an "instant live account" idea: commercially tempting, operationally brittle, and too dependent on policy exceptions. GitHub stored the verdict log in the concept PRs, making the kill decisions transparent rather than political.

Human designer judgment. The designer made the balancing decision that no model can make on its own: the staged portfolio could not be three versions of digital self-service. Calder needed coverage across the service ecology. That led to a deliberate three-concept set: one concept for standard-volume simplification, one for complex human-led cases, and one for the acquisition channel outside the bank's walls. The designer also compressed the concept sheets so each fit on one screen in the decision meeting; brevity forced clarity.

Artifact produced. NUF sheet, decision matrix, three concept briefs, value proposition canvas, and business model canvas.

4.4 Deliver — stage the concepts for prototyping

Goal. Turn the winning concepts into prototype-ready design objects with clear assumptions and KPIs.

Method & SOP. Kimbell's concept-brief logic treats a concept as a structured design object: target actors, value proposition, experience flow, capabilities, assumptions, and business case mechanics (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/04-ideation-and-concept-development.md). We added two overlays before the gate. First, a value proposition canvas for the lead concept to check whether its pain relievers really matched the user profile. Second, a business model canvas pass to ensure the economics were explicit rather than implied (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/05-developing-ideas.md).

AI execution. Claude Code translated the selected concepts into a prototype backlog: what needs to be mocked, who should test it, and which assumption each prototype must answer. OpenCode created a structured issue set for the next phase. GitHub provided the gate mechanism: each concept needed (a) a traceability row, (b) a prototype hypothesis, and (c) named owners. The sponsor reviewed the three PRs in one session rather than through a slide deck.

Convert C1 C2 C3 into prototype briefs.
For each concept output:
- test question
- user segment and sample size
- touchpoints to simulate
- backstage assumptions to fake
- KPI movement expected if the hypothesis holds
Do not add any feature not already named in the concept brief.

Human designer judgment. The designer refused to let the sprint end with applause and vague next steps. Each concept had to have a reason to exist in prototype form. For Calder LaunchPad, the primary test was whether receipt confirmation plus a visible status heartbeat materially reduced anxiety and branch contact intent. For Guided Start, the question was whether named ownership increased confidence enough to justify the extra RM time on complex cases. For Accountant Connect, the real test was channel behavior: would accountants actually do pre-validation if it saved client time but gave the bank more structure?

Artifact produced. Prototype backlog, future-state service blueprint, KPI targets, and traceability chain to carry forward into the next phase.

# Service Concept Brief - C1 Calder LaunchPad
Concept Name and Version: Calder LaunchPad v1 / 2026-04-29
Target Beneficiaries and Ecosystem Actors: Primary - owner-managed and simple-structure SMEs new to Calder; Secondary - RMs, KYC ops, digital support
Need or Opportunity Addressed: INS-01 and INS-02 - status blindness and document-loop friction make standard onboarding feel risky and slow
Core Value Proposition: Open a Calder business account with one guided flow, one document vault, and reliable status updates so the owner spends minutes acting instead of days guessing
How the Service Works: Preflight checklist before application; guided application with prefilled data where held; document vault confirms each accepted item; status heartbeat every 48 hours or on milestone change; approval-day digital access pack
Key Channels and Touchpoints: Web and mobile journey, email and SMS updates, branch scan fallback, RM escalation trigger
Actor Roles and Resource Contributions: Customer uploads once; accountant may pre-assemble documents; KYC ops review flagged cases only; RM intervenes on exceptions
Required Backstage Capabilities and Integrations: CRM and core read adapters, document vault, event hub, completeness rules engine, approval-to-digital-access trigger
Key Assumptions to Test: Desirability - owners trust the heartbeat without a named human; Feasibility - receipt SLA of 1 hour is operationally realistic; Viability - handling cost falls from 146 EUR to 98 EUR on standard cases
Strategic Rationale and Business Case Mechanics: Applies to 57% of annual volume; modeled cycle 19 -> 9 days; abandonment 18% -> 8% on eligible cases; status calls sharply reduced
# Service Concept Brief - C2 Guided Start
Concept Name and Version: Guided Start v1 / 2026-04-29
Target Beneficiaries and Ecosystem Actors: Primary - SMEs with multiple directors, credit need, or non-standard ownership; Secondary - RMs, credit ops, branch advisors
Need or Opportunity Addressed: Complex applicants do not fail because they hate humans; they fail because no one owns the case end to end when complexity rises

6. Use cases / case studies

6.1 One-stop digital SME onboarding — Calder LaunchPad

Scenario. Eva wants a new business account before a supplier contract starts next week. Her old path involved a branch visit, scanned attachments, and multiple chasers. The new concept gives her one guided route.

What was designed. Five-step flow: preflight checker, guided application, document vault with receipts, status heartbeat, approval-day activation pack. The design deliberately limits human involvement to exceptions rather than making the customer guess when a human is needed.

AI working session summary. Claude Code generated the first 36 variants of the standard-case flow across three modes; OpenCode clustered them; Codex later killed the chatbot variants and kept the mechanismal core — checklist, receipts, heartbeat, activation. Representative prompt:

For persona Eva, build ten structurally different ways to make standard SME onboarding
feel certain and low effort. Avoid copy variants. Change the mechanism each time:
checklist, tracker, document vault, partner handoff, branch fallback, activation pack.
Every idea must cite INS-01 or INS-02.

Outcome. The sponsor staged LaunchPad for prototype first because it covers the biggest volume slice: 57% of annual cases. Modeled impact on eligible cases: cycle time 19 → 9 days, document re-request rate 68% → 24%, abandonment 18% → 8%, and status inquiries 34 → 11 per 100 applications.

6.2 Relationship-manager-coached onboarding — Guided Start

Scenario. Malik is opening accounts for a multi-director importer and wants a working-capital line at the same time. Pure self-service is the wrong answer; he needs visible ownership and better cross-team coordination.

What was designed. A named RM owns the case, a shared checklist shows who owes what, and a scheduled pre-decision call replaces the old silence. The concept keeps complexity visible without making the customer chase it.

AI working session summary. The most useful AI contribution was not generation but synthesis: the agent team recognized that several separate ideas — RM dashboard, kickoff call, shared task board, explanation call — were one concept family. A later Codex pass challenged whether this was just "more service" rather than better service; the answer was that Guided Start is a different operating model for the top 18% of complex cases, not a premium layer on every case.

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Attack Guided Start. Assume you are a skeptical
operations director. Explain why named ownership may fail. Then identify the smallest
version of the concept that still preserves its value hypothesis."

Outcome. Guided Start was staged because it protects the highest-value and highest-risk cases. Modeled impact on complex cases: cycle time 31 → 18 days, RM rework minutes down 28%, and customer confidence score at decision stage up from 2.4 to 4.0 out of 5.

6.3 Embedded banking via accountant channel — Accountant Connect

Scenario. A local accountant refers 18 Calder prospects a year. Today she assembles documents through email, tells clients what the bank probably wants, and phones branches when the case disappears. The bank benefits from her work but cannot see or structure it.

What was designed. Accountant Connect provides a consented pre-flight route: referral link, checklist, document packaging, completeness feedback, then structured handoff to Calder. The concept treats accountants as real ecosystem actors rather than accidental intermediaries.

AI working session summary. This concept emerged only after the human workshop corrected the AI's digital-only bias. The accountant partner described her workaround; the designer reframed it as a service pattern; Claude Code then expanded it into multiple operational variants. OpenCode compared them by channel leverage and implementation load.

Take the accountant workaround described in workshop notes.
Generate five service concepts that formalize it without exposing uncontrolled third-party access.
For each variant specify consent model, document-sharing mechanism, and RM handoff point.

Outcome. Accountant Connect staged as the third concept because it opens a defensible acquisition route the bank already half-uses informally. Modeled impact on referred cases: first-pass completeness 46% → 82%, branch appointments per referred SME down 39%, and referral-to-application conversion up 11 points.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

The sprint encoded four SOPs for AI use: HMW generation, divergence at quota, cluster-and-dedupe, and adversarial concept critique. The meta-rule across all four was identical to Article 06: agents may propose only what they can trace to evidence, and humans retain kill authority.

Meta-SOP.

sop: calder-concept-sprint
version: 1.0
stages:
  - id: handoff
    input: article06 evidence bundle
    output: validated HMW register
    gate: sponsor and designer signoff
  - id: divergence
    input: HMW register plus personas plus red lines
    output: 180 agent idea seeds
    rules: [no evaluation, quota per persona, three mechanism modes]
  - id: workshop
    input: clustered seed set
    output: 24 concept families plus 20 human additions
    gate: mixed-room review
  - id: convergence
    input: concept families
    output: 11 shortlisted concepts then 3 staged concepts
    checks: [traceability, NUF, desirability, feasibility, viability]
  - id: delivery
    input: staged concepts
    output: concept briefs, value and business model checks, prototype backlog
    gate: named owner and KPI per concept

HMW-generation prompt template.

You are the Calder HMW generator.
Input: validated insight cards, journey pains, persona table.
Task: create one How Might We prompt per pain.
Required fields: source_insight, persona, journey_stage, tension, HMW, lateral_prompts, red_lines.
Guardrails:
- Every HMW must quote or cite a source insight.
- Never embed a solution in the HMW.
- Never assume policy relaxation, core replacement, or new data rights.
- If the evidence does not support a pain, output UNSUPPORTED rather than inventing.

Ideation-run SOP as code.

ideation_run:
  hmw_count: 5
  held_back_hmw:
    - HMW-06
  persona_quota:
    eva_owner_operator: 4
    malik_finance_manager: 4
    relationship_manager: 2
    accountant_channel: 2
  modes:
    - direct_solutioning
    - cross_sector_analogy
    - severe_constraint
  dedupe_rule:
    similarity_threshold: 0.85
    collapse_on_same_mechanism: true
  anti_repeat_rules:
    - reject_copy_only_variants
    - reject_same_channel_same_mechanism_same_actor
    - keep one strongest variant per mechanism family
  expected_output: 180_agent_ideas

Critique prompt for the adversarial concept-kill role.

You are the concept critic.
For each shortlisted concept, try to kill it.
Questions:
1. Which validated insight does this concept actually answer?
2. Is it meaningfully different from another shortlisted concept?
3. What regulatory, operational, or economic assumption is weakest?
4. If this concept failed in six months, what would have caused it?
Output one verdict only: stage, park, or kill.
Prefer killing weak duplicates over polishing them.

Guardrails that kept the sprint honest.

  1. Traceability over eloquence. No concept entered the shortlist without an insight ID and HMW ID. This prevented the common agent failure of drifting into generic "better portal" ideas.
  2. Feasibility reality-check against banking rules. Every concept had a red-line review by KYC and risk before it was allowed into the decision matrix.
  3. Quota by persona. Without explicit persona quotas, the agent pool over-produced for the owner-operator and under-produced for the finance-manager and partner-channel cases.
  4. Mechanism dedupe. Similarity-based clustering stopped the false appearance of novelty created by renaming the same status-tracker idea nine times.
  5. Human sign-off points. HMW framing, shortlist, and staged portfolio all required named human approval.

Failure modes observed and mitigated.

Failure mode What happened Mitigation
Incremental variant storm Agents produced many superficial tracker variants Deduplicate by mechanism, not wording
Digital-only bias Early rounds ignored human-led and partner-led routes Persona quotas plus mixed workshop tables
Policy fantasy Some ideas assumed instant approval or weak KYC Red-line review before shortlist
Popularity bias Dot votes favored the most legible concept, not the best portfolio Dot vote treated as signal only; NUF and triple lens decide
Concept inflation Teams tried to merge too many features into one "super concept" Force one-sentence value proposition and one primary value hypothesis

9. AI tool playbook (this engagement)

Claude Code — orchestration and artifact authoring. Claude Code ran the ideation agent fan-out, authored the HMW register, drafted concept briefs, and maintained consistency between traceability tables, charts, and concept narratives. It excelled at structured generation under explicit quotas and schemas. It failed when prompts were too open; the first divergence round drifted toward generic fintech ideas until the evidence-only rule was tightened. Human takeover point: portfolio balance and final concept naming.

OpenCode — clustering, dataset work, and mechanical structuring. OpenCode turned raw idea outputs into normalized CSV and markdown tables, flagged near-duplicates, and generated the concepts_sample.csv file used throughout the sprint. It excelled at repetitive data hygiene and catalog maintenance. It failed at distinguishing mechanismally distinct ideas that used similar words; the designer had to rename and separate those families manually.

Codex — adversarial critic. Codex was used deliberately late, after enthusiasm existed. Its job was to kill. It excelled at exposing duplicate concepts, over-optimistic policy assumptions, and vague business-case language. It failed when asked to propose new concepts from scratch; its comparative strength here was critique, not ideation.

GitHub — governance and decision memory. GitHub issues represented concept families; PRs carried HMW edits, shortlist decisions, and critique logs; the project board showed sprint stage. What it excelled at was making design decisions auditable. The human took over in review meetings and decision calls; GitHub was the memory, not the judge.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  1. AI can make ideation shallow if volume becomes the goal. The breakthrough came when the team treated 200 ideas as raw ore, not success in itself.
  2. Dot voting is a mood detector, not a strategy. The room loved the WhatsApp concept because it was familiar; the critic killed it correctly.
  3. Persona quotas matter. Without them, agents collapse toward the most legible customer and miss the edge cases where opportunity often hides (See: 15-change-by-design/02-convert-need-into-demand.md).
  4. Concept briefs are anti-slop tools. The moment a concept must declare actors, backstage capabilities, and business mechanics, weak ideas lose their glamour fast.
  5. Frontline participants do not just validate; they reframe. Accountant Connect existed because a human partner described a real workaround the models had not seen.
  6. The critic role should be explicit. If nobody is assigned to kill ideas, organizations quietly carry weak concepts into prototyping out of politeness.
  7. Evidence traceability speeds politics. The sponsor did not have to trust taste; she could inspect lineage from insight to concept to KPI.
