01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

Ember Hospitality runs 60 casual-premium restaurants across the Netherlands, Belgium, and western Germany, plus a delivery operation and a shared table-booking app. It serves about 2.4 million restaurant covers a year, yet its so-called special-occasion experience — birthdays, anniversaries, graduations, reunions — was barely a service at all: a free-text booking note, a hurried staff whisper, an occasional candle, and a high chance that dietary needs, deposits, or surprise plans would be dropped somewhere between app, host stand, kitchen, and table. Over six weeks, the team used a prototype ladder — storyboard and bodystorming, service walkthrough, Wizard-of-Oz, and a bounded concierge pilot — to test four increasingly concrete versions of a new experience. Across 210 guest participants, the winning concept was the Celebration Concierge: a structured but light-touch service that captures occasion intent at booking, routes only the risky bookings to a human concierge, and gives floor staff a usable celebration brief instead of a vague note. Ember moved from generic gestures to a testable whole service, then into rollout with a blueprint, measurement plan, and clear unit economics.

02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

Ember Hospitality is a fictional restaurant group positioned between neighborhood brasserie and polished casual dining. It operates 60 sites across three countries, with a central booking product team, shared CRM, and regional operations model. Annual dine-in volume is about 2.4 million covers, or roughly 40,000 per restaurant. A little under 60% of dinner covers are reservation-led, mostly through Ember's own app and web flow; the rest come from walk-ins, phone bookings, or marketplace referrals. Digital maturity is uneven. Ember has a decent booking product and loyalty stack, but restaurant execution still depends on manual host notes, shift briefings, and what each site manager remembers five minutes before service.

Special occasions mattered commercially and emotionally. About 6.5% of annual covers — around 156,000 covers — were tagged after the fact by staff or survey responses as birthdays, anniversaries, graduations, reunions, or similar milestone meals. These visits produced higher average spend than standard covers, but the variance was enormous: some tables felt cared for, others received nothing or, worse, a failed surprise. Ember's leadership did not have a design problem statement yet; they had a complaint pattern.

The challenge

The current experience was generic by default and heroic only when a strong floor manager compensated manually. Occasion information entered at booking as a free-text note. The host stand often saw it too late. Dietary requirements lived in a separate note field that kitchen staff sometimes viewed only after menus were dropped. Group bookings of 10 or more required a deposit, but the promise communicated to guests — table timing, cake handling, menu preselection, surprise coordination — differed from site to site.

Baseline evidence from 12 weeks of booking logs, 4,200 post-visit surveys, 18 floor-staff interviews, and 6 service shadows showed five structural failures:

  1. Weak signal capture. Only 18% of celebratory visits were identifiable at booking in structured form; most arrived as vague notes like "birthday maybe cake".
  2. Backstage fragmentation. The booking app, host notes, kitchen brief, POS, and shift plan did not form one joined-up service (See: 05-good-services/06-designing-good-services.md).
  3. Promise failure. Dietary nuance and surprise timing were lost in handoff, especially under Friday and Saturday load.
  4. Frontline improvisation cost. Celebratory tables required 11.4 minutes of scramble time on average: checking notes, finding candles, clarifying deposit status, or apologizing for missed cues.
  5. No testable model. Leadership kept jumping to "build occasion features in the app" before learning which parts of the experience needed software, people, script, or policy.

Constraints

Budget for the alpha sprint was capped at €190,000, including temporary labor, prototype build, and measurement. Ember wanted a go or no-go decision in six weeks, before the summer demand peak. No POS replacement, CRM replacement, or kitchen-display rollout was possible in the horizon. Privacy mattered: no public singing, no photographing guests without explicit consent, no storing sensitive health detail beyond what was needed for dietary safety, and no revealing a surprise by forcing the wrong question at the table. The service had to work in restaurants of different size and staffing mix, not only in flagship sites.

Why service design

The problem crossed booking, app copy, host behavior, kitchen visibility, timing, payment, and revisit. That is a whole-service problem, not an app-flow problem (See: 05-good-services/06-designing-good-services.md). It also demanded prototyping because the riskiest questions were experiential and operational: would guests want to coordinate surprises in-app, would staff trust a celebration brief, would manual concierge work pay for itself, and where would dietary and deposit promises break? The right move was to build to think — cheap prototypes answering one question at a time — instead of building software to defend an untested idea (See: 15-change-by-design/04-building-to-think.md). The team explicitly adopted the progressive-fidelity logic from service prototyping practice: start rough, stage the service, then move into Wizard-of-Oz and concierge delivery only after earlier assumptions hold (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/06-prototyping-methods.md; 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/06-prototyping-services.md; 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/05-prototyping-and-testing-services.md).

03 · Approach

Approach: the AI-first service design process

The engagement followed the Double Diamond, but the working engine was a prototype ladder. Discovery established the current-state service and hypothesis bank. Define turned those risks into a ladder of experiments. Develop ran progressively higher-fidelity prototypes. Deliver ended with a bounded concierge pilot, service blueprint, and rollout gate. TISDD supplied the prototyping discipline; Kumar's modes kept the team honest about where it was: sense intent, know context, know people, frame insights, explore concepts, frame solutions, realize offerings.

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Discover: make the current service legible

Goal. Understand where the special-occasion experience actually breaks today, across both guests and staff.

Method & SOP. The team started with a whole-service audit rather than ideation. That meant booking-flow review, note-field analysis, survey coding, floor shadowing, and a current-state journey map. Good Services is explicit that teams must solve a whole problem for users, not just optimize one channel (See: 05-good-services/06-designing-good-services.md). The designer encoded the initial problem as a user-need set and a risk register: surprise coordination, dietary promise, deposit promise, timing visibility, and staff readiness.

AI execution. OpenCode normalized 12 weeks of booking and spend data, joining reservation tags, group size, deposit status, survey sentiment, and recovery-comp flags into one prototype-ready table. Claude Code then converted findings into a hypothesis bank and first-pass prototype brief. Codex attacked the reasoning before any prototype was built.

GitHub was used as the operating spine. Every risky assumption became an issue (celebration-signal, dietary-visibility, group-deposit-promise, surprise-consent, staff-brief-format), and the board was sorted by risk, not by function area.

Human designer judgment. The designer made two crucial framing calls. First, the team would prototype the service moment, not a new premium package; the question was whether Ember could reliably deliver confidence, not whether it could upsell champagne. Second, special occasions were split into three service jobs: emotional surprise, dietary safety, and group coordination. Treating them as one use case would have muddied every prototype.

Artifact produced. Current-state journey map, baseline metrics, and a five-risk hypothesis bank that drove the ladder.

4.2 Define: build the prototype ladder and start cheap

Goal. Turn vague solution ideas into a sequence of cheap experiments, each answering one question.

Method & SOP. This phase followed the core logic of build to think: get the service out of abstract decks and into enacted scenes fast (See: 15-change-by-design/04-building-to-think.md). The team used storyboard panels, bodystorming with staff, and a service walkthrough before any live guest testing. TISDD and Kimbell both stress that service prototypes must stage frontstage and backstage together, because a lovely guest script with a broken handoff is still a failed service (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/06-prototyping-methods.md; 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/05-prototyping-and-testing-services.md).

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the storyboard, prototype ladder, and a filled prototype plan from the hypothesis bank. OpenCode turned the booking concept into a clickable low-fidelity flow and generated fake restaurant rosters, menus, and table notes so the walkthrough felt like service, not a demo. GitHub PRs became the revision gate for each prototype step.

Human designer judgment. The designer used bodystorming to expose a hidden flaw before guests saw anything: staff hated the first draft because it surfaced occasion information too early and too broadly. They did not want every floor runner hearing that a proposal or fertility-anniversary dinner was in progress. The brief had to distinguish who needs to know from who merely needs timing cues.

Artifact produced. Prototype ladder, filled plan canvas, storyboard, walkthrough script, and the first version of the celebration brief.

4.3 Develop: Wizard-of-Oz the risky middle

Goal. Test whether a structured special-occasion flow creates a better guest experience without creating backstage chaos.

Method & SOP. Weeks 4 and 5 ran the most revealing test: a Wizard-of-Oz prototype across six restaurants. Guests used a seemingly functional booking and pre-arrival flow, but humans completed the backstage work: triaging requests, checking surprise visibility, messaging sites, and creating the celebration brief. This is exactly the point of Wizard-of-Oz in service design: learn desirability and operational burden before funding automation (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/06-prototyping-methods.md; 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/06-prototyping-services.md).

The test had three bounded use paths:

  • birthday surprise tables,
  • dietary-request celebratory meals,
  • group bookings of 10 or more with deposit promise.

Each live session ended in a same-day debrief with guest notes, staff effort scores, and pass/fail markers.

AI execution. Claude Code generated moderator guides, host scripts, and end-of-night issue digests. OpenCode maintained the live frontstage prototype and a lightweight internal dashboard where the hidden operators tracked each booking manually. Codex reviewed daily findings and challenged optimistic interpretations.

GitHub usage was concrete. Each day's findings were filed as issues with severity, route, and owner. Fixes to the staff brief or booking copy had to merge before the next day's live tests. The PR thread, not the meeting, became the decision log.

Human designer judgment. The designer constrained the cohort aggressively. No proposal dinners, no bereavement occasions, and no severe multi-allergen bookings were used in the Wizard-of-Oz path; those were judged too sensitive for a partially faked experience. The designer also killed one seemingly attractive feature — staff-triggered tableside applause — after a single bodystorm showed how culturally brittle it was across sites.

Artifact produced. Testing dashboard, issue log, mock dataset, and a narrowed service model: not every celebratory booking needed a human; only the high-risk ones did.

4.4 Deliver: prove the concierge model and make it roll-outable

Goal. Validate the winning concept under real operating conditions and leave with a blueprint Ember could actually scale.

Method & SOP. The final step was a bounded concierge pilot represented through fictional guest scenarios, site constraints, manual delivery, narrow volume, and explicit economics tracking. Polaine, Løvlie, and Reason describe this as prototyping the business model, not merely the interface (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/06-prototyping-services.md). Kimbell makes the same point: a pilot is a learning experiment, not a vanity soft launch (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/05-prototyping-and-testing-services.md).

The winning service was named Celebration Concierge. It had three parts:

  1. structured capture at booking,
  2. rules that route only risky bookings to a concierge,
  3. a concise celebration brief visible to the right staff at the right time.

The concierge pilot ran for 84 guests in week 6 across 12 restaurants. Routing rules were manual but documented. The service blueprint was rehearsed column by column with ops, host teams, kitchen representatives, and finance.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the future-state blueprint and rollout beta plan from the live findings. OpenCode simulated routing rules against a year of booking patterns to estimate likely concierge load per restaurant. Codex reviewed the unit economics and challenged scope creep.

GitHub carried the rollout decision. Prototype findings were summarized as issues, the blueprint and KPI plan were proposed in PR #27, and the COO approval was recorded as the merge gate. Ember then approved an 8-week private beta in 12 restaurants, followed by a 30-site expansion if the first three KPIs held.

Human designer judgment. The human made the call that the concierge would be selective, not universal. AI tools kept proposing richer personalization because they optimized for possible delight; the designer optimized for reliable delivery. The right service was the one Ember could keep on a wet Friday night with two sick staff and a kitchen ticket backlog.

Artifact produced. Winning concept, future-state blueprint, measurement plan, and rollout beta gate.

PROTOTYPE PLAN - Ember Hospitality - P3 Wizard-of-Oz - v3
Service moment: Special-occasion booking to table execution
Method source: TISDD prototyping test plan adapted for hospitality

1. Assumption to test
   Guests will share structured occasion details at booking if the form is short,
   discreet, and explicit about what staff will do with the information.

2. Hypothesis
   If Ember asks only four relevant questions and promises a discreet service,
   then at least 80 percent of recruited celebratory guests will complete the flow,
   and fewer than 30 percent of live sessions will be rated high effort by staff.

3. Prototype method and fidelity
   Wizard-of-Oz service prototype.
   Frontstage real enough to feel usable: clickable booking flow, SMS confirmation,
   host brief on staff tablet.
   Backstage manual: hidden operator triages bookings and sends site cues.

4. Subjects
   n=66 guests across birthdays, anniversaries, dietary-sensitive meals, and group
   bookings of 10 plus; 6 restaurants; 18 host and floor staff involved.

5. Session structure
   Booking completed 24-72h before visit.
   Operator prepares celebration brief.
   Staff run service live.
   Guest debrief within 30 minutes of payment.
   Staff debrief at close of shift.

6. Success criteria
   - Guest completion rate >= 80 percent
   - Special-occasion NPS in-test >= 50
   - High-effort staff sessions <= 30 percent
   - Zero critical dietary or surprise-consent breaches
   - Attach revenue uplift >= 4 EUR per celebratory cover versus matched baseline

7. Failure criteria
   - Any severe dietary miss or publicly exposed surprise
   - More than 2 bookings per shift requiring manager rescue
   - Staff brief longer than 45 seconds to interpret

8. Evidence captured
   completion flag | guest quote | effort score 1-7 | attach spend | cue timing |
   failure mode tag | owner | next change

9. Gate decision
   Pass if all safety conditions hold and 4 of 5 success criteria pass.
   Otherwise cut scope, simplify routing, and repeat at lower volume.

Template 2 — Service walkthrough script

NOTE
SERVICE WALKTHROUGH SCRIPT - Ember P2 - 45 minutes
Roles: host planner, booking app, concierge, host stand, server, kitchen observer,
manager, note taker.

Scene 1 - Booking
- Planner books a table for 8 and selects birthday.
- Ask: what information feels safe to share now, and what would feel intrusive?
- Observe: hesitation, abandonment point, wording confusion.

Scene 2 - Pre-arrival
- Planner receives confirmation and optional follow-up.
- Ask: is the promise clear about what Ember will and will not do?
- Observe: whether guests assume cake, song, or photo without being told.

Scene 3 - Arrival
- Host stand receives the table with one celebration brief.
- Ask staff to narrate what they know, what they do not know, and what they need.
- Observe: duplicate questions, privacy risks, deposit confusion.

Scene 4 - Table moment
- Server handles dietary cue and celebration timing.
- Inject edge case: one guest arrives late; one asks privately about the surprise.
- Observe: whether the brief survives pressure.

Scene 5 - Payment and close
- Bill arrives; follow-up is drafted.
- Ask: what closes the experience without forcing sentiment?
- Observe: whether the close creates a revisit trigger.

06 · Use cases

Use cases

Use case A — Birthday surprise via Wizard-of-Oz

Scenario. Elisa books a dinner for six for her partner's 40th birthday. She wants one discreet dessert cue, no public singing, and one non-alcoholic sparkling option for a pregnant guest.

What was designed. The prototype added a short booking branch: occasion type, who knows, what must stay discreet, and whether Ember should offer a dessert moment, a drinks moment, or nothing visible. The hidden operator turned this into a host brief and a kitchen cue.

AI working session summary. Claude Code drafted the discreet-language variants and the staff cue script. OpenCode built the frontstage booking branch and a tablet-style host brief. Codex reviewed the first-day logs and flagged that one copy variant implied a guarantee of surprise success when late arrivals could still break it.

Illustrative Codex host command — emitted pack; live case execution unverified.

TERMINAL
codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Review copy/celebration_discreet.md against
wizard/day1_birthdays.md. Flag any line that promises certainty Ember cannot
control in a live dining room."

Outcome. In the Wizard-of-Oz cohort, the birthday path moved from a 29 NPS baseline to 63 in tested sessions, with dessert attach from 22% to 61%. The critical learning was not the dessert itself; it was that guests valued confidence and discretion more than theatricality.

Use case B — Dietary-request table service

Scenario. An anniversary dinner for four includes one gluten-free guest and one nut allergy. In the baseline service, staff often handled these as kitchen notes unrelated to the emotional occasion.

What was designed. The prototype treated dietary promise as part of the special-occasion service, not a separate compliance note. The celebration brief surfaced both the emotional context and the safety constraint, while the kitchen cue card showed only the information required for safe execution.

AI working session summary. Claude Code created the split-visibility brief: full guest context for host and supervisor, minimal safe detail for kitchen and runners. OpenCode mocked the kitchen cue card and staff tablet. Codex analyzed test results and challenged whether the improved scores came from unusually strong managers at two sites; after re-cutting by site, the effect still held.

Outcome. Dietary promise-kept rate improved from 82% baseline to 97% in prototype-plus-beta cohorts. Staff effort fell because the cue arrived earlier and in one place; average observed effort score on this path fell from 5.1 to 3.4.

Use case C — Group booking for 10 plus with deposit and promise flow

Scenario. A 14-person graduation dinner needs a deposit, two vegan meals, one late arriver, and a clear promise about table-hold time.

What was designed. Ember replaced an inconsistent email chain with a staged promise flow: deposit request, menu-selection window, late-arrival policy, and one concierge touch if the booking met the risky threshold. The service was not luxurious; it was specific.

AI working session summary. Claude Code wrote the deposit promise template and escalation rules. OpenCode built a pre-arrival confirmation flow with realistic mock booking data. GitHub issues tracked every promise failure by restaurant. Codex reviewed the group path and argued against routing all 10+ tables to concierge; the data showed only mixed dietary, timing, or high-spend groups justified the human cost.

Outcome. Deposit completion rose from 62% to 88%, last-minute group cancellations fell from 18% to 9%, and average check for the tested group path increased by €8.10 per cover because the promise window made pre-orders and add-ons feel safer, not pushier.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

This engagement encoded four SOPs for agent execution. The pattern stayed fixed: method → step list → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

1. Prototype-plan SOP

This was the core SOP. Claude Code turned research evidence into a one-question prototype plan, with TISDD-style fields for assumption, hypothesis, method, subjects, signals, failure conditions, and gate.

NOTE
You are SERVICE-PROTOTYPE-PLANNER.
Input: one user need, one risky assumption, one operational constraint.
Steps:
1. Restate the user need in plain language.
2. Name the single riskiest assumption.
3. Choose the cheapest prototype that can test it.
4. Define guest subjects and staff roles.
5. Define success signals and explicit kill conditions.
6. Define what evidence will be captured.
Output schema:
{need, assumption, hypothesis, prototype_method, fidelity, subjects,
frontstage_fake_vs_real, backstage_fake_vs_real, metrics, kill_conditions,
next_gate}
Acceptance:
- one assumption only
- both guest value and staff effort measured
- safety-critical cases flagged
- no automation proposed where role-play is enough
Review gate: human designer + Codex challenge pass.

2. Test-script generation prompt

The team used Claude Code to generate moderator and staff scripts that were consistent enough to compare, but flexible enough for live dining conditions.

NOTE
You are TEST-SCRIPT-GENERATOR for live hospitality prototypes.
Given a prototype branch, create:
- moderator intro
- task statement
- exact observation points
- one injected edge case
- guest debrief questions
- staff debrief questions
- evidence capture fields
Rules:
- never lead the participant
- avoid feature praise language
- include one question that checks whether the guest still felt they had to manage the service alone
- include one question that checks whether staff could actually run this on a busy Friday

3. Wizard-of-Oz runbook

NOTE
WIZARD-OF-OZ RUNBOOK - Ember
Pre-open
- Confirm which bookings are in the cohort
- Check surprise visibility permissions
- Confirm dietary detail with site manager if unclear
- Load host brief and kitchen cue card

Live
- Hidden operator monitors booking branch completion
- If ambiguity appears, route to manager not to generic automation
- Log timestamps for arrival, cue, dessert, payment
- Stop the test immediately on any safety-critical failure

Post-close
- Capture guest debrief within 30 minutes
- Capture staff effort score before memory decays
- File issue with route, severity, and owner in GitHub
- Update dashboard before the next service

4. Lean validation guardrails

The most useful SOP was the anti-bias layer. Service teams fall in love with polished prototypes. AI falls in love even faster because it can generate polish cheaply. The guardrails were explicit:

  • test the riskiest assumption first,
  • prefer the lowest fidelity that answers the question,
  • track staff effort alongside guest delight,
  • force at least one kill condition into every plan,
  • separate pleasant reaction from observable success,
  • re-cut results by site to avoid flagship bias,
  • never move up the fidelity ladder because stakeholders are impatient.

Meta-SOP and failure modes

The meta-SOP was: research → current-state map → prototype plan → cheap enactment → live test → debrief → dashboard → gate → blueprint. No prototype advanced without a written learning question, a named owner, and a stop condition.

Observed failure modes and mitigations:

  • Prototype-love. Pretty booking screens created false confidence. Mitigation: Codex red-teamed every result summary.
  • Confirmation bias. Staff wanted the service to work. Mitigation: guest completion and effort thresholds were pre-written, not negotiated after the fact.
  • Backstage invisibility. Teams praised guest delight while ignoring kitchen burden. Mitigation: effort score and backstage notes were mandatory in every session.
  • Flagship bias. Best sites made the concept look stronger than it was. Mitigation: site-level cuts before any rollout claim.
  • Safety blur. Dietary and surprise issues should not be treated like copy bugs. Mitigation: safety-critical stop conditions and human override.

AI tool playbook

Claude Code — research synthesis, prototype planning, artifact authoring

Claude Code was the design operations engine. It drafted the hypothesis bank, prototype plans, storyboard, walkthrough script, blueprint narrative, and most article-ready artifacts. It excelled at turning messy evidence into comparable prototype plans. It failed when asked to judge cultural subtlety or service tact on its own. The human took over on tone, privacy, and what counted as truly special versus embarrassingly performative.

OpenCode handled mechanical build work: booking flow prototypes, staff tablet mocks, synthetic booking datasets, and routing simulations against historical volume. It excelled at producing believable frontstage artifacts quickly. It failed when asked to simplify by itself; left unguided, it added more fields than the service needed.

10 · Lessons learned

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • The highest-value prototype was not the prettiest one. The storyboard and bodystorming round killed the wrong questions early and saved later rework (See: 15-change-by-design/04-building-to-think.md).
  • Selective service beat universal service. Guests liked personalization, but Ember could not operationalize full white-glove treatment for every celebratory table.
  • Dietary reliability is part of delight. Guests do not split emotional and operational experience the way organizations do.
  • AI tools over-produce fields by default. Hospitality teams need fewer, sharper inputs, not more personalization variables.
  • Wizard-of-Oz exposed the real bottleneck. The bottleneck was not app UX. It was backstage ambiguity and timing.
  • A concierge can validate a business model before software exists. Manual routing gave Ember the unit-economics evidence leadership needed (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/06-prototyping-services.md).
  • Prototype reports must be issue-driven. Without GitHub issues and PR gates, the same failure would have been rediscovered across sites.
  • Done differently next time: include accessibility review of confirmation messages in week 2, not after the pilot, and recruit more non-native-language households in the first live cohort.
