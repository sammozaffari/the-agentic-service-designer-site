01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

Aria Telecom served 6.8 million subscribers across mobile, broadband, and converged bundles, yet its complaint journey behaved like six separate services stitched together after the fact. Billing, network, retail, app support, reseller care, and regulatory affairs each owned a slice; the customer experienced the seams. The result was 720,000 complaints per year inside 4.1 million annual support interactions, only 41% first-contact resolution, median complaint closure of 8.6 days, 18,600 ombudsman escalations, and a sharp loyalty penalty after service failure. Over 14 weeks, the redesign treated recovery itself as a designed experience: current-state complaint mapping with justice-gap annotations; a future-state recovery blueprint; AI-authored recovery playbooks, apology ladders, and compensation rules; and a root-cause learning loop that fed billing and network defects back into operations. In the pilot, first-contact resolution rose to 64%, median resolution time fell to 3.7 days, 30-day repeat complaints dropped from 27% to 12%, and 90-day post-complaint churn fell from 14.8% to 9.2%. The core lesson held exactly as Grönroos argues: recovery is not back-office clean-up; it is the moment that decides whether a dissatisfied customer becomes loyal, hostage, or destructive. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/06-service-recovery.md; 20-the-service-profit-chain/04-customer-loyalty-and-profit.md.

02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

Aria Telecom is a fictional converged telecom provider headquartered in Western Europe with 6.8 million active subscribers: 4.9 million mobile lines, 1.6 million fixed broadband households, and 620,000 TV add-ons. Digital maturity is uneven. The self-service app is strong for routine tasks such as top-ups and plan changes, but complaint handling still depends on an aging CRM, separate billing and outage systems, two outsourced call-centre partners, and 410 branded retail stores. Roughly 29% of the base is bundle-heavy, 17% is prepaid, and 14% comes through reseller channels that introduce additional handoffs.

The challenge

The board did not have a “complaints problem” in the abstract. It had a loyalty leak. Of 4.1 million annual support interactions, 720,000 were coded as complaints or escalations. Only 41% were resolved on first contact; 27% reopened inside 30 days; median closure time was 8.6 days; and vulnerable-customer cases crossed 3.2 teams on average before completion. Billing errors generated the largest single cluster of ombudsman referrals, while network complaints carried the highest emotional intensity because customers experienced both the outage and then the silence around the outage.

Furthermore, following TARP’s foundational complaint research, the board recognized that these 720,000 cases were merely the vocal minority. The silent defectors who experienced the same failures without calling in represented an unmeasured, existential churn risk.

Complaint journeys frequently violated all three recovery-justice dimensions:

  • Procedural justice failed when customers had to repeat the same explanation across channels, chase status, or wait for manual callbacks. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/06-service-recovery.md.
  • Interactional justice failed when agents apologized without owning the next step, or hid behind scripts such as “the system is still updating.”
  • Distributive justice failed when compensation was inconsistent: one customer received €10 credit for a duplicate invoice, another spent two weeks arguing over €74 and got nothing.

The operating consequence was expensive failure demand. Complaint contacts cost Aria about €5.20 blended per touch. Each reopened case averaged 2.4 additional interactions. Aria estimated €9.7M annual complaint handling cost before counting churn. The commercial consequence was sharper: complainants who rated recovery “fair” had 88% 12-month retention; those rating it “unfair” fell to 71%. In Jones and Sasser’s terms, unrecovered customers were becoming hostages and defectors in a highly competitive market. See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/04-customer-loyalty-and-profit.md.

Success criteria

Constraints

Budget was capped at €260,000. The engagement had 14 weeks to produce a pilot before the annual consumer-regulation review cycle. Aria could not replace its CRM or billing engine in-year. Regulatory affairs required complaint copy to respect Aria’s policy matrix on acknowledgement, escalation, and ombudsman signposting. Reseller complaints sat partly outside Aria’s direct control. Any vulnerable-customer design had to work across phone, app, SMS, and assisted pathways, not only digital self-service. See: 05-good-services/05-principles-control.md.

Why service design

The complaint journey was a whole-service problem. Customers did not care whether the handoff was between billing operations, network NOC, CRM, or outsourced care; they experienced one story and judged Aria on whether that story felt fair, understandable, and complete. Complaint recovery therefore required both journey mapping and service blueprinting: the journey to expose lived pain, and the blueprint to reveal how the organization produced that pain below the line of visibility. See: 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md. Lou Downe’s Good Services principles were equally relevant: a complaint service should set expectations upfront, avoid dead ends, make human help easy, explain decisions, and require no insider knowledge from users. See: 05-good-services/03-principles-usability.md; 05-good-services/04-principles-trust.md; 05-good-services/05-principles-control.md.

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Diagnose complaint demand and justice gaps

Goal. Understand where complaint journeys break, which failures create the strongest fairness deficit, and where recovery design should intervene first.

Method & SOP. The team combined complaint-log analysis, call listening, ombudsman-case review, and a justice audit against Grönroos’s three dimensions: procedural, interactional, distributive. The SOP was simple: sample cases by complaint type; tag every case for failure origin, recovery actions, handoffs, compensation outcome, and reopen pattern; then score each case on whether the process felt easy, respectful, and fair. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/06-service-recovery.md.

AI execution. OpenCode processed the full 720,000-record complaint archive and trained a classifier that normalized free-text reason codes into one complaint taxonomy across app, voice, store, and reseller channels. The design brief required routing plus sentiment, so the build prompt forced both:

Illustrative OpenCode host command — emitted pack; live case execution unverified.

TERMINAL
opencode run --agent measurer "Read complaints_2025_redacted.parquet with 720000 rows.
Create a complaint-classification pipeline that outputs: complaint_type,
root_cause_owner, sentiment_band, vulnerable_flag_hint, justice_gap_hint,
reopen_risk_score. Train on fixed-seed samples, evaluate on a holdout set,
and export weekly aggregates for billing, outage, reseller, and ombudsman paths."

Claude Code then codified a case-review rubric so three researchers could review 180 cases consistently. Each case review generated structured fields rather than prose notes. GitHub issues captured every repeat fail point with an owner tag such as billing-ledger, outage-comms, reseller-handoff, or script-copy.

Human designer judgment. The human chose the framing: a complaint is not proof of customer irrationality but evidence that the service has already failed once. That judgment shifted the operating language from “deflection” to “recovery,” which mattered politically. The human also decided to separate outage severity from outage communication quality, because many customers were angrier about three days of silence than the original network defect.

Artifact produced. Complaint taxonomy, justice-gap register, failure-demand baseline, and a ranked top-five fail-point list: duplicate bill shock, outage silence, status opacity, reseller ping-pong, and vulnerable-customer dead ends.

4.2 Map the current journey and blueprint the recovery service

Goal. Make the complaint experience visible as one end-to-end journey and trace each pain point to the backstage systems and policies producing it.

Method & SOP. Current-state journey mapping defined phases from the customer’s point of view: report, classify, queue, investigate, resolve, follow through, escalate if needed. Then a canonical service blueprint was built from the same spine: physical evidence, customer actions, frontstage responses, backstage actions, support systems. Fail points and justice gaps were annotated explicitly. See: 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md.

AI execution. Claude Code wrote both the journey map and the future blueprint as mermaid-first artifacts in Git, enabling diffable changes after each workshop. Codex reviewed the draft maps with a deliberately adversarial brief focused on timeline promises and fairness:

Illustrative Codex host command — emitted pack; live case execution unverified.

TERMINAL
codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read README-draft.md sections A1 and A2 plus
aria_policy_complaints_matrix.md. Find any step that violates stated complaint
acknowledgement, escalation, or signposting timelines. Then score each customer-
facing message for procedural, interactional, and distributive justice from 1 to 5.
Flag any apology that owns emotion but not action."

Codex caught three real defects: the first blueprint version signposted ombudsman too late for Aria’s internal legal matrix; the outage path had no proactive explanation during engineering investigation; and the vulnerable-customer path still forced one identity-reverification step that carers could not complete unaided.

Human designer judgment. The designer decided where the complaint journey truly begins: not at “ticket created,” but at the moment the subscriber decides “I have to complain.” That meant signposting, expectation-setting, and pre-complaint dead ends were in scope. The designer also kept reseller complaints inside the same map rather than excluding them as “not fully ours,” because customers do not experience contractual nuance.

Artifact produced. A1 current-state complaint journey, A2 future-state recovery blueprint, and the first version of the traceability table.

4.3 Design the recovery service, playbooks, and copy system

Goal. Translate recovery principles into executable frontline behavior: what to say, what to fix, when to compensate, when to escalate, and how to learn.

Method & SOP. The recovery design used Grönroos’s five-step sequence — acknowledge, empathize, correct, compensate fairly, follow up — but adapted it into a telecom operating rule: fix first, then compensate, then prove the fix held. Compensation without correction was treated as a false recovery. Good Services principles shaped the copy: clear purpose, consistent timelines, no dead ends, human help easy to access, and explanations that non-experts could understand. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/06-service-recovery.md; 05-good-services/03-principles-usability.md; 05-good-services/04-principles-trust.md; 05-good-services/05-principles-control.md.

AI execution. Claude Code generated apology-script variants by complaint type and justice gap, then wrapped them in YAML-like SOP blocks so reviewers could diff them. The prompt forced ownership and next-step specificity:

NOTE
You are RECOVERY-COPY. Draft complaint response copy for Aria Telecom.
Input: complaint_type, customer_impact, investigation_state, compensation_band,
vulnerable_flag.
Rules:
1. Start with acknowledgement in plain language.
2. Name what Aria will do next and by when.
3. Never imply the customer is mistaken until evidence is checked.
4. If compensation applies, state basis and amount clearly.
5. End with a next step, status route, and human contact option.
6. Score your own draft for procedural, interactional, distributive justice 1-5.
Reject drafts where any score is below 4.

GitHub stored the resulting playbooks as code. Every copy change required PR review from operations plus regulatory affairs. A rebuttal-log captured cases where frontline agents rejected AI-generated wording as unsayable or unfair; that log became training data. Codex then ran a “justice-ness” review across generated copy, often catching subtle gaslighting such as “we understand this may have caused inconvenience” in cases where Aria had demonstrably overcharged a customer.

Human designer judgment. The human removed two patterns AI kept producing: over-explaining backend complexity and apologizing before confirming the concrete next step. In this domain, a polished apology that leaves the customer in the queue is worse than a shorter, clearer message with a real commitment.

Artifact produced. Recovery playbooks, apology ladder, compensation matrix, copy review checklist, and GitHub review workflow.

4.4 Pilot the recovery service in live queues

Goal. Test whether the redesigned recovery service improves both fairness and operational outcomes in real complaint conditions.

Method & SOP. Aria piloted two high-volume queues: duplicate-billing complaints and multi-day outage complaints. Frontline agents rehearsed the playbooks through roleplay before go-live. Vulnerable-customer cases were routed to a named assisted path with tighter handoff limits. Recovery decisions were reviewed daily for variance and weekly for root-cause patterns. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/06-service-recovery.md.

AI execution. OpenCode generated daily pilot extracts, mock scenarios for training, and compensation-band lookup tables. Claude Code produced roleplay packs and daily exception summaries. GitHub project boards tracked playbook defects exactly like software defects: missing timeline promise, compensation mismatch, status-notification failure, and reopened case after “resolved” state.

Human designer judgment. The pilot’s most important decision was empowerment. Agents received authority to apply up to €50 compensation without supervisor approval on approved scenarios, and up to €120 when a vulnerable-customer flag or billing proof existed. That is not a technical change; it is an organizational design decision. Without it, the complaint journey would still bottleneck on manager sign-off.

Artifact produced. Pilot scorecard, live compensation rules, rebuttal/error log, and revised playbooks after week 2.

4.5 Measure value and close the learning loop

Goal. Ensure recovery work ends in systemic remediation, not only individual appeasement.

Method & SOP. Every resolved complaint generated five outputs: resolution outcome, justice score, compensation band, root-cause owner, and reopen status. Weekly Pareto reviews routed the largest failure clusters back to billing, network, or reseller operations. This followed the closed-loop learning model exactly: complaints are diagnostic signals, and recovery is incomplete if the root cause is left untouched. See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/06-service-recovery.md.

AI execution. OpenCode aggregated weekly recurrence, reopen, and compensation cost patterns into a dashboard. Claude Code drafted the executive readout from the same query outputs, eliminating hand-typed metrics. GitHub Actions validated the article’s tables and complaint-copy snippets, while the case repo’s CI checked that every playbook referenced a compensation rule, a timeline promise, and a learning-loop destination.

Human designer judgment. The human set the threshold for success: Aria would not declare recovery improved unless both fairness and economics moved together. Faster closure alone was insufficient if distributive justice worsened; higher credits alone were insufficient if reopen rates stayed high.

Artifact produced. Root-cause learning loop, before/after value table, and handover recommendations for scale.

A6 — Templates and playbooks

Recovery playbook template filled for billing-error duplicate bill

NOTE
PLAYBOOK ID: AR-RP-BILL-01
SCENARIO: Duplicate bill or duplicate direct-debit collection
TRIGGER: Customer reports overcharge or billing line repeated across cycles
OWNER: Billing recovery cell

1. ACKNOWLEDGE
- Say what happened in customer language: "I can see you have been billed twice for the same charge."
- Confirm impact: amount, due date, payment state, vulnerability flag.
- Promise next update time before ending the first contact.

2. FIX
- Freeze collections and late-fee automation immediately if duplicate charge appears likely.
- Validate ledger entries, payment events, and tariff history.
- Correct the account before discussing goodwill credit.
- If evidence is incomplete, give a named next step and due-by time.

3. COMPENSATE
- Apply compensation band after correction decision.
- Explain basis: financial loss, effort, time at risk, vulnerability.
- Never offer compensation as substitute for correction.

4. LEARN
- Tag root cause: tariff migration, duplicate payment posting, reseller setup, manual adjustment.
- Record reopen risk and whether customer had to chase.

5. PREVENT
- Create defect ticket if cause is systemic.
- Trigger next-bill confirmation message.
- Review in weekly billing Pareto if >5 similar cases in 7 days.

Apology ladder

Level When used Required language pattern Prohibited pattern
L1 Acknowledge Minor friction, no proven loss yet "I can see why this is frustrating, and I am checking it now." "This may have caused inconvenience."
L2 Own Aria likely caused the issue "This sits with us, and I will take it through to the next update by 16:00." "The system is being updated."
L3 Explain Decision or delay needs clarity "Here is what we found, what it means for your account, and what happens next." "Per policy, your case is under review."

06 · Use cases

Use cases / case studies

Use case A — Billing-error overcharge from a duplicate bill

Scenario. Helena Varga, the vulnerable pensioner persona, sees a duplicate €74 charge and fears the direct debit will leave her short before pension day.

What was designed. The billing-error recovery playbook creates a protected path: first-contact agent confirms the charge, freezes collection risk, sets a due-by time, and routes to the billing recovery cell without forcing Helena to retell the story. Once the ledger defect is confirmed, the account is corrected before compensation is discussed. Helena then receives a next-bill confirmation saying exactly where the correction will appear.

AI working session summary. Claude Code generated three script variants for vulnerable billing errors; frontline review rejected one as too formal, and the rebuttal log recorded why. Codex then reviewed the surviving copy for fairness and signposting. OpenCode generated 40 synthetic duplicate-bill scenarios for agent rehearsal.

Outcome. In pilot, duplicate-bill FCR rose from 36% to 68%, median resolution fell from 11.2 days to 1.8 days, and billing-case reopen rate fell from 31% to 10%.

Use case B — Network outage with three-day silence

Scenario. Marco Duret loses mobile service for three days during a regional outage. Under the old journey, he received one generic “we are aware” message, then nothing. By day two he was comparing competitor eSIM offers.

What was designed. The outage recovery journey adds proactive status and a visible service promise. Investigation remains operationally complex, but the customer story is simple: acknowledgement, next update time, plain-language cause, expected restore window, then compensation only after restoration. Silence is treated as its own service failure.

AI working session summary. OpenCode reconstructed outage complaint timelines from network and CRM logs. Claude Code drafted update-copy variants calibrated to technical certainty levels: suspected, confirmed, restored. Codex rejected one draft for pretending certainty too early.

Outcome. Major-outage repeat contacts per case fell from 2.7 to 1.4, resolution time from 6.3 to 3.1 days, and 90-day churn in the affected cohort from 18.2% to 11.6%.

Use case C — Vulnerable-customer assisted recovery path

Scenario. A low-income fixed-line customer with hearing impairment cannot navigate IVR loops and needs a human-assisted route when service and billing fail together.

What was designed. Aria created a vulnerable-customer assisted path with one owner, tighter callback windows, and capped handoffs. The path supports proxy contact where authorized and makes no demand for internal account knowledge beyond what the customer can reasonably know. See: 05-good-services/05-principles-control.md.

AI working session summary. Claude Code authored the assisted-path SOP; frontline and accessibility reviewers edited it in GitHub. Codex checked the copy for dead ends and explanation quality. The vulnerable flag in the classifier remained suggestive only; human confirmation was mandatory.

Outcome. Vulnerable cases with more than two handoffs fell from 46% to 9%, fairness score rose from 2.1 to 4.3, and ombudsman referrals in this path halved during pilot.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Three SOP families were encoded for agent use, each following the same contract: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

7.1 Justice-based service-recovery SOP

NOTE
SOP SR-01 JUSTICE-BASED RECOVERY
1. Identify complaint type and likely root-cause owner.
2. Detect vulnerable hints but do not auto-declare vulnerability.
3. Acknowledge issue in customer language.
4. State next action and due-by time.
5. Investigate and correct the defect.
6. Apply compensation only after correction basis is known.
7. Explain outcome, basis, and next step.
8. Confirm fix held on next bill or next service state.
9. Tag root cause and recurrence signals.
OUTPUT: case summary, due-by time, justice score, compensation band, follow-up task.
ACCEPTANCE: no apology without action; no closure without explanation; no compensation without recorded basis.
REVIEW GATE: frontline lead + regulatory reviewer on sampled cases.

7.2 Complaint-classification prompt

NOTE
You are COMPLAINT-CLASSIFIER.
Input: transcript or complaint note.
Output fields only:
- complaint_type
- root_cause_owner
- sentiment_band
- vulnerable_flag_hint yes or no
- procedural_gap yes or no
- interactional_gap yes or no
- distributive_gap yes or no
- reopen_risk low medium high
Rules:
- Use customer language, not internal jargon, when summarizing the issue.
- If evidence is ambiguous, choose unknown and explain why.
- Never infer vulnerability from age alone.
- Never infer fraud from anger.

7.3 Compensation decision rules

NOTE
COMPENSATION RULES CR-02
- First decide provider fault: proven, likely, unclear, not found.
- Then decide customer impact: time loss, financial loss, service loss, vulnerability multiplier.
- Map to band C0-C4.
- If provider fault is unclear, do not deny unfairly; state what evidence is pending and next review time.
- If vulnerability multiplier present, escalate one band unless disproportional.
- Always record basis in one sentence a customer could understand.

7.4 Guardrails

  • Regulatory timelines. Complaint copy must use Aria’s approved timeline matrix for acknowledgement, next update, final response, and ombudsman signposting. Agents can draft; only approved policy values can publish.
  • No gaslighting copy. Phrases that minimize proven harm, shift blame to the user, or hide behind generic system language fail review.
  • Vulnerable-customer flags. AI may suggest a flag; a trained human must confirm the path.
  • Evidence discipline. Generated explanations must cite the case facts available, not invent backend causes.
  • Human sign-off points. Compensation exceptions, vulnerability confirmation, ombudsman letters, and any copy change that affects legal rights.

7.5 Failure modes and mitigations

Failure mode Why it happens Mitigation
Agent apologizes without fixing LLMs optimize for tone before operations SOP requires next action + due-by time before apology is accepted
AI over-classifies vulnerability Pattern-matching from age or emotion Human confirmation mandatory
Compensation becomes substitute for correction Teams want fast closure Fix-first-then-compensate rule in playbook and audit
Copy drifts across channels Separate teams edit locally GitHub single-source copy library and CI
Classifier mistakes anger for fraud or abuse Sentiment conflated with intent Separate sentiment and risk labels; human review

The meta-SOP across the engagement was consistent: diagnose → map → blueprint → playbook → pilot → measure → learn. No playbook shipped before the journey and blueprint aligned; no compensation rule shipped before fairness and legal review; no success claim was made without reopen and churn evidence.

Causal logic. The economics improved precisely because Aria spent money more intelligently, not because it spent less on customers. Procedural justice improvements reduced chasing and reopening. Interactional justice improved fairness perception, reducing escalations and churn. Distributive justice increased average compensation on the cases that truly warranted it, but total cost-to-serve still fell because repeat demand collapsed. This is classic Service Profit Chain logic: internal service quality for frontline teams creates better external value, which raises loyalty and reduces destructive defection. See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/04-customer-loyalty-and-profit.md.

A simple retained-value view made the commercial case legible. On the 118,000 pilot complaints, a 5.6-point reduction in 90-day churn preserved roughly 6,608 customers. At an illustrative annual contribution of €86 per retained subscriber, that is about €568k annualized retained contribution, before counting reduced ombudsman handling and complaint-cost savings.

09 · AI tool playbook

AI tool playbook

Claude Code

Role: operating system for the engagement. It encoded the recovery SOPs, authored the journey and blueprint diagrams, drafted apology-script variants, created the playbooks, and summarized daily pilot exceptions. It excelled at turning service-design methods into structured, reusable operational artifacts. It failed at knowing what frontline staff would actually say aloud without review. Human takeover point: framing, facilitation, and final fairness judgment.

OpenCode

Role: complaint-classification engine, training-data generation, and pilot analytics. It processed the 720,000-record archive, generated the mock dataset and training cases, and maintained weekly before/after measurement. It excelled at multi-file agentic build work and reproducible aggregates. It failed when prompts left policy nuance underspecified; it would produce clean pipelines with the wrong business rule. Human takeover point: defining label taxonomies, thresholds, and interpretation.

Codex

Role: adversarial reviewer for compliance and justice. Aria used it specifically to challenge complaint timelines, fairness of generated copy, and whether drafts truly explained decisions. It excelled at second-opinion critique when prompted narrowly. It failed when treated as an oracle; every finding had to be checked against Aria’s approved policy pack.

GitHub

Role: playbook-as-code and review system. Issues represented fail points and root-cause tickets. PRs reviewed copy, playbooks, and compensation rules. Actions CI enforced consistency on diagrams and complaint copy. Illustrative workflow:

10 · Lessons learned

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • Silence is a service failure in its own right. The biggest insight from outage complaints was that customers often tolerated the underlying defect more than the unkept promise of the update.
  • Apology quality without procedural justice creates rage faster. “I’m sorry” followed by “please wait 5 working days” is not recovery.
  • Compensation consistency matters more than raw generosity. Customers accepted smaller credits when the rationale was clear and the defect was already fixed.
  • Vulnerability should widen care, not narrow autonomy. Assisted routes worked best when they were faster and more respectful, not paternalistic.
  • AI-generated copy drifts toward soft evasiveness. Gaslighting phrases are the hidden tax of LLM writing in complaint contexts; a justice review gate is mandatory.
  • Root-cause learning must be owned, not admired. Weekly Pareto charts changed nothing until each cluster had a named operational owner.
  • Done differently next time: bring reseller partners into the mapping workshop earlier, and baseline the assisted-path accessibility audit in week 1 rather than after the first prototype round.
