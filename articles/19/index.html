Transformative Services & Behavior Change: Reframing Metabolic-Risk Coaching at Sprout Wellness

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Sprout Wellness is a fictional composite client built from recurring patterns across European digital health-coaching services; no employer, member, coach, clinician, or dataset in this article represents a real organization or person. All numbers, screenshots, journey artifacts, and datasets are illustrative and anonymized. The article shares the method, guardrails, and AI-first operating system of the work rather than client specifics. The engagement itself was run AI-first: Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were the primary tools for research synthesis, intervention logic, simulation, review, and governance, while the human designer held framing, ethics, and clinical-boundary decisions.

1. Executive summary

Sprout Wellness, a Copenhagen-headquartered EU digital health-coaching service with 380,000 active users across B2C subscriptions and employer-funded programmes, had a problem familiar to preventive health services: the product measured usage, while the client bought outcomes. Its existing metabolic-risk programme looked busy on a dashboard — articles opened, reminders sent, steps logged — yet only 28% of enrolled members reached week 12, only 22% recovered within a week after a missed check-in, and employers increasingly asked what they were actually paying for. Over 20 design weeks plus a 12-week pilot tail, the service was rebuilt as a transformative service rather than a content programme: the member became the hero, the service became a mentor and recovery scaffold, behavior-change techniques were explicitly mapped to touchpoints, relapse was designed as part of the journey rather than treated as failure, and clinical-safety gates were embedded into every automated flow. The redesigned steady habits programme lifted enrolment from 31% to 43%, week-12 completion from 28% to 49% overall, relapse recovery from 22% to 57%, and member NPS from +11 to +34, with the strongest cohort projecting a 1.8:1 employer ROI on a 12-month view. 2. The brief

Client portrait

Sprout Wellness sits in the crowded middle of the European digital-prevention market: a strong coaching proposition, a competent app, and intense employer pressure to prove that lifestyle coaching translates into measurable risk reduction. The business serves roughly 380,000 monthly active users across the DACH region, the Nordics, and Benelux. About 58% are B2C members on low-cost subscriptions; 42% enter through employer-funded or specialist-referral programmes. The company manages 146 enterprise clients, 62 health coaches, five part-time clinical advisors, and a benefits-platform stack that routes eligibility, consent, payroll support, and referral workflows. It is digitally mature — event telemetry, experimentation, content CMS, multilingual messaging, and an existing nurse-review workflow — but behaviourally immature: the service had been assembled as a library plus reminders, with only loose ties between coaching touchpoints and the outcomes the service claimed to support.

The challenge

The metabolic-risk programme targeted employees identified through annual screenings, pharmacist screenings, or specialist referrals as having elevated risk of pre-diabetes or broader metabolic syndrome. Historical cohort analysis across 26,400 members and 1.8 million engagement events showed three structural flaws. First, the intervention framed the product as hero: a generic week-1 lesson dump, a step target copied from consumer fitness apps, and a dashboard that congratulated logging rather than change. Second, it confused co-production with co-creation. Members were asked to fill forms, log meals, and self-schedule check-ins — labour for the platform — without enough support to turn that labour into value-in-context (See: 12-service-dominant-logic/03-value-co-creation.md). Third, relapse was absent from the logic. If a member missed two check-ins, the system escalated into louder nudges; the implicit story was that non-compliance was personal failure. Shift workers, post-pregnancy members, and members with previous stop-start histories churned fastest.

The baseline numbers made the problem legible. Of eligible members, 31% enrolled. Of those enrolled, 28% reached week 12, 36% hit weekly goals from weeks 4–12, and just 18% of consenting members showed an improved biometric proxy by programme end. Coaches spent 74 minutes per member over 12 weeks, mostly on manual chasing. Member NPS sat at +11. Employers received beautiful dashboards and weak answers.

Constraints

Budget was capped at €195,000 for research, design, prototype, and pilot instrumentation, with no bespoke clinical platform rebuild. The programme had to run through existing employer eligibility feeds and the current coach console. GDPR and employer privacy boundaries were strict: individual health data could never be visible to line managers, and employer reporting required aggregation thresholds of at least 15 members per cell. Any copy implying diagnosis, treatment, reversal, or guaranteed biomarker improvement required clinician sign-off; the service could support behaviour change but could not impersonate medical care. Shift workers and parents returning from pregnancy-related risk could not be penalized by rigid time windows. Finally, several enterprise clients had active works councils, so coercive mechanics — streak debt, shame nudges, leaderboard exposure, payroll-linked penalties — were prohibited.

Why service design

This was a service-design problem because the outcome lived in the member's life, not inside the product. Transformative services succeed when they expand real capability and wellbeing over time, especially in the messy daily ecology where stress, family routines, shift patterns, pharmacies, benefits systems, and primary care all intersect (See: 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/05-transformative-services-and-social-innovation.md). Service-dominant thinking sharpens the diagnosis: Sprout does not deliver health value complete; it proposes resources that members must integrate with food, time, social support, and medical advice in their own context (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/02-service-dominant-thinking.md). In that sense, the environment is pure white water: high coupling, changing constraints, and no fixed, tidy problem statement (See: 11-design-unbound/01-the-white-water-world.md). A product-only redesign would polish the app. A service-design intervention could reframe the hero, redesign the relationships, and build a recovery-capable system.

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

The engagement followed the Double Diamond, but the working posture was explicitly white-water and probe-led. Discover mapped daily ecology and value breakdowns. Define recast the member as hero, the service as mentor, and the programme as a capability-building system rather than a content series. Develop turned that logic into touchpoints, scripts, and safety gates. Deliver piloted three cohorts with intervention versioning, ethics review, and weekly adaptation. The TISDD cycle ran underneath it: research, synthesize, map, prototype, measure. Kumar's seven modes were visible as well: sensing intent, knowing context, knowing people, framing insights, exploring concepts, framing solutions, and realizing offerings. Agents handled the extraction, drafting, and telemetry; the human designer handled framing, ethics, and boundary judgments.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Discover: map value in daily life, not in the app

Goal. Understand where metabolic-risk members actually succeed or fail in daily life, and where the current service co-destroys value.

Method & SOP. Four methods ran together. (1) Daily-Ecology Journey Mapping captured routines across work, food, stress, and care coordination rather than app screens alone (See: 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/05-transformative-services-and-social-innovation.md). (2) Value-in-Use contextual inquiry documented what members had to contribute — time, family support, food access, sleep, devices, emotional bandwidth — before the programme could work at all (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/02-service-dominant-thinking.md). (3) White Water Context Assessment tested whether the challenge was stable enough for a fixed intervention or needed adaptive probes (See: 11-design-unbound/01-the-white-water-world.md). (4) Hero casting interviews kept asking why a member wanted change until the actual stakes emerged: not "lose weight," but "avoid starting medication," "keep up with my child," or "stop feeling that work shifts have broken my body" (See: 10-the-users-journey/02-the-user-as-hero.md).

AI execution. OpenCode built the baseline pipeline from historical cohorts, eligibility files, and anonymized coach notes. Representative command:

opencode run "Build scripts/metabolic_baseline.py. Read exports/eligibility.csv,
exports/checkins.csv, exports/coach_notes.csv. Output cohort_baseline.csv with
enrolment, week12 completion, lapse week, recovery-within-7d, goal attainment,
consenting biometric proxy improvement. Hash all member IDs."

Claude Code dispatched three parallel evidence-extraction agents: shift workers, post-pregnancy members, and repeat relapsers. Each agent returned verbatim quotes plus line references; if evidence was missing, the schema required NO EVIDENCE. Representative prompt:

You are a service-design research analyst. Read diary weeks and interview
transcripts for the shift-worker segment only. Extract: explicit want,
underlying need, daily barriers, resource deficits, moments of shame,
recovery strategies that already work, and named ecosystem actors.
Return markdown table with verbatim quote and line reference for every row.
Never infer beyond the quote.

Codex did two things early: it assembled a research brief on BCTTv1 so the team would use consistent terms later, and it challenged the team's first instinct that "motivation" was the central problem. Its strongest counter-argument was useful: the data showed resource breakdowns more than low intent — food availability on late shifts, childcare interruptions, and confusion after screening results. That pushed the work away from persuasion and toward support.

GitHub was the system of record. Every research question had an issue. Every evidence synthesis merged by PR only after the designer checked quote integrity and the employer sponsor confirmed plausibility. No synthesis was allowed to cite aggregate employer data without the DPO label.

Human designer judgment. The designer decided that diary capture had to include weekends and night shifts, not just neat weekday routines. She also rejected the client's first draft segmentation label, non-compliant members, and replaced it with relapse-prone context because the original framing made the user the problem. That linguistic change altered the whole engagement.

Artifact produced. A current-state journey map showing the old product-as-hero arc, a white-water diagnostic, and a relapse audit proving that the service treated normal lapses as exceptions rather than design requirements.

4.2 Define: make the member the hero and the intervention legible

Goal. Translate evidence into a service logic that links behaviour-change techniques, human support, safety gates, and measurable outcomes.

Method & SOP. The defining stack combined User-as-Hero framing, Value Co-Creation mapping, and Transformative Wellbeing Outcome Mapping (See: 10-the-users-journey/02-the-user-as-hero.md; 12-service-dominant-logic/03-value-co-creation.md; 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/05-transformative-services-and-social-innovation.md). The designer wrote the dramatic question first: Will this member build steady daily habits despite shift disruption, prior relapse, and health anxiety so that they feel capable and medically safer in ordinary life? From there, the team mapped the service as an invitation to co-create value: Sprout contributes coaching, prompts, scripts, and analytics; the member contributes time, honesty, local food choices, and consent; employers contribute funding and protected participation time; GPs and pharmacists remain clinical safety anchors. The intervention logic then specified which BCTs belonged at which touchpoints and what mechanism each was supposed to activate.

AI execution. Claude Code did the first BCT-to-touchpoint pass. Representative prompt:

Map the steady-habits service to BCTTv1 techniques. Use only techniques that
fit preventive metabolic-risk coaching and do not require diagnosis.
For each touchpoint return: week, touchpoint, BCT label, mechanism of action,
risk of coercion, evidence quote, and required human gate.
Reject any technique that depends on guilt, public comparison, or false scarcity.

The output was not accepted raw. Codex reviewed it against the official BCT taxonomy and a clinical-claims rubric. Representative command:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read docs/bct_map_v1.md and claims/allowed_language.md.
Verify BCT labels against BCTTv1 naming. Flag any touchpoint that implies
diagnosis, treatment, guaranteed prevention, or biomarker change without
clinician review. Return corrected table with rationale."

GitHub enforced the governance. The repo carried three mandatory PR labels for intervention changes: clinical-claim-review, ethics-gate, and consent-impact. A PR without all required approvals could not merge into the pilot branch. Claude drafted the intervention logic model, but the designer and clinical advisor edited the safety boundary lines by hand.

Human designer judgment. Two judgement calls mattered. First, the designer refused to anchor the service to a streak. The clinical advisor liked its simplicity; the designer argued that streak loss would turn relapse into identity collapse for the exact members the programme most needed to support. Second, she split goals into minimum viable habit and stretch habit, so shift workers and parents were not forced into one normative rhythm.

Artifact produced. The future-state transformation journey, the intervention logic model, the stakeholder map, and the core design principles: user owns the verbs; relapse is planned; automation never imitates medicine; employer reporting remains aggregate only.

4.3 Develop: prototype the touchpoints, scripts, and relapse machinery

Goal. Turn the intervention logic into a service that coaches can run, members can live with, and employers can trust.

Method & SOP. This phase worked as problem-solution co-evolution (See: 11-design-unbound/02-design-as-a-way-of-acting.md). Every prototype was treated as a probe, not a finished answer. The team tested goal-setting scripts, weekly check-ins, relapse flows, peer-accountability options, and employer-report formats. A co-production vs co-creation audit cut busywork: several legacy forms disappeared because they extracted labour from members without increasing value-in-context (See: 12-service-dominant-logic/03-value-co-creation.md). Service blueprinting then positioned every touchpoint across frontstage, backstage, support, and safety layers.

AI execution. OpenCode built the journey simulator and relapse-risk flags from telemetry. Representative command:

opencode run "Create scripts/relapse_sim.py. Inputs: cohort_baseline.csv,
weekly_touchpoints.yaml, coach_capacity.csv. Simulate 12-week journeys for
control, steady-habits-v1, and steady-habits-v2-relapse cohorts. Output weekly
checkin demand, expected relapse flags, coach minutes, and sensitivity bands."

Claude Code authored the service scripts and SOP-as-code templates. One useful loop produced a weekly check-in prompt, then rewrote it against an anti-coercion checklist, then rewrote it again for CEFR B1 plain language. Codex acted as the hostile reviewer whenever copy drifted into medical territory:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Review scripts/week4_checkin.md.
Flag any sentence that sounds like medical advice, blame, or unsupported outcome
promise. Suggest wording that preserves the BCT while keeping the member as hero."

GitHub stored intervention versions like product releases. v1 was the structured 12-week core. v2 added the relapse-recovery layer: smaller fallback goals, coach triage, and alternate channels for members who stopped opening the app. Actions ran validate-mermaid, claim-lint, and consent-schema on every PR.

Human designer judgment. Coach rehearsal changed the service more than wireframes did. In the first table read, coaches said the week-4 message still sounded like a disappointed schoolteacher. The designer rewrote the tone live with them: shorter sentences, no compliance language, and the recovery offer before the explanation. She also inserted the pause and refer branch for any symptom or reading that might require clinical input; no agent was allowed to infer health safety from silence.

Artifact produced. The future-state service blueprint, the week-by-week BCT touchpoint table, the relapse-handling flow, the script pack, and the ethics checklist.

4.4 Deliver: pilot with versioning, ethics gates, and measured adaptation

Goal. Pilot the redesigned programme in live employer settings, compare cohorts, and decide what scales.

Method & SOP. The delivery model followed the white-water rule of reversible probes rather than a big-bang release (See: 11-design-unbound/01-the-white-water-world.md). Three cohorts ran in parallel: control-standard coaching, steady habits v1, and steady habits v2 relapse. Weekly readouts reviewed operational metrics, member experience, safety events, and employer reporting quality. The measurement model linked coach quality and service responsiveness to member behaviour, then to proxy outcomes, then to employer value.

AI execution. GitHub Projects tracked cohort readiness: eligibility feed passed, consent copy approved, coach training complete, reporting template approved, and safety escalation owners confirmed. OpenCode produced the weekly readout pack. Claude Code wrote the narrative readout in plain language for steering meetings, with every claim linked back to a metric or quote. Codex did the adversarial review at the end of week 12, focusing on whether the team was overstating health impact from short-term evidence.

A representative Claude prompt for the weekly steering pack:

Read data/week08_metrics.csv, risks/week08_flags.csv, and notes/week08_retros.md.
Write a one-page steering summary with: what changed, what probably caused it,
what remains uncertain, which claims are safe to make externally, and what
experiment should run next week. Every causal statement must cite evidence or be
marked HYPOTHESIS.

Human designer judgment. The designer chaired the ethics gate and retained veto power over any change that increased pressure without clear benefit. One proposed employer dashboard showed members by manager hierarchy; she killed it. Another proposal would have auto-notified members after two missed check-ins at 07:00 local time. Shift-worker interviews had already shown how harmful fixed-hour assumptions were, so that rule died as well.

Artifact produced. The outcome KPI tree, the before/after table, the pilot cohort charts, and the rollout decision note: scale the relapse layer, keep clinician review mandatory, and never sell the programme as a substitute for medical care.

Template 4 — Relapse-recovery protocol

RELAPSE RECOVERY PROTOCOL
Trigger: first missed check-in or goal attainment below threshold for 7 days
1. classify the lapse
   - schedule disruption
   - emotional overload
   - illness or medical concern
   - digital fatigue
2. run the safety screen
   - if medical concern -> clinician review and GP/pharmacist route
3. choose the recovery move
   - schedule disruption -> smaller habit for 3 days
   - emotional overload -> self-kindness script plus coach call
   - digital fatigue -> switch from app to SMS or voice
   - repeated lapse -> peer support option and review goal fit
4. re-check in 72 hours
5. if no recovery in 7 days, coach case review
6. document what helped so relapse becomes learning, not stigma

6. Use cases

Use case A — Retained-at-risk employee cohort in a logistics employer

Scenario. A 2,400-employee depot network in Belgium had a cluster of metabolic-risk employees already known to occupational health, but prior wellbeing campaigns were ignored as corporate hygiene theatre. The new cohort targeted 186 eligible employees with the steady habits v2 relapse pathway.

What was designed. The key move was protected participation time: each member could schedule one 15-minute paid check-in window during work hours every fortnight. The mini flow was simple — invite in member language, week-1 goal contract, week-4 recovery branch if needed, week-12 exit path into maintenance or GP follow-up.

Step Touchpoint Designed difference
1 Employer invite Benefits lead writes sponsor note; no manager surveillance language
2 Kickoff Shift-safe goal setting with minimum viable habit
3 Relapse branch SMS recovery option for workers who avoid app after a bad week
4 Exit Aggregate team report only; no named data

AI working session. Claude Code drafted the invite variants, OpenCode simulated coach load by depot shift pattern, and Codex reviewed whether the employer note implied obligation. Representative prompt:

Draft three employer invite variants for depot shift workers.
Keep the member as hero, state the privacy boundary explicitly, and avoid any
phrase that sounds mandatory or compliance-led.

Outcome. Enrolment reached 46% of eligible employees, week-12 completion hit 57%, and 33% of consenting members improved at least one validated proxy. The employer's modeled ROI for this cohort was 1.9:1, driven mostly by retention in the programme and lower follow-up demand on occupational health.

Use case B — Post-pregnancy metabolic-risk referral

Scenario. Sprout partnered with a hospital outpatient team to support women flagged after pregnancy-related glucose complications. The prior generic programme felt absurdly tone-deaf: long lessons, fixed exercise assumptions, and no space for sleep deprivation.

What was designed. The service changed three things: goals were framed around recovery and energy, not weight-loss performance; check-ins could run asynchronously by voice note; and the safety branch routed feeding, medication, or symptom questions directly away from automation.

Step Touchpoint Designed difference
1 Referral onboarding Clinician-approved welcome pack; plain-language consent
2 Weekly rhythm Voice-note check-in option for interrupted routines
3 Safety gate Direct clinical escalation for medication or symptom questions
4 Handoff Exit plan links to GP or postpartum clinic when needed

AI working session. OpenCode helped prototype the voice-note flow. Claude wrote the empathetic weekly script. Codex challenge-reviewed every phrase that might imply medical advice. Representative command:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Review postpartum_checkin_v2.md.
Flag any wording that could be interpreted as clinical advice. Keep the supportive
behavior-change intent but remove unsafe phrasing."

Outcome. This segment showed the highest member-reported usefulness score in the pilot. Week-12 completion reached 52%, 61% maintained check-ins through week 8, and 35% of consenting members improved at least one validated proxy. Safety escalation was used in 9% of cases, which the clinical team considered a success rather than a failure because automation stopped pretending to know medicine.

Use case C — Relapse-prone yo-yo segment re-engagement narrative

Scenario. Members with two or more prior dropouts were previously excluded from employer success stories because they made the dashboard look bad. In reality, they were the acid test of whether Sprout was designing for transformation or for neat vanity metrics.

What was designed. The programme rewrote the narrative. The member entered with an explicit recovery plan on day 1, not only with a behaviour goal. At the first lapse, the coach referenced the recovery plan and offered a lighter goal rather than a warning. At the second lapse, the system switched channel before it escalated tone.

Step Touchpoint Designed difference
1 Day-1 contract Recovery step written before optimism fades
2 First lapse Self-kindness note and 72-hour restart
3 Repeated lapse Channel switch plus coach review
4 Week-12 story Progress framed as restart skill, not perfect adherence

AI working session. Claude Code was especially useful here because it could generate multiple recovery-script variants and then rewrite them against the anti-coercion checklist. OpenCode's simulation showed that a channel switch at the second lapse reduced coach minutes more than louder push reminders did. Codex caught one failure mode: an early draft said get back on track; reviewers said the phrase carried moral judgment, so it was replaced by restart from today.

Outcome. Historically this segment completed at 21%. In the pilot, steady habits v2 relapse lifted week-12 completion to 49%, with 58% of first lapses recovering inside seven days. Member comments shifted from apology language to problem-solving language — a small but important sign that the hero had changed.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

This article encoded six SOP families for agents, all following the same contract: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate. The pattern mattered more than the model. Claude Code excelled when it received a strict schema and real evidence. OpenCode excelled when the job was simulation, data transformation, or CSV generation. Codex excelled when asked to be adversarial: verify taxonomy names, challenge health claims, question causal leaps. GitHub held the gates.

Encoded SOPs in this engagement:

  1. Behavior-change mapping SOP. Input: touchpoint list + evidence register. Output: BCT map with mechanism, coercion risk, and human gate. Acceptance: every BCT cited to evidence and safe under the claims rubric.
  2. Intervention content governance SOP. Input: draft message or script. Output: annotated content with claim risks, tone risks, and required reviewers. Acceptance: no diagnosis/treatment language without clinician sign-off.
  3. Relapse-risk triage SOP. Input: telemetry + member context. Output: recovery path recommendation, not just a probability score. Acceptance: recovery path can be enacted by a coach in under 2 minutes.
  4. Ethics and consent SOP. Input: new data field, new channel, or new employer report. Output: consent impact note and DPO checklist. Acceptance: explain to a member in one sentence what changed and why.
  5. Weekly readout SOP. Input: metrics and notes. Output: what changed / why / what is uncertain / what next. Acceptance: every causal statement evidentially backed or explicitly tagged HYPOTHESIS.
  6. Clinical-claims lint SOP. Input: any external-facing copy. Output: safe claims list, blocked claims, escalation requirements. Acceptance: clinician approval required for any borderline sentence.

Meta-SOP for the whole engagement. Research → synthesize → map → prototype → measure → deliver. Each phase had a gate. No mapping without evidence. No script without claim lint. No cohort change without ethics review. No external result statement without denominator and scope.

Representative prompt template:

You are executing a service-design SOP.
Method: [name]
Evidence sources: [paths]
Output schema: [exact table or YAML]
Acceptance criteria:
1. Every non-obvious claim cites evidence.
2. If evidence is absent, write NO EVIDENCE.
3. If copy enters medical territory, stop and flag CLINICAL REVIEW REQUIRED.
Review gate: [designer / coach lead / clinical advisor / DPO]
Return only the schema.

Guardrails. Hallucination control came from evidence-linked extraction, not from optimistic prompting. Privacy was protected through hashed IDs, aggregate employer reporting, and explicit consent scopes. Human sign-off was mandatory at four points: narrative framing, BCT selection for sensitive touchpoints, clinical-claims review, and employer reporting. GitHub made those approvals visible.

Failure modes and mitigations.

  • AI generated subtle health claims. Mitigation: claim lint plus clinical advisor PR review.
  • Risk model over-read silence as non-compliance. Mitigation: add channel and schedule context before flagging relapse.
  • Scripts sounded like corporate wellness speak. Mitigation: coach table reads and CEFR B1 rewrites.
  • Too much data tempted the team to measure whatever was available. Mitigation: KPI tree limited metrics to those tied to member value and employer decisions.
  • Employer stakeholders kept asking for named data. Mitigation: design and DPO held a hard aggregate-only boundary.

The causal logic followed service-profit-chain reasoning but translated into preventive-health terms (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/01-the-service-profit-chain.md). Better internal quality — clearer scripts, more accurate relapse routing, safer claims, lower coaching waste — improved the member experience. That, in turn, increased check-ins, goal attainment, and recovery confidence. Those behaviour outcomes improved validated proxy outcomes among consenting members and reduced follow-up demand for employers. The service did not create value alone; it created better conditions for members to co-create value in their own lives (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/02-service-dominant-thinking.md; 12-service-dominant-logic/03-value-co-creation.md).

The KPI tree in A6 was the measurement backbone. The most important measurement lesson was negative: usage metrics alone were demoted. Lesson opens, content views, and streak counts survived only if they explained a behavioural or outcome change. Otherwise they were dropped from steering.

9. AI tool playbook

Claude Code — intervention logic, synthesis, artifact authoring. Claude Code ran the research-analysis agents, drafted the journey maps, wrote the BCT-touchpoint matrix, and maintained the repo narrative. Representative usage: claude "Read evidence/. Draft the week-by-week touchpoint map for steady habits. Use BCTTv1 labels only, cite evidence, and flag coercion risk." It excelled at consistent artifact authoring across many sections. It failed when prompts were vague; the first BCT draft became generic until the schema tightened. The human took over for framing, anti-coercion taste, and clinical-boundary decisions.

OpenCode — telemetry pipelines, simulations, and mock datasets. OpenCode built the baseline pipeline, the relapse simulator, and the CSV sample used in this article. Representative command: opencode run "Create scripts/relapse_sim.py ..." It excelled at deterministic data work and scenario modeling. It failed at nuance in member-facing language, which is why it never wrote final scripts without Claude or human review.

Codex — deep research, claim review, adversarial challenge. Codex verified BCT naming against the official taxonomy site, challenged causal leaps, and reviewed any copy that might look like medical advice. Representative command: codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Review docs/bct_map_v1.md ..." It excelled at saying no and at surfacing hidden overclaiming. It failed when asked for too much repo context at once; narrow, document-specific reviews worked best.

GitHub — governance, versioning, and experiment control. GitHub Issues held research questions and decision records. PR templates enforced labels: ethics-gate, clinical-claim-review, consent-impact. Actions ran Mermaid validation, claims lint, and consent-schema checks. Cohort definitions were versioned as code, so steady habits v1 and v2 relapse were auditable artefacts rather than verbal descriptions. GitHub excelled at making governance real. It failed only when reviewers tried to shortcut the gate; branch protection solved that.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • Relapse design was the leverage point. The largest behaviour lift did not come from more educational content; it came from treating restart as a designed skill.
  • Co-production is easy to inflate accidentally. Health services love forms and tracking. Every extra field should have to justify itself in value-in-use terms.
  • Clinical boundaries must be visible in the workflow, not buried in policy. The claim lint gate changed more copy than any brand review did.
  • Shift work breaks most default service assumptions. Time windows, meal advice, and reminder cadences all had to be redesigned when the team actually studied night shifts.
  • Member dignity is a design variable. Shame language had operational consequences: once it was removed, recovery improved.
  • Employer ROI is downstream, not primary. When the team chased employer-proof too early, it produced surveillance temptations and worse member trust.
  • Simulation is useful, but rehearsal is better. OpenCode predicted coach load well; only live table reads revealed which scripts sounded human.
