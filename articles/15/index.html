Co-Design Workshops, AI-Facilitated: Night Buses Designed by the People Who Ride Them at Meridian Move

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Meridian Move is a fictional composite client, assembled from patterns observed across several European regional transport authorities and demand-responsive transport schemes; no real authority, resident, or driver is depicted. All names, figures, datasets, and quotes are illustrative and anonymized. The article shares knowledge and process — facilitation SOPs, AI prompts, ethics protocols, artifacts — never client specifics. The engagement ran AI-first: Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were the primary working tools, while facilitation, consent, safeguarding, and every decision about what the service should become stayed with humans — and, by design, with the residents themselves.

1. Executive summary

Meridian Move, the public transport brand of a fictional European region of 178,000 residents, was about to lose its evening on-demand minibus service, Meridian Flex. Ridership in low-density hours had fallen 31% in three years, only 41% of households in the rural villages sat within 400 metres of an evening pickup point, and the regional council was preparing to cut the €627,000 annual budget at the next review. The authority's first instinct was a consultancy report and a redesigned app. Instead, over 18 weeks, one service designer amplified by an AI agent team ran a participatory co-design programme: AI digested 380 survey responses and 22 community interviews into persona cards and tension maps; workshop kits were produced in four languages; 104 residents — elderly night-shift carers, warehouse and care workers, students, parents — designed the future service across 6 community workshops and 3 youth sessions, with Claude Code live-synthesizing sticky notes mid-session and OpenCode tallying dot-votes in real time. The result was a resident-authored service concept — community stops, a Buddy Ride scheme, a driver-friending network, and an ethical flat evening fare — a 6-week implementation plan, and a 12-week pilot on two night routes. Pilot corridors saw weekly evening rides rise 58%, non-app bookings reach 31% of all bookings, and perceived safety after dark move from 2.3 to 3.8 out of 5. The council voted 11–2 to fund the scale-up, and the co-design panel became a permanent governance body.

What you will learn:

  • How to run a genuinely participatory co-design programme — not a consultation theatre — with AI doing the digestion, translation, and synthesis heavy lifting while residents keep decision authority (See: 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/03-co-design-and-participation.md).
  • How to facilitate workshops as designed experiences: run sheets, hierarchy neutralization, silent-majority cards, and 24-hour synthesis, with agents compressing the overnight work to minutes (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/08-facilitating-workshops.md).
  • How to prevent AI from flattening marginalised voices: stratified synthesis, quote sign-off, and adversarial review of who is missing from the summary.

2. The brief

Client portrait

Meridian Move is the transport brand of the fictional Velderland region: the town of Kesselt (62,000 residents) plus 14 villages spread over 210 km². It runs fixed-line buses by day and, since 2022, Meridian Flex — an evening on-demand minibus service (6 vehicles, 18:00–01:00) bookable only through an app. Flex carries roughly 64,000 rides a year from 2,400 registered accounts, at an operating cost of €627,000 (€9.80 per ride). The ridership is majority-diverse: 38% of account holders were born abroad or are first-generation, and the evening service disproportionately serves elderly residents, night-shift care and warehouse workers, and students. Digital maturity is middling: the authority has a competent web team but no service design capability, and the Flex app is Dutch-only with card-only payment.

The challenge

Late-evening ridership had declined 31% over three years (1,710 to 1,180 weekly rides). Coverage analysis showed only 41% of households in the low-density villages within 400 metres of an evening pickup point. Complaints ran at 9.4 per 1,000 rides, dominated by "the bus never came" and "I can't book without the app." A member survey put perceived safety while waiting after dark at 2.3 out of 5. The political context made it urgent: the council's mobility committee had flagged the €627,000 Flex budget for review, and a straightforward cut was the default option. The authority's transport director suspected — correctly, it turned out — that the service was failing the people who needed it most, not that demand had disappeared.

Constraints

Budget €118,000 all-in, including participant honoraria; 18 weeks to a pilot charter the council could vote on; public-procurement rules governing any vehicle or infrastructure change; GDPR plus public-sector records law; accessibility obligations under national equality legislation; and a hard political constraint — the council would not fund anything perceived as "designed in an office by consultants," so legitimate, visible community authorship was itself a success criterion.

Why service design

The problem was not a vehicle-scheduling problem; it was a legitimacy and participation problem. Co-design here is not an empathy technique but a reconfiguration of who gets to decide: the Scandinavian participatory tradition holds that those affected by a system have a democratic right to shape it (See: 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/03-co-design-and-participation.md). The failure modes — an app-only front door for a digitally excluded ridership, stops sited for planners' convenience rather than riders' safety — were exactly the kind that residents can see and staff cannot. And because the design had to survive a council vote, the process needed Sanders and Stappers' full say-do-make repertoire: surveys and interviews for what people say, ridership data for what they do, and generative make-tools for the tacit fears and hopes they could not put into words (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/03-generative-and-co-design-methods.md).

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

The engagement ran as an "AI-augmented co-design factory": a strict rhythm of pre-workshop digestion, live sessions, overnight synthesis, and validation loops, with AI in a scribe-and-synthesizer role and residents in the decider role. The oscillation between divergence (open community listening, generative making) and convergence (dot-voting, concept selection, blueprinting) follows Brown's mental matrix — the two modes were never mixed inside a session (See: 15-change-by-design/03-the-mental-matrix.md). The article therefore uses four named phases — Pre-workshop, Workshops, Synthesis, and Validation — instead of the default Discover-Define-Develop-Deliver labels, because the co-design cadence had to keep Brown's divergence and convergence modes separated across evidence intake, live making, overnight synthesis, and resident sign-off.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Pre-workshop: AI digests, humans frame (weeks 1–2)

Goal. Convert existing evidence — 380 survey responses and 22 community interviews the authority had collected but never analyzed — into workshop-ready materials, without letting the AI decide what matters.

Method & SOP. Say-level analysis of the survey and interviews, cross-checked against do-level ridership data, following the levels-of-knowledge ladder (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/03-generative-and-co-design-methods.md). The SOP: (1) pseudonymize all responses; (2) extract evidence items verbatim; (3) cluster into themes with active-sentence names; (4) draft persona cards and a tension map; (5) run a bias review asking who is absent; (6) human designer frames the workshop questions.

AI execution. The survey and interview corpus was processed by a Claude Code team against the evidence-extraction prompt in Section 7 — 412 evidence items, every quote verbatim-verifiable. A second agent drafted six tension statements (things residents simultaneously demand and distrust) and the quadrant tension map below. Codex then ran the bias review:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read synthesis/tension-map.md and
research/survey-demographics.csv. The survey skews to app users because
invites went to registered accounts. Identify which rider groups are
structurally under-represented in the evidence, which tension statements
would change if they were included, and what the workshop recruitment
must correct. Be specific; cite response counts."

Codex's finding reshaped recruitment: paper-survey respondents and interviewees included almost no one under 25 and almost no shift workers on rotating rosters — precisely the evening service's core riders. The youth sessions and the warehouse-estate pop-up were added to the series in response. OpenCode generated the workshop-evidence dataset (A7) schema so that every later session fed the same register.

Human designer judgment. The framing decision: the workshops would ask "what should evening mobility in Velderland be?" not "how do we fix the app?" The first question is the community's; the second is the authority's. Also: every persona card was marked DRAFT — residents corrected them in session 1.

Artifacts produced. Persona cards (A5, draft), tension map (below), workshop kit master (A6), recruitment quotas.

4.2 The workshop series: human-led, AI-scribed (weeks 3–10)

Goal. Put decision authority in residents' hands across 6 community workshops and 3 youth sessions, and let AI carry the documentation load so facilitators could facilitate.

Method & SOP. Each session was designed as a service in itself: onboarding pre-reads in the participant's language, a run sheet with minute-level scripting, alternating cognitive modes, and 24-hour synthesis (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/08-facilitating-workshops.md). Methods rotated through the generative repertoire: make-tool canvases ("build your ideal trip home at midnight"), business origami with paper bus and stop tokens on a tabletop map of the region, and one bodystorming session at a real unlit rural stop (See: 18-universal-methods-of-design/03-generative-and-co-design-methods.md). Every session opened with silent generation before any discussion — the standard HiPPO neutralizer, critical in rooms mixing council officers with residents — and every participant had "silent majority" cards: pre-paid postcards, in their language, that could be filled in privately and posted or handed to the safeguarding lead, guaranteeing a channel for anyone unwilling to speak in the room.

AI execution. Claude Code was the live scribe, never a voice in the room. During each session, a runner photographed flipcharts and sticky-note walls at every break; a Claude Code agent transcribed, translated, and clustered them so that thematic clusters were visible on a projector by the next exercise — participants watched their own words organize themselves, and corrected the clustering on the spot ("no, that note is about cost, not about trust"). OpenCode maintained the dot-vote pipeline: paper dot sheets were photographed, tallied against the A7 schema, and published to the session's GitHub issue within the hour, so the convergence rounds worked from real counts rather than facilitator memory:

opencode run --agent build "Read sessions/W4/dots-photos/. Transcribe each
dot sheet to rows matching data/workshop_evidence_sample.csv schema:
participant_hash from badge, session W4, per-theme dot counts, accessibility
flag from badge code. Flag any sheet with more than 10 dots for human check.
Output to sessions/W4/votes.csv and print theme totals."

Human designer judgment. Everything relational: the facilitation itself, the co-facilitator pairing (a Turkish-speaking community co-facilitator led W4), the decision to hold W5 outdoors at the actual stop in the cold because no flipchart conveys standing in the dark for 24 minutes, and the call to exclude council officers from the first hour of each session so residents could speak before the institution arrived.

Artifacts produced. Nine session walls (photographed and transcribed), 1,220 dot-votes across six themes, 30 attendances with at least one silent card, the co-designed future-state journey (Section 5), and the four-part service concept.

4.3 Synthesis: stratified, signed-off, adversarially reviewed (weeks 10–12)

Goal. Produce a synthesis report that residents recognize as their own — and that survives scrutiny about whose voice is in it.

Method & SOP. Rolling affinity synthesis with two hard rules learned the expensive way (Section 10): stratification — theme summaries must report cohort composition and never merge a minority cohort's position into a majority average — and quote sign-off — no attributed quote enters the report until the participant (or their co-facilitator, in their language) has approved the wording and translation.

AI execution. Claude Code produced the overnight synthesis after each session and the rolling theme register; at series end it assembled the synthesis report shell (A6) with every claim linked to session, cohort stratum, and evidence IDs. Codex ran the adversarial pass — instructed specifically to hunt for flattened minority positions:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read synthesis/report-draft.md and
data/workshop_evidence_sample.csv. For each of the 6 themes: (1) does the
summary preserve cohort-stratified positions or average them away,
(2) find any claim where a minority cohort position (students, shift
workers, accessibility-flagged) is contradicted or diluted by the summary,
(3) verify every attributed quote has a sign-off record. List violations
with line numbers."

It caught two genuine flattenings: the draft said "participants accept dynamic pricing," when shift workers had explicitly rejected it and only car-owning daytime riders in the survey had tolerated it; and it flagged that accessibility-flagged participants' stop-safety notes had been merged into a generic "lighting" theme, diluting a step-free boarding demand. Both were corrected before the validation assemblies.

Artifacts produced. Synthesis report (38 pages, 4 languages, plain-language summaries), theme register with cohort stratification, signed-off quote bank.

4.4 Validation, governance, and the 6-week plan (weeks 12–18)

Goal. Convert the co-designed concept into a blueprint, a governance promise, and an implementable plan — with residents as credited co-authors.

Method & SOP. Two validation assemblies walked the future-state journey and blueprint column by column with 41 returning participants, storymapping style: first-person spoken walkthroughs ("I finish my shift at 23:10, I open the door of De Linde...") to expose plot holes (See: 10-the-users-journey/06-workshop-and-application.md). Governance followed the power-sharing matrix: the community panel received defined authorities — sign-off on route changes, veto on pricing changes, a standing seat in pilot review (See: 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/03-co-design-and-participation.md).

AI execution. Claude Code authored the blueprint and journey mermaid as version-controlled files in a public GitHub repo, diffed in PRs that community co-facilitators reviewed with the designer (screenshotted and annotated in plain language). The 6-week implementation plan was decomposed into 87 GitHub issues across four workstreams (stops and lighting, booking channels, driver rostering and the friending network, pricing and communications), each linked to the session evidence that justified it. OpenCode built the pilot KPI dashboard scaffold seeded with mock data matching baseline rates.

Human designer judgment. Negotiating the co-production agreement with the authority's legal team — who initially wanted the panel "advisory only" — and holding the line that the pricing veto was non-negotiable, because tokenistic participation is worse than none (See: 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/03-co-design-and-participation.md).

Artifacts produced. Co-designed future-state journey map (below), pilot service blueprint (A2), co-production agreement (A6), 87-issue implementation backlog, pilot charter for night routes N1 (Kesselt–Noordhaven industrial estate) and N2 (Bergenveld–Westermolen–Oostend village loop).

5. Artifacts portfolio

All figures illustrative.

The co-designed service concept: "Flex Community Night"

Four resident-authored components, each traceable to session evidence:

  1. Community stops. Twenty-two hosted pickup points at shops, a library, a bakery, and a care home — lit, sheltered, with a named local host, real-time display or a simple "bus is coming" lamp, and phone/SMS booking on the spot.
  2. Buddy Ride. Opt-in matching so riders travelling the same corridor after dark are grouped at the same stop and, where wanted, walked the last stretch together; companions ride free.
  3. Driver friending network. Fixed evening drivers per route (not rotating agency staff), introduced by name and photo at the stop and in the app; riders can request "my driver" and drivers know their regulars.
  4. Ethical route pricing. A flat €2.00 evening fare (€1.00 students/concessions, companions free), no dynamic pricing — the shift-worker cohort's explicit red line — with the fare rule written into the co-production agreement.

6. Use cases

Use case A — The elderly night-shift carers' route (N1)

Scenario. Rosa (persona 1) finishes at De Linde at 23:10 with two colleagues. Before the pilot: a 24-minute walk to an unlit stop for a bus that might already have left, or a €14 taxi.

What was designed. On N1, the three carers book by one SMS from the care-home door (the home is a registered community stop host). The confirmation names driver Karel and lamp code 3. The minibus — routed directly at shift-change, co-timetabled with De Linde and the Noordhaven warehouses — collects all three as a Buddy Ride group; Karel waits at Bergenveld until each door light is on; arrival texts go to Rosa's daughter automatically. Fare: €2, charged to a weekly invoice the care home offered to handle.

AI working session. Claude Code drafted the SMS conversation flow in plain Dutch, English, Turkish, and Arabic; a Codex review flagged that the v1 SMS asked for a location pin — impossible for Rosa's usage — and it was replaced with named-stop codes from the poster. OpenCode seeded the booking-engine stub with the A7 cohort mix so hallway tests used realistic names, languages, and accessibility flags.

Outcome (illustrative). N1 weekly rides 173→289 in pilot month 3; 41% of N1 bookings by SMS or host; zero safety complaints on the route; Rosa's taxi spend €120→€18/month.

Use case B — The "never used the app" youth session (Y2)

Scenario. Recruitment for youth sessions deliberately targeted non-users. At Kesselt Youth Hub, 11 of 12 participants had never opened the Flex app; the opening exercise — "show me how you'd get home from the warehouse tonight" — produced cycling routes and colleague lifts, not the service.

What was designed. The Y2 group designed the student layer: €1 fare, SMS group booking ("one person books for four"), and the N1 timing aligned to the 22:00 warehouse shift change. They rejected — unanimously and unprompted — a gamified loyalty scheme the authority had sketched, calling it "a trick to make us feel watched." Three participants became paid youth mobility champions, testing each prototype iteration.

AI working session. The make-tool canvases (built from foam tokens and map printouts) were photographed and transcribed by Claude Code between exercises; when the live clustering merged "fare price" and "feeling watched" into one "cost" cluster, the room corrected it on the projector — surveillance was a values objection, not a price objection. That correction is preserved verbatim in the synthesis report's "what the AI got wrong" section, and it directly produced the ethical-pricing component and the no-tracking clause in the co-production agreement.

Outcome (illustrative). Student ridership on pilot corridors 28→96 weekly rides; group bookings 22% of youth rides; the loyalty scheme was formally withdrawn by the authority.

Use case C — Rural stop accessibility (W5, Westermolen)

Scenario. W5 was held at the actual Westermolen stop at 20:30 in February: unlit, no shelter, no kerb, a gravel verge. Participants included two wheelchair users and a rollator user; the bodystorming — boarding a parked minibus with a portable ramp — made the failure physical: the gravel verge made ramp angles unsafe.

What was designed. The De Hoek bakery, 180 metres away, became a hosted community stop: step-free pavement, the bakery's lit forecourt as shelter, the owner as paid host at peak windows, and a "bus is coming" lamp visible from indoors. The move cost €4,300 in minor civils versus €38,000 quoted for rebuilding the original stop.

AI working session. Claude Code generated the stop-comparison matrix (usage radius, households within 400 m, civils cost, host availability) overnight from the session notes; Codex reviewed the accessibility claims against national stop-design guidance and caught that the lamp's proposed mounting height was unreadable from a seated position — corrected before the civils order. The accessibility quadrant (A8) was re-scored by the accessibility-flagged participants themselves at the validation assembly.

Outcome (illustrative). Households within 400 m of a step-free evening pickup in the pilot corridors rose 41%→68%; all 8 accessibility-flagged N2 regulars reported boarding without assistance by pilot week 6.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Four SOPs were encoded for agent execution, each following method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Co-design process SOP (participant-first). The meta-rule: AI is scribe, translator, and synthesizer — never decider, never a voice in the room. Steps: (1) humans frame the question; (2) AI prepares materials; (3) humans facilitate; (4) AI transcribes/clusters/tallies; (5) the room corrects the AI; (6) AI drafts; (7) participants sign off; (8) Codex reviews stratification; (9) humans decide. Acceptance: every published claim traceable to session + cohort stratum + signed-off quote. Gate: validation assembly.

  2. Evidence digest SOP (pre-workshop). The extraction prompt:

You are EVIDENCE-EXTRACTOR. Input: one pseudonymized survey batch or
interview transcript. Output JSONL evidence items:
- evidence_id, source (survey|interview), participant_code, cohort
  (elderly|shift_worker|student|parent|other), language
- type: verbatim_quote | stated_preference | reported_behavior | reported_workaround
- text: copy-paste exact (quotes never paraphrased; keep original language
  plus translation marked [translation])
- candidate_theme, tension_candidate (true|false)
RULES: a quote not found verbatim in the source fails. Never merge or
average positions across cohorts. Flag low-sample cohorts instead of
silencing them. Include source_ref for every item.
  1. Live-synthesis SOP (in-session). Triggered at each break by the runner's photos:
You are LIVE-CLUSTERER for session [id]. Input: transcribed sticky notes
and flipchart text (participant hashes only). Output:
1. Clusters with active-sentence names, each note id mapped
2. A "minority positions" list: any note whose position is held by fewer
   than 20 percent of contributors — NEVER merge these into majority
   clusters; surface them side by side
3. Ambiguities for the facilitator to resolve live (max 5, phrased as
   questions the room can answer in one sentence)
4. A plain-language cluster summary at CEFR B1 for projection
FORBIDDEN: inventing notes, smoothing disagreement, naming participants.
  1. Ethics & safeguarding SOP. Enforced as a pre-session and pre-publication checklist:
sop: MM-ETH-001
before_every_session:
  - consent_forms_in_session_languages: true
  - safeguarding_lead_named_and_present: true
  - silent_cards_stocked_4_languages: true
  - interpreter_confirmed_or_session_postponed: true
  - honorarium_cash_on_site: true
before_any_publication:
  - every_quote_signed_off_in_participant_language: true
  - cohort_stratification_preserved: true
  - minority_positions_listed_not_merged: true
  - no_faces_without_separate_consent: true
  - participant_data_never_in_external_training: true
review_gate: community panel co-chair countersigns the report

Guardrails. No real names, faces, or raw recordings in any prompt, ever — agents work on badge hashes, photographed walls, and pseudonymized transcripts. Verbatim quote verification runs in CI. Translation uses back-translation spot checks by the multilingual co-facilitators. Public repo contains anonymized extracts only.

Failure modes observed and mitigations.

  • AI flattening minority voices. The highest-stakes failure mode. The W3 overnight draft merged shift workers' rejection of dynamic pricing into "mixed views on pricing." Mitigation: stratified synthesis (SOP 4), the minority-positions rule (SOP 3), Codex adversarial review specifically hunting dilution, and the co-production agreement's verbatim dissent logging.
  • Translation drift. "Buddy ride" initially translated into Turkish with a romantic connotation; caught by co-facilitator back-translation, fixed before printing. Mitigation: community translators, never unreviewed machine translation, for anything printed.
  • False consensus pressure. Dot-votes can manufacture unanimity. Mitigation: silent cards, cohort-stratified vote reporting, and the report's "tensions preserved" section.
  • Participation washing. The risk that 104 residents decorate a decision already taken. Mitigation: the pricing veto, the panel's sign-off authority, and publishing the decision log.

Causal logic. Participation breadth produced service fit: non-app channels and hosted stops removed the access costs that suppressed latent demand; fit produced ridership; ridership density produced unit economics (cost per ride −26%) that made the service defensible at council. The chain runs internal legitimacy → external service value → usage → fiscal sustainability, the public-sector analogue of the Service Profit Chain (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md). Estimated annualized effect at network scale (illustrative): recovered evening ridership worth ≈ €190k in fare revenue and avoided taxi spend retained locally, against ≈ €86k incremental operating cost for hosts and extended phone hours.

Measurement plan (abridged). North-star: weekly completed evening rides by corridor. Drivers: booking-channel mix, buddy-match rate, lamp/display uptime, driver continuity (% rides with the route's named driver). Experience: safety and fairness pulse surveys at stops monthly, in 4 languages. Governance: panel review monthly; dissent log published. Lagging: cost per ride, complaints, council KPI pack quarterly.

9. AI tool playbook

Claude Code — digestion, kits, live scribe, artifact authoring. Ran the 402-item evidence digest, drafted persona cards and workshop kits in four languages, acted as the live clustering scribe at all nine sessions, authored every mermaid artifact as version-controlled code, and assembled the synthesis report shell with full traceability. Excelled at: overnight compression of workshop output into next-morning materials; consistent stratified schemas across nine sessions. Failed at: judging when a translation "felt wrong" (the Turkish buddy-ride incident) and when a cluster merger was politically significant (the fare/surveillance merge). Human took over: facilitation, framing, ethics, and every room correction.

OpenCode — data pipelines and vote tally. Maintained the dot-vote pipeline (photo → schema-valid CSV → live totals), generated the seeded A7 dataset, and scaffolded the pilot KPI dashboard. Excelled at: fast, self-directed data plumbing against a fixed schema. Failed at: anything requiring judgment about what a vote meant — it would happily report totals with a cohort silently under-counted; the schema's mandatory cohort field was the leash.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • AI makes participation cheaper, not optional. The agents compressed roughly 15 hours of overnight synthesis per session to about 40 minutes; that time went back into recruitment, translation, and sitting in cold village halls. The savings fund participation — they never replace it (See: 07-designing-for-service-key-issues/03-co-design-and-participation.md).
  • Live synthesis changes the power dynamic in the room — in both directions. Watching their words cluster in real time energized participants and let them correct the machine publicly; but the first session, run without the "room corrects the AI" step, let a bad clustering steer 20 minutes of discussion. The correction step is load-bearing (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/08-facilitating-workshops.md).
  • The minority-positions rule is the single most important prompt line in this engagement. Every flattening incident traced to an agent doing what agents do: finding the central tendency. Stratification, minority surfacing, and verbatim dissent are the counterweights.
  • Silent cards surfaced what workshops could not. Thirty attendances produced silent cards, including the domestic-safety disclosure that triggered the arrival-text feature and a wage-theft concern (correctly referred, out of scope). Design the private channel as carefully as the public one.
  • Paying participants is a design decision, not a budget line. The honorarium policy produced the cohort mix — 30 elderly, 27 shift workers, 33 students — that made the design right. Unpaid co-design would have drawn retirees with daytime flexibility and produced a different, worse service.
  • Codex caught the survey's blind spot before the first workshop. The bias review turned "we have 380 responses" into "we have 380 app-skewed responses." Adversarial review of who is missing is now standard in every research plan.
  • The youth killed our best idea. The loyalty scheme had internal champions and a budget line; 11 young people called it surveillance and they were right. Co-design that cannot kill the sponsor's favourite idea is theatre.
  • Done differently next time: involve the drivers' union from W1 (they arrived at W5 with legitimate rostering concerns that cost two weeks), and budget for community co-researchers, not just co-facilitators — the peer-interview pilot in week 9 outperformed the professional interviews for candor.
