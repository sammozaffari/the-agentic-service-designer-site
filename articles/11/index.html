Service Blueprints as Operational Diagnostics: Fernwood Council's Housing Repairs Service

00 · Provenance note

Confidentiality & provenance note

Fernwood Council is a fictional composite of several UK-style unitary local authorities; every name, figure, dataset, and quote in this article is illustrative and anonymized, and monetary values are expressed in euro for series consistency. Nothing here is real client data. What is real is the process: an AI-first service design engagement in which Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were the primary working tools, with a human designer owning framing, ethics, political sensitivity, and resident relationships. The intent is to share method and workflow, not client specifics.

01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

Fernwood Council, a fictional local authority of 310,000 residents responsible for housing, planning, and streetcare, was spending more energy administering its reactive housing repairs service than fixing homes. The service handled 28,000 repair requests a year across roughly 19,000 council homes, yet 41% of open jobs attracted at least one status-chase contact — about 3,100 failure-demand calls a month — because no system, letter, or person could tell a resident what was happening. Over a 10-week blueprint program we treated the service blueprint not as a documentation exercise but as a diagnostic instrument: we validated a resident journey spine, built the five-lane current-state blueprint strictly from evidence, annotated every fail point (F) and wait point (W) with frequency, time, and cost overlays, and traced each fail point downward through the backstage and support lines to its system root cause. The future-state blueprint — one job record, status events emitted on every state change, a contractor portal, a single case file per property — was then piloted for 8 weeks with two repairs teams. Status-chase contacts fell 31% in the pilot cohort, first-time-fix rose from 62% to 71%, and mean routine repair wait fell from 23.5 to 18.7 days. All figures are illustrative.

What you will learn:

  • How to build a current-state service blueprint as a diagnostic instrument — evidence-gated lanes, F/W annotations, time and cost overlays — using AI agents without letting a single unevidenced node through.
  • How to trace fail points downward from the resident's symptom, through backstage operations, to system root causes (no shared job ID, contractor incentives, batch sync), and prioritize by F/W density × cost.
  • How to run blueprint-as-code: markdown lanes and mermaid diagrams in GitHub, with PR gates, a lane lint, and an evidence-per-node CI check.
02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait. Fernwood Council is a fictional unitary authority in a mid-sized European city-region: 310,000 residents, three service directorates (housing, planning, streetcare), and a housing stock of roughly 19,000 council-owned homes. The repairs service logs about 28,000 reactive repair requests a year — 1.5 per home — which create 30,200 jobs once follow-on works are included. Delivery is mixed-economy: 24 in-house operatives in four area teams handle about 40% of jobs; three framework contractors take the rest. Digital maturity is low. Intake is 62% phone, 24% web form, 9% email, 5% in person at the One-Stop Shop. Three job-management systems coexist: RepairsDB (a 2009 in-house system), ContractorLink (the contractor-side portal), and AssetPlan (asset and stock data). Contact-centre agents answering a status call alt-tab across these three plus the telephony CRM — four windows per call — a pattern documented in the earlier operating-system engagement at Fernwood (See: ../01/README.md).

The challenge. The council's own numbers framed the failure. Of the 30,200 jobs opened each year, 41% — 12,400 jobs — attracted at least one status-chase contact, at an average of 3.0 chases per chased job: about 37,200 status-chase contacts a year, roughly 3,100 a month, at a fully loaded €6.40 per contact. That is €238k a year spent telling residents the service had not forgotten them — pure failure demand in Seddon's sense: contact caused by the organization failing to deliver right the first time (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md). First-time-fix (FTF) sat at 62%[^ftf]; 19% of first visits ended in no-access or a no-show; stage-1 repairs complaints ran at 92 per quarter. A previous "repairs transformation" had bought a chatbot business case. The earlier one-district pilot at Fernwood had already shown a 31% drop in status-chasing from manual SMS milestones alone (See: ../01/README.md); the sponsor's question for this engagement was why the failure demand existed and what the permanent service should be.

[^ftf]: Fernwood defines first-time-fix strictly as completion in a single continuous visit; same-day returns for parts count as a second visit, which suppresses the baseline against peer councils that use looser definitions.

Success criteria, agreed with the Director of Housing:

  1. A current-state blueprint in which every node carries at least one evidence reference — accepted line by line by the practitioner panel.
  2. A costed fail-point register: each F and W with frequency, time, and cost overlays, adversarially checked.
  3. Pilot on two repairs teams: status-chase contacts per job −25% or better; first-time-fix +5 points or better; no regression on emergency attendance.
  4. A future-state blueprint signed off as the operational specification for the permanent service, translatable to a delivery backlog.

Constraints. Program budget €235k; run-rate cap €95k/yr. RepairsDB cannot be replaced within the horizon — integration must wrap it. Contractor framework agreements fixed for 18 more months, so incentive changes are limited to what the existing schedule of rates allows. GDPR and council data-protection rules: no resident-identifiable data in design artifacts. Operative and scheduler working practices subject to union consultation. The pilot had to run inside normal operations — no protected sandbox.

Why service design. The council's instinct was another channel: a chatbot, a bigger contact centre. But the calls were a symptom of a service that emitted no status, not of a channel gap. When the question is "how does the organization produce this experience, and where does it break," the service blueprint is the right instrument and the journey map alone is not (See: 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md). And because the process is the product in a service, redesigning the process — not adding a buffer in front of it — is the only durable fix (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/07-designing-and-managing-the-service-process.md).

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

Phase 1 — Discover: the evidence base and the journey spine (weeks 1–3)

Goal. Establish what actually happens to 28,000 repair requests a year, from the resident's side and from the organization's side, before drawing a single lane.

Method & SOP. Three evidence streams ran in parallel. (1) Chronological journey-centric interviewing with 18 residents who had a repair in the last 90 days — anchor in a concrete episode, reconstruct the timeline, probe emotion and channel choice (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/04-understanding-the-journey.md). (2) Forty-five frontline and backstage interviews (contact-centre agents, schedulers, supervisors, contractor managers) plus 12 ride-alongs with operatives — the service-safari/shadowing method from the same chapter. (3) Process mining: reconstruct one event sequence per job from 12 months of logs across the three systems (See: 19-service-management-and-marketing/07-designing-and-managing-the-service-process.md).

AI execution. OpenCode ran the log pipeline in agent mode; the join logic was the finding:

TERMINAL
opencode run "Read exports/repairsdb_jobs.csv exports/contractorlink_jobs.csv \
  exports/assetplan_visits.csv (12 months). Reconstruct one event sequence per \
  job: created, triaged, scheduled, visit_1, parts, visit_2, completed, invoiced. \
  Join across systems on property_token + 10-day window + trade code. Emit \
  clean/job_events.parquet and qa/unmatched_jobs.csv. Never emit raw addresses \
  or names; property_token only. Report the join rate."

The join rate was 91%. The 9% unmatched — jobs that existed in one system with no counterpart in another — became evidence LOG-01, the first quantitative proof of the no-shared-job-ID root cause. Claude Code coded the 63 interview transcripts into an evidence register (evidence/register.jsonl) with one rule: every row carries {evidence_id, source, lane, step, quote_or_fact, confidence}; quotes capped at 25 words; resident names and addresses tokenized to property IDs before any agent saw a transcript. The journey spine — report, wait, visit, complete, invoice-and-close — was validated in a residents' panel session before any blueprinting began; a blueprint built on an unvalidated spine is folklore with lanes (See: 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md).

Human designer judgment. The designer ran the interviews and ride-alongs (agents cannot sit in a resident's kitchen), chose the spine granularity, and excluded two ride-along notes that named a resident's medical condition — irrelevant to the service and a privacy risk in any artifact.

Artifact. Evidence register (388 rows), validated journey spine v3, and the quantitative baseline: 30,200 jobs/yr; 41% chased; mean routine wait 23.5 days; first-time-fix 62%.

Phase 2 — Define: the current-state blueprint as a diagnostic (weeks 3–6)

Goal. A five-lane current-state blueprint in which every node is evidence-gated, annotated with fail points, wait points, time, and cost.

Method & SOP. Blueprinting from the validated journey spine: place the customer-action lane as the spine; for each column, derive physical evidence above and frontstage, backstage, and support work below; draw the three lines — interaction, visibility, internal interaction; then annotate F, W, time standards (S), and cost overlays (See: 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md, 19-service-management-and-marketing/07-designing-and-managing-the-service-process.md). The cross-functional blueprinting workshop (schedulers, agents, supervisors, ICT, contractor manager) walked the printed blueprint line by line — the moment where documented SOPs and frontline reality diverged, exactly as the method predicts.

AI execution. Claude Code ran four lane-builder subagents against the blueprint-build SOP (Section 7, the flagship encoding), one per lane, then a linker agent marked every line-of-visibility crossing:

NOTE
claude "Spawn four lane-builder subagents under SOP-BP-001 (lanes:
physical_evidence, frontstage, backstage, support) against spine v3 and
evidence/register.jsonl. Then a linker agent marks every crossing of the
line of visibility and line of internal interaction. Critic agent verifies:
no OK-status node without >=1 evidence_id; every W tag carries a duration;
every F tag carries a frequency. Open the result as a PR to
blueprint/current-state/. Do not merge."

Codex then adversarially reviewed fail-point attribution and cost math (Section 7 for the full prompt). It caught two real defects: CR-2, the invoice-cycle cost double-counted contractor admin (once in cost-per-job, once in dispute handling); and CR-5, our first draft attributed the no-access fail point to "resident not in" — the log data showed 62% of no-access visits had a contractor-initiated reschedule within the prior 48 hours, moving the root cause from the customer lane to the support lane where it belonged.

Human designer judgment. Facilitating the workshop, adjudicating lane disputes (is the contractor re-booking call frontstage or backstage? Ruling: backstage — the resident cannot perceive it until the letter arrives), and keeping the session diagnostic rather than blameful: the panel's rule was "we name systems, not people."

Artifact. The current-state blueprint (Section 5, star artifact) with 8 fail points, 6 wait points, time and cost overlays, and the fail-point root-cause trace diagram.

Phase 3 — Develop: prioritization and the future-state blueprint (weeks 6–10)

Goal. Decide what to fix, and specify the future service as an operational contract.

Method & SOP. Prioritization by F/W density × annualized cost (the fail-point and bottleneck diagnostic from 09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md, with the tripartite productivity lens — internal, external, capacity efficiency — from 19-service-management-and-marketing/07-designing-and-managing-the-service-process.md). Then future-state blueprinting as the delivery bridge artifact (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/06-designing-for-scale-and-delivery.md), translated column-by-column into a delivery backlog (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md).

AI execution. The future blueprint was authored as code: markdown lane files plus mermaid diagrams in GitHub, with CI running a mermaid parse, a PII regex gate, and the evidence-per-node check on every PR. OpenCode generated the 61-ticket backlog from the blueprint diff (every removed F/W and every new node became a ticket linked to its blueprint cell); Claude Code drafted the operational-readiness scorecard (staffing, training, IT latency, data governance, contractor SLAs, rollback) per the readiness-review method (See: 06-the-service-innovation-handbook/06-designing-for-scale-and-delivery.md).

Human designer judgment. The designer cut one agent-favorite intervention — auto-approval of all routine scheduling — because contractor calendars were only 71% populated; scheduling against missing data would have automated the no-access problem. Human override, logged on the PR.

Artifact. Future-state blueprint (Section 5), prioritization quadrant, 61-ticket backlog, readiness scorecard: 6 Green, 2 Amber (contractor calendar coverage; SMS gateway failover) — both remediated before gate G3.

Phase 4 — Deliver: pilot with two repairs teams (weeks 11–20)

Goal. Prove the blueprint changes in live operations with a comparison group.

Method & SOP. Bounded pilot per the piloting method: representative sites, strict timebox, rollback triggers, weekly measurement (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md); performance tracked on the four public-service KPIs — completion, satisfaction, cost per transaction, digital take-up (See: 05-good-services/06-designing-good-services.md). Pilot: teams North and South (12 operatives, 2 schedulers, 1,160 jobs over 8 weeks), running the future-state slice: single job record, structured triage, status events at six state changes, contractor portal same-day confirmation. Comparison: teams Central and East on the legacy journey (1,140 jobs).

AI execution. GitHub Actions regenerated the weekly KPI dashboard from job-record extracts; an OpenCode agent normalized each extract and flagged schema drift (ContractorLink added a column in week 5 — caught in CI). Codex reviewed the evaluation language before the gate: "contribution, not attribution." PR review with named approvers was the sign-off gate, as on every artifact.

Human designer judgment. Weekly stand-ups with the two teams, the week-3 decision to keep the phone channel fully staffed despite falling call volume (vulnerable residents first; capacity was redeployed, not cut), and the go/no-go recommendation at G4.

Artifact. Pilot scorecard (Section 8): every gate metric passed.

06 · Use cases

Use cases

UC1 — Emergency repair: dangerous fault, high-severity escalation

Scenario. A void property's smashed ground-floor window is reported at 19:40 by a neighbour; separately, an occupied home reports water pouring through a light fitting. Baseline: emergency mean attendance 5h10m, 71% within the 4-hour target, and the escalation path lived in the duty supervisor's head. What was designed: an escalation slice of the blueprint — coded triage rules auto-flag emergency combinations (water + electrics; unsecured void), the event bus pages the duty supervisor with the job record attached, the operative's tablet carries the structured diagnosis, and the resident (or reporting neighbour) gets the tracker link with a 4-hour expected time.

AI working session. Claude Code derived the 14 emergency trigger combinations from 12 months of emergency logs and scheduler interviews; Codex attacked the list for false-positive cost (every false emergency page costs a call-out); the panel tuned thresholds on two historical winters. Outcome (illustrative, pilot): emergency mean attendance 5h10m → 2h50m; 94% within 4 hours; zero missed-escalation incidents vs 3 in the comparison teams.

UC2 — Planned vs reactive coordination: the gas safety collision

Scenario. Fernwood runs an annual gas safety check programme across all 19,000 homes — a planned service with its own appointments — while reactive boiler repairs run on a separate track. The blueprint's support lane exposed the collision: 640 times a year, a reactive boiler visit and a planned gas check landed at the same property within 21 days of each other — two visits, two time-off-work windows, and, in 11% of those cases, contradictory advice from two different engineers (evidence LOG-11, INT-38). What was designed: one case file per property-issue and a scheduling join: any reactive heating job checks the planned-works calendar before offering a slot; when both are due within 21 days, one combined visit is offered.

AI working session. OpenCode mined the overlap from the two calendars (the join query was itself the diagnosis — the systems had never been joined before); Claude Code drafted the combined-visit message copy, reviewed by the gas compliance manager for regulatory phrasing; the designer negotiated the scope boundary (compliance-critical checks keep their legal deadlines; the combine is an offer, never a delay). Outcome (illustrative, pilot cohort): 71% of overlapping visits combined; ~450 duplicate visits/yr avoided at scale; zero compliance deadline breaches.

UC3 — Contractor first-visit reschedule cascade

Scenario. The worst sequence in the current-state blueprint: contractor reschedules → the appointment letter is already posted → the resident waits in → no-access is recorded against the resident → the job re-enters the booking queue at the back → the resident chases, and the call lands on an agent who can see none of this. 62% of no-access visits had a contractor-initiated reschedule in the prior 48 hours (Codex review CR-5). What was designed: a portal reschedule becomes a status event — it cancels the old slot in real time, fires an SMS with the new confirmed slot, removes the no-access risk, and logs against the contractor's schedule-of-rates record, which is where the incentive conversation now lives.

AI working session. Claude Code traced 214 historical cascades from the event sequences into a failure-tree; OpenCode built the reschedule-event simulator on data/repairs_jobs_sample.csv so the panel could watch the cascade die; the designer handled the contractor-commercial conversation (portal compliance became a framework-review criterion, within the existing agreement's data clause). Outcome (illustrative, pilot): first-visit no-access 19% → 12%; cascade-linked complaints −58%; contractor same-day confirmation rate 88%.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs on this engagement:

  1. Blueprint-build SOP (SOP-BP-001) — the flagship. Encoding pattern: method → step list → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate. This is how a complex, workshop-grade method becomes agent-executable without losing its evidentiary spine:
NOTE
SOP-BP-001: BUILD BLUEPRINT LANE FROM EVIDENCE
Role: Lane-builder agent for the Fernwood repairs blueprint.
Inputs: evidence/register.jsonl; spine/journey_spine.md (validated); lane name.
Steps:
1. Read the validated spine. Each customer action is a column anchor.
2. For your lane (physical_evidence | frontstage | backstage | support),
   propose one node per column where the lane acts or produces evidence.
3. Every node MUST cite >= 1 evidence_id. No evidence -> status: GAP,
   and no F/W tag may be attached.
4. F tags require a quantified frequency; W tags require a duration;
   qualitative-only signals get status: NEEDS_QUANT.
5. Mark every vertical dependency crossing the line of visibility or
   the line of internal interaction.
Output schema (JSONL, one row per node):
{node_id, lane, column, label, f_tag, w_tag, t_std, cost_eur,
 evidence_ids[], status: OK | GAP | NEEDS_QUANT}
Acceptance: 100% of OK nodes carry >= 1 evidence_id; mermaid lint passes;
no resident-identifying strings (regex gate); lane minimums per the
lane-data dictionary are met before the blueprint leaves draft.
Review gate: practitioner panel walks the printed blueprint line by line;
disputed nodes return to the backlog with their evidence attached.
  1. Fail-point attribution prompt — the diagnostic conscience of the register:
NOTE
You are the Attribution Checker. Input: one fail-point register row
{f_id, symptom, suspected_causes[], evidence_ids[]}. Trace the fail point
downward: frontstage -> backstage -> support -> system root. Every hop
needs an evidence_id. If the trace ends above the system layer, mark
root_cause: UNKNOWN and name the observation that would resolve it.
Never attribute to "staff error" without a process-level explanation.
Output: trace chain, confidence, and any contradicting evidence found.
  1. Event-sequence mining SOP (OpenCode pipeline, Section 4.1) — join logic, tokenization, and the unmatched-records report as a first-class finding.
  2. The meta-SOP — research → synthesize → map → prioritize → specify → pilot → measure, with G1–G4 as PR-merge gates with named human approvers.

Guardrails. No resident PII in any blueprint artifact: names, addresses, and tenancy numbers are tokenized to property IDs before any agent sees source material, and a CI regex gate re-scans every PR — redaction is verified, not trusted. Evidence minimums per lane (A6d) stop a lane shipping on vibes. Every numeric overlay recomputes from cost/volumes.md in CI. Human sign-off: G1 evidence (ops lead), G2 current-state (practitioner panel), G3 pilot go (DPO + union consultation), G4 rollout (Director of Housing). No agent output crosses a gate unmerged.

Failure modes observed. (1) The invented lane step. A lane-builder agent emitted a plausible backstage node — "job sheets printed nightly for supervisors" — with no evidence. It was exactly the kind of operational folklore a workshop would nod through. The evidence-per-node CI check failed the build; the node went in as GAP, and a ride-along confirmed the practice had died two years earlier. Mitigation: every node needs ≥1 evidence ref, full stop. (2) Lane confusion. The linker agent placed the contractor re-booking call in frontstage; the workshop ruling ("if the resident cannot perceive it, it is not frontstage") is now a SOP rule. (3) Attribution drift toward people. Early drafts blamed operative skill for F-04 and residents for F-03; the attribution prompt's no-"staff error" rule plus Codex's CR-5 review kept both traces at the system layer, where the fixes actually live. (4) Mermaid drift. Duplicate node ids across lanes failed the parse lint twice; the diagram conventions are now in the PR template.

10 · Lessons learned

Lessons learned & pitfalls

  1. A blueprint without evidence gates is a mural. The one node that slipped through unevidenced was the most believable one. Believability is the enemy; the CI gate, not vigilance, is the control.
  2. The join rate is a finding. OpenCode's 9% unmatched jobs said more about the system than any interview. In blueprint diagnostics, pipeline failures are data.
  3. Blame migrates upward when you force the trace. Every F that started as "resident fault" or "operative skill" ended at a system root once the attribution prompt forbade person-level explanations. The fix list changed completely.
  4. Codex earns its keep on attribution and math, not copy. CR-5 and CR-2 were worth the entire review budget; its wording suggestions were mostly noise. Route tools to their strengths.
  5. Contractor incentives are a blueprint layer. F-03's root cause lived in the payment schedule, not in any system. If the support lane stops at IT, you miss the commercial machinery that drives behavior.
  6. The workshop is where agents get corrected. Lane confusion, folklore nodes, and severity mis-ranking were all fixed by the practitioner panel in three hours. Budget the workshop; do not let the artifact outrun it.
  7. Keep the phone channel fully alive in pilot. Digital take-up rose when the phone channel was not threatened — residents with low digital confidence (persona Petra) move when trust exists, not when the door closes.
  8. Diagram lint from day one. Two mermaid failures were caught in PRs that would have been steering-deck embarrassments.
