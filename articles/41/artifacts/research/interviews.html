Method, consent and capture protocol N221

Eight semi-structured interviews across the first three weeks of the six-week field study (4–22 May 2026), covering every role named in the engagement brief: store leadership, crew, franchise partner, courier, customer, contact centre and distribution centre. Six interviews anchor on the two flagship grill-house sites (Harbourgate Flagship #14 and Northline Flagship #3); two extend beyond the store to the delivery and support perimeter so the blueprint sees the whole operation. The ASD Researcher conducted all eight; the ASD Orchestrator observed interviews 01 and 08. Guides follow template kind 04 (segments, questions, probes, capture protocol, consent). Consent (simulated): every participant received a verbal and written briefing on the fictional-composite framing, agreed to a synthetic recording, and was told extracts would be anonymised into the evidence ledger with a right to withdraw — not exercised by any participant. Transcripts below are lightly edited for length; every number is spoken by the participant as recorded. Interview 01 — Aisha Rahman · General Manager, Harbourgate Flagship #14

Store leadership · roster, peaks, compliance, retention

Backstage voice
Date Tue 5 May 2026, 07:40–08:55 (75 min)
Location Back office, Harbourgate Flagship #14
Interviewer ASD Researcher (ASD Orchestrator observing)
Consent (synthetic) Written + verbal; synthetic recording on file; anonymised; right to withdraw not exercised
Format context Flagship grill-house · 1,850 orders/day · 18 crew at peak
Evidence 6 notes (222–227)
Guide topics
  1. A week in the roster: draft, publish, survive
  2. Peak orchestration, 11:30–13:30 and 17:30–19:30
  3. Q-Pulse compliance and what "done" costs
  4. The aggregator counter
  5. Keeping crew past month twelve

R: Walk me through last week's roster, from first draft to published.

AR: Sofia's team sends the labour model Wednesday. I build in ShiftOrbit Wednesday night, fight with it Thursday, publish Friday about six. For a Monday start that's — I've done the maths — sixty-two hours before. I know. I know it's under the seventy-two.

R: And once it's published?

AR: It never survives. Someone calls in sick Friday night, I rebuild half of it by phone. The company number is eleven swap requests a store a week — mine runs thirteen, fourteen. Bigger crew, more moving parts.

R: You said "fight with it" — concretely, what's the rework?

AR: Skills. A third of swaps come back to me — thirty-one percent company-wide, it feels worse here — because the person proposing the swap doesn't know the shift needs a grill certification. ShiftOrbit doesn't hold skills against shifts. The skills matrix is a spreadsheet Bo keeps on his phone. I'm approving labour with folklore.

R: Friday, twelve noon, this floor — where are you standing?

AR: At the pass, calling the board. Eighteen on the line at peak. I do about 1,850 orders a day here and roughly 1,150 of them land inside four hours — the two peak windows carry sixty-two percent. CopperPOS queues the lane screens, I queue the humans.

R: Where does the time go wrong?

AR: Aggregator orders jump everything. Three tablets, three sounds. If we miss the accept window on Wheelio the store gets flagged — so somebody's watching a tablet instead of a grill. Delivery is barely a quarter of our mix but at dinner it feels like half the board.

R: Tell me about Q-Pulse at close.

AR: Thirty-four line items. Company-wide ninety-six point two percent of checks get done. Done, and done at the right time — those are different sentences and everyone in this company knows it. Mine? My close team logs the day's temperatures at twenty to midnight, in one pass. I know exactly what that means. I've decided to live with it, which is a sentence I shouldn't be saying to a researcher.

R: What would honest logging actually cost?

AR: Maybe forty minutes spread across a day. With the crew I can keep? I lose one in three inside a year — ninety-four percent turnover annualised, that's the number. Training someone to log honestly takes longer than the check, and they're gone before it pays back.

R: Waste runs at 3.8 percent of chicken weight against a 2.5 target. Where does yours go?

AR: End of every LTO. Firebasket, the last promo — final two days, StockBeak kept proposing to the old curve, the chiller's full of promo thighs and the menu's moved on. The system doesn't know the promo died.

R: The courier counter — how is it working?

AR: There's no standard. Jack, one of the Dashly regulars, waits six, eight minutes some nights. My crew are kind — kindness doesn't scale. Zero point seven percent of delivery orders break somewhere in that handover, and every one of those is a family's Friday night.

R: What do you read out of VoiceBoard?

AR: Monday morning, forty to sixty new items for my store. Network-wide the top code is order accuracy, thirty-one percent of everything. For me it's dips. I reorder dips by vibes at this point.

R: You've kept some crew for years. What works?

AR: Predictability beats perks. Two students stayed an extra year because I fixed their Tuesdays. Bo's on four years. People don't leave chicken; they leave not knowing next Tuesday.

R: One change tomorrow?

AR: Publish my roster eight days out, and make swaps self-serve with the skills matrix built in. I'd take an hour a day back.

R: Anything I should have asked?

AR: Ask the crew about the signing thing. Not me. They're the ones choosing between honest and employed.

Interview 02 — Bo Chen · Shift Leader, Harbourgate Flagship #14

Shift leadership · close shift, swaps, peaks, handover

Backstage voice
Date Wed 6 May 2026, 14:10–15:00 (50 min)
Location Crew room, Harbourgate Flagship #14 (between peaks)
Interviewer ASD Researcher
Consent (synthetic) Written + verbal; synthetic recording; anonymised; right to withdraw not exercised
Format history 2 years drive-thru express before 2 years flagship
Evidence 6 notes (228–233)
Guide topics
  1. Anatomy of a close shift
  2. Approving swaps from a phone, mid-service
  3. The two peaks, lunch vs dinner
  4. Courier handover at the counter
  5. Memory of drive-thru: what 180s takes

R: Take me through a close, minute by minute.

BC: Kitchen down 22:40. Then temps and the Q-Pulse checklist — thirty-four items. Honestly? Fridge, line, close audit, all in one sitting, 23:35, 23:40. Nobody's doing grill temps at 19:00 during a dinner rush.

R: What's inside the close audit?

BC: Waste log into StockBeak, hold-bin discard — anything past twenty minutes in the hold gets binned and written — then charcoal ash-out and clean. That part I refuse to fake; ash is ash.

R: You approve swaps too?

BC: When Aisha's off, yes. ShiftOrbit pings my phone mid-service. I'm approving a Thursday swap while calling the pass on twelve tickets. That's exactly when I make mistakes.

R: What kind of mistake?

BC: Approved a swap once that left Saturday with no grill-cert. Sunday scramble, called one of the crew in on their rest day. Cost me goodwill I still owe.

R: The two peaks — how do they differ?

BC: Lunch is the office crowd, in and out, order-to-handover around five, six minutes on a good day. Dinner skews families and delivery — aggregator tickets are a third of our evening board some nights. Different stress, same eighteen bodies.

R: You worked drive-thru before this?

BC: Two years, drive-thru express. Network median window time is 246 seconds — we'd celebrate 230. Target's 180. I hit 180 exactly once, with a runner whose only job was the window. On a bad Friday the ninetieth percentile pushed past 400 seconds. 411 was our worst month.

R: What's window time made of?

BC: Order point, grill board, hold bins, payment, hand. The card reader alone eats forty seconds when it's thinking. It's never one thing; it's eleven things each stealing four seconds.

R: The aggregator counter at night?

BC: Three tablets — Dashly, Wheelio, Pedalr — three sounds. At 18:50 you genuinely cannot tell which one is shouting. Route56 is supposed to merge them into one screen; the pilot reaches our region in June. I'll believe it at 18:50 on a Friday.

R: Handover to couriers — what breaks?

BC: Tagging. Wheelio orders print a sticker, Pedalr doesn't — depends which tablet. So the rail's half stickered, half faith. Couriers dig. That's where wrong bags happen.

R: Orders that vanish — how often?

BC: About once a fortnight here, a courier takes the wrong bag — two orders, similar names. Zero point seven percent company-wide sounds tiny until it's one family's dinner and a one-star paragraph.

R: What would make the close honest?

BC: A checklist that takes ninety seconds at 18:00, not thirty-four lines at midnight. Split the checks across the day and give me the time to do them where they land.

R: One change?

BC: Kill the triple tablets. And teach the swap app who's grill-certified. Two changes — sorry.

Interview 03 — Grace Mwangi · Crew, Northline Flagship #3 (8 months)

Crew experience · onboarding, roster life, breaks, signing

Frontstage voice
Date Fri 8 May 2026, 16:00–16:40 (40 min)
Location Crew room, Northline Flagship #3 (end of shift)
Interviewer ASD Researcher
Consent (synthetic) Written + verbal; synthetic recording; anonymised; right to withdraw not exercised

R: How did you start at Copperline?

GM: Applied in October. Two shadow shifts, then the grill-cert booklet. Third day I was on the char-grill during Friday dinner. I learned fast because I had to.

R: Did the training feel like enough?

GM: The booklet's good, honestly. Nobody had time to walk me through it. There's a difference between a booklet and a person.

R: How much notice do you get for your shifts?

GM: The rota comes Friday night or Saturday, for Monday. We all check ShiftOrbit at nine on Friday, like it's the lottery. My college timetable came out in April — theirs came out in October. I just — manage.

R: Tell me about the last thing the short notice cost you.

GM: My cousin's confirmation. I got the Monday rota Saturday, I was on the Sunday, and by the time I'd asked everyone it was too late to swap. I sat in the car park at break and listened to it on the phone. That one still — yeah.

R: Have you tried the swap flow in the app?

GM: Twice. You post it and it just… sleeps. Nobody sees it. I ended up messaging three people myself; one said yes. Now I just ask the shift leader face to face. The app is where swaps go to sleep.

R: Your hours week to week?

GM: Twenty-four one week, thirty-one the next. Great when you need money, awful when you need a bus pass or a babysitter. You can't plan a life on a Wednesday guess.

R: What's 12:30 like on the grill line?

GM: It doesn't stop. Eighteen of us at peak, and it's loud and it's hot and you don't drink water because there's no gap to step out. You just decide you're not thirsty.

R: Breaks?

GM: Thirty minutes, unpaid, on paper. In the 18:30 rush my break starts forty minutes late, and then there's no point, you lose it. You can't complain mid-shift — complain to who, mid-shift?

R: Do you do Q-Pulse checks yourself?

GM: Fridge temps at open, yes, that one's real. Sometimes the manager says "just sign the ones from lunch, we're behind." I sign. Is that bad? Everybody does it.

R: Best part of the job?

GM: The crew. And the regulars — there's a man who orders the same quarter-chicken every Tuesday. He knows my name. That's silly, but it's the thing.

R: Worst part?

GM: When an app order comes out wrong and the customer shouts at me at the counter for something a tablet did. I didn't take that order. I'm just the face standing nearest.

R: A year from now?

GM: My course goes full-time in January. If the rota could know that — I'd stay till then, maybe around it. Ask me in December.

R: If you could fix one thing?

GM: The rota, two weeks early. I'd organise my whole life. Me and everyone I work with. That's it — that's the whole answer.

Interview 04 — Elena Petrova · Franchisee, 6 stores (Petrova Group)

Franchise partner · Flamebook, LTOs, audits, six-store rostering

Support & partner voice

Location Petrova Group office, above Standard store #907, Eastcliff region
Interviewer ASD Researcher
Consent (synthetic) Written + verbal; synthetic recording; anonymised; right to withdraw not exercised
Estate 2 Standard · 2 Food-court · 1 Drive-thru express · 1 Travel-hub kiosk · ~147 crew

  2. Flamebook as the franchise operating system

R: Describe your estate.

EP: Six stores, nine years in. Two standards, two food-court, one drive-thru express, one travel-hub kiosk. A hundred and forty-seven crew, give or take a Tuesday.

R: How do you run rostering across six sites?

EP: Sunday night, six ShiftOrbit consoles, one of me. Forty-one percent of stores publish under seventy-two hours — in my estate it's the food-court pair, because the mall doesn't confirm trader hours till Thursday and my week isn't knowable before theirs.

R: Swaps across stores?

EP: I allow cross-store swaps — ShiftOrbit won't treat my six as one pool, so I approve them manually. Sunday night is swap night. Last week: sixty-one requests across the estate, I reworked nineteen myself. Nineteen. On a Sunday.

R: Let's talk Flamebook.

EP: The grill manual is v4.2. The LTO annex pinned on top of it says v4.1. The allergen matrix is a PDF from March — I don't know which March. My food-court GM printed three versions before we found the current one. That's not an intranet, that's an archive with opinions.

R: Walk me through the last LTO launch.

EP: Firebasket. Six days' notice of the price file. The CopperPOS update landed at 21:00 the night before launch — my GMs updated tills at midnight on their knees. The stock arrived with the pallets unlabelled against the new POS codes. My people are good; the pipeline treats launch day as a surprise to everyone including itself.

R: What do LTOs do to your waste line?

EP: Network runs 3.8 percent against a 2.5 target. My kiosk runs five-plus in the last week of any promo — the demand curve drops off a cliff and StockBeak's replenishment proposal lags two days behind the cliff. I can see the chicken; the algorithm can't.

R: Audit exposure — how real is the Q-Pulse risk for you?

EP: Twenty-two percent of checks logged in bulk at close is an open secret with a timestamp. Auditors sample timestamps. I tell my GMs: the truth, logged late, beats fiction logged on time. But the tool makes fiction easier — that's the indictment.

R: Your relationship with Daniel Okafor's team?

EP: Good people, slow loops. Compliance notices land in Flamebook with no read receipt, no expiry, no priority. I have 340 unread notices. Nobody on earth has 340 unread emails — why would anyone design a notice board that way?

R: Company stores vs franchise — same systems, different support?

EP: The company stores get regional visits, pop-up help desks, pilots. We get the same dashboard and an email address. I'm not bitter — I'm paying for the same licence and getting half the ladder.

R: Aggregators across your formats?

EP: The drive-thru express is my best-rated store on Dashly — 4.5 — because its handover window has an actual shelf. The food-court pair sat at 4.1 on Wheelio for a month over cold fries. When your rating dips under 4.2, the app buries you. That's rent, not a review.

R: What would you change first?

EP: One Flamebook with versions that expire. Not a notice board — a manual with a spine. Current or it's gone.

R: Anything else I should have asked?

EP: Ask what happens to my good GMs. The best one left for a bank in March. Ninety-four percent turnover company-wide — nobody asks whether roster stress cares how good you are. It doesn't.

R: One more: what keeps you in the system?

EP: The chicken's good and the model works when the week is calm. Give me calm weeks and I'm your cheapest advocate at the partner conference.

Interview 05 — Jack Doyle · Aggregator Courier (Dashly + Wheelio)

Delivery perimeter · pickups, waits, three apps, breakage

Frontstage voice
Date Thu 14 May 2026, 13:20–13:50 (30 min)
Location Courier van, Harbourgate retail park (between pickups)
Interviewer ASD Researcher
Consent (synthetic) Verbal; synthetic recording; anonymised; right to withdraw not exercised
Context 14 months courier · serves the Flagship #14 cluster · declined Pedalr work
Evidence 6 notes (246–251)
Guide topics
  1. A night of pickups, 17:00–21:30
  2. Waiting at the counter
  3. Three aggregators, one courier
  4. What breaks at handover
  5. What a good store does differently

R: Typical evening?

JD: On the road 17:00 to 21:30, nine to twelve drops. Half my pickups land between 18:30 and 19:30 like everything else in this country. Whatever's happening in those stores at dinner, it's happening to me too, from the other side of the counter.

R: Walk me into a store.

JD: Best ones: a rack by the counter, name facing out, I'm gone in ninety seconds. Worst: I queue behind the dinner rush with everyone else, shouting my order code over the hand dryer. Same chain. Same hour. Different planet.

R: How long do you wait, typically?

JD: Six and a half minutes average — I've started timing it, that's sad, isn't it. Some stores it's ten. And you feel every one, because your next pickup is already pinging on the seat next to you.

R: How do the apps treat your time?

JD: Dashly counts my dwell time at the store and scores me on it. Wheelio pays per drop and the wait is unpaid. So one app punishes me for their queue, the other one just doesn't care. Pedalr — I don't ride for Pedalr any more. Make of that what you want.

R: The "ready" pings — accurate?

JD: The app says ready before the bag exists. Four, five minutes early, median. I've stopped trusting it. I park around the corner and do my own maths on how long a charcoal grill takes.

R: Tell me about the last broken handover you saw.

JD: Two Johns. Two bags. I took the wrong one — exactly once, touch wood. Store remade it, customer waited forty minutes, one star from her, a paragraph from Dashly about me. The bag I took had no sticker. The stickerless ones are always the third tablet.

R: How often does that kind of thing happen?

JD: Zero point seven percent company-wide — your people told me that number, and it feels right. It's never the rush-hour bag with the careful label. It's the quiet one, the unstickered one, 21:15 on a Tuesday.

R: Cold food — whose fault is it?

JD: Shared. Store holds it six minutes in a draft by the door, my bag's thermal but it's not magic, and some of those flats have elevators from 1990. There's enough blame to go around and no system for sharing it.

R: How do the crew treat you?

JD: Crew are class. Mostly kids being shouted at by tablets — honestly, I'm the polite one in their evening. The #14 lot know my van now. That's not favouritism; that's the only system that works — knowing each other.

R: Does your pay structure change how you wait?

JD: Per drop plus a peak bonus. The bonus punishes waiting — every minute at that counter I'm choosing between polite and paid. That's not a character flaw, that's arithmetic.

R: If Copperline fixed one thing tomorrow?

JD: One shelf, one label standard, all three apps. And make the tablet tell the truth about when the bag's actually sealed. Two things. Everyone says two things to you, right?

R: Anything good to report?

JD: The GM at #14 had a rail made — proper stainless, name slots. Cut my median wait there to under four minutes. It exists. It cost, what, a shelf? It's just not a rule anywhere else.

R: How often does Copperline feature in your week?

ML: Twice at lunch, and on late Wednesdays our floor orders delivery together — one basket, one poor soul whose turn it is to chase the courier.

R: Once you've ordered — how long to food?

R: And the app, for pickup?

R: Scale of the operation?

RP: Four contact channels — voice, chat, email, social. Voice is twelve of us plus me; the fortnight your team pulled is 3,206 calls. Most refunds self-serve in the app now, so the ones that reach a human are the hard ones. Mondays are refunds day. Every Monday since the dawn of time.

R: Reason profile?

RP: Thirty-eight percent refunds. Twenty-two percent where-is-my-order. Then missing items, app issues, wrong orders. If I could fix one thing upstream, half my floor exists because a bag was sealed wrong somewhere. We are the national apology department for a label printer.

R: AHT is 4:51 network average — how is that managed?

RP: Dashboard colour. Green under 4:30, red over 5:30. Red agents get coached. The dashboard doesn't know the call ran red because the store phone rang out for four minutes. We coach the symptom and starve the cause.

R: What actually drives long calls?

RP: Aggregator orders — six-oh-seven median. Dashly says it's the store, the store says it's Dashly, and my agent is Switzerland with a headset. Every aggregator call starts with a three-minute argument about whose system has the truth.

R: Tell me about the empowerment bands.

RP: Ladder: agent can comp a side, senior agent a full meal, team lead a full refund. My agents use the bottom rung brilliantly and are scared of the top. Above a full combo it needs a lead — and the leads are on calls, because they're always on calls.

R: Escalations — one in eight, I believe?

RP: Twelve percent, yeah. Half of them are "the agent said no, correctly, and the customer wants a bigger no from someone with a different lanyard." The other half are real — the ones where three systems disagree and only a human with seniority can pick a truth.

R: Repeat contact?

RP: Seventeen percent call again inside a week. Usually about the same refund. Refund-to-card runs about three days — that's a long time to stay angry about dinner, so they check we exist. Fair enough.

R: Do calls and VoiceBoard connect anywhere?

RP: Separate universes. The review says "never again," the call log says refund issued, and nobody joins them up. Your team is the first to ask for both halves. I've been waiting six years for someone to ask for both halves.

R: When do your peaks land?

RP: We track the stores with an hour's delay. Our volume doubles 17:30 to 19:30 — when the stores drown, we drown an hour later, and then Monday morning we do the paperwork for the drowning.

R: Your own floor's conditions?

RP: Turnover's lower than stores — thirty-odd percent — but climbing. They put us on ShiftOrbit last year and now my agents get their rota late too. It's contagious, apparently. Latency, I mean. Latency is contagious.

R: What would you fix first?

RP: One screen showing the order state from all three aggregators plus CopperPOS, and let my good agents refund without hunting a lead. You'd halve my red dashboard and my escalations in a fortnight.

R: Anything else?

RP: Log the free sides. Stores comp hundreds of sides a week at the counter and nobody counts them. That's the cheapest recovery data in the company and it evaporates at the till. Put a button on it.

R: Why am I here at two in the morning?

OV: Because inbound arrives overnight so trucks can roll 05:00 to 09:00 and stores get chicken before open. The whole region's dinner gets decided by what happens on this dock before three. You wanted the start of the journey — this is earlier than the start.

R: What comes in at night?

OV: Chilled birds and portions, zero to four degrees, fourteen inbound loads a week here — and there are four of us DCs doing the same maths. Temperatures get logged at the dock, for real, because an auditor can physically stand in my chiller. Downstream, I'm told, it gets more… creative.

R: How do store orders reach you?

OV: StockBeak proposes, the store confirms, cutoff's 20:00. About 170 order files a night for my region. And the late ones are always the late-rota stores — a GM who built their roster Sunday night discovers their Thursday labour on Tuesday and amends their order after cutoff. The lateness travels.

R: How often does that happen?

OV: A fifth of stores amend after cutoff. Thirty-odd manual lines a night, at one in the morning, by twelve tired people. Every amendment is a choice between accuracy and the 05:00 wave.

R: Tell me about the last LTO from your side.

OV: Firebasket. The stock hit the deck with forty-eight hours' notice, pallets unlabelled against the new POS codes. We matched cartons to codes by eye and carton count. Launch morning, the region ate correctly by luck and overtime.

R: What happens when you're short?

OV: Substitution — wings for thighs, with a sticker on the cage. Store finds out at 05:30 at the back door. Then their menu board is telling a small lie until somebody notices. The lie is usually noticed by a customer.

R: Do you see the waste number?

OV: I see over-supply; the waste happens in store. Last week of a promo, the clawback orders don't come down fast enough, and the chiller's full of Firebasket thighs going somewhere sad. 3.8 percent network-wide. I ship the problem; stores bin it.

R: Is there a loop back from stores to you?

OV: Theoretically. The store's waste log goes to a dashboard nobody at my end reads, and StockBeak's curve lags two days behind reality. The loop exists on the org chart. In this building, at 2 a.m., the loop is me squinting at a chiller.

R: Your drivers — do they wait?

OV: Twenty-five minutes at the bays some nights — the 05:00 wave overbooks the docks. Same disease as your courier counter, bigger vehicles. Everyone in this company waits; nobody queues the waiting.

R: Your own team's conditions?

OV: Night premium, and I keep people — my turnover's nothing like the stores. But I lose the good ones eventually to day roles at the Support Centre. Night shift is a career onboarding programme for people who then leave me politely.

R: What would you fix first?

OV: Freeze the order file earlier, price the amendment into the system instead of onto my crew, and label promo pallets with POS codes before they reach me. Three things. You'll notice none of them are my things.

R: Anything else for the record?

OV: Your courier fellow and I should talk, seriously. His six minutes at the counter and my twenty-five at the bay — it's the same wait, wearing different uniforms. Design the waiting once, and you've fixed both ends.
