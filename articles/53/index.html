01 · Executive summary

Executive summary

A global quick-service restaurant chain's self-service ordering channel doubled every year for three consecutive years and reached roughly half its restaurants — while the operating model around it stayed frozen. No channel training (onboarding courses for order-taking and register work had zero self-service content, the learning platform held only IT troubleshooting), no rollout process for new installations, no floor role (the time & attendance platform could not even roster a team member to stand in the foyer), and no design for what the machine cannot do — no cash, express-kiosk exclusions, menu mismatches, no failure script.

2×/yrchannel growth for three consecutive years
~15%kiosk share of in-store sales, from low single digits
~41%kiosk share at inline stores, vs ~9% drive-thru-led
6,700+guest comments mined in the wide sweep

Over 28 weeks, a regional channel and learning working group ran the full arc: a channel review on four evidence streams (performance data, guest-feedback mining at two scales, benchmarking of franchisee floor-role programs, pilot measurement), an omnichannel journey map, seven insights, a concept from four converging franchisee role designs, a training ecosystem built on practice, competition, and recognition, labour-deployment mathematics, and audit-linked brand standards. The villain of the story is the gap between installation and adoption; the hero is the foyer host — a named, rostered, trained human who stands between the door and the machine.

What you will learn:

  • Why installation is not adoption: usage follows store format, customer flow, and the presence of floor support — not the presence of the machine.
  • How to treat feedback absence as a success signal: when the floor role works, the channel disappears from guest comments; when it fails, the machine gets the blame and the crew get the review.
  • How four franchisee floor-role designs converged into one standard, and how a training ecosystem, labour mathematics, and audit-linked governance made it stick.
02 · The brief

The brief

Client portrait

The chain operates several thousand restaurants across a multi-market footprint, roughly evenly split between company and franchisee operations, with franchisee groups running materially different operating cultures. Self-service kiosks were installed in just over half the regional network (~400 restaurants), with a global mandate that all new builds operate kiosks from 2024 onward. The business case was proven in other markets — 10–20% ticket uplift, payback under two years — and the pandemic had shifted customer preference from human interaction to digital ordering. Yet the operating system that should have served the channel — training, rollout, rostering, recovery — did not exist.

The challenge

The challenge. The stated brief, as the research deck recorded it, was direct: "There is currently no process in place to help train and upskill our teams on kiosks once they are installed into their restaurant. Troubleshooting and development documents exist, however they are not sent to restaurants proactively or collated." The training gap was a symptom; four interlocking gaps surfaced in the first pass:

Gap What was actually true
No channel training Onboarding courses for order-taking and register work had zero self-service content; channel training did not exist for any ordering channel; training was only built where 80%+ of restaurants had the technology — and kiosks fell below that bar for years
No rollout process No defined process for installing kiosks and bringing a restaurant team up to speed; the user guide distribution had lapsed entirely
No floor role The foyer customer-service role was not formalised — the time & attendance platform could not roster someone as a foyer host or greeter; the concierge section of the user guide was outdated and stuck in the IT backlog
No design for channel limitations The kiosk is not a replica of the register or the app: no cash, express-kiosk exclusions on specific items, menu availability mismatches, and no team script for machine failure

Success criteria (agreed with the client)

Success criteria (agreed with the client). The channel lead's remit named the KPIs the project had to move: penetration (% of stores equipped), adoption (% of in-store sales), sales and conversion, technology performance, and guest feedback. Against those, the working group agreed a baseline and target set:

Metric Baseline Target
Kiosk penetration (% of stores equipped) ~50% 100% of new builds from 2024; ≥90% of all stores by end-2025
Kiosk adoption (% of in-store sales) ~15% network; ~40% at top formats ≥80% adoption within the first 6 months of any new installation
Ticket vs counter ~10% higher, mostly incremental Protect ≥10% uplift without discounting
Guest feedback Kiosk-friction complaints (waiting, cleanliness, cash, tech) Staff recognition becomes the top positive theme; kiosk-failure cluster near zero
Labour No foyer role rostered; foyer cleanliness "predominantly negative" Every ~60 self-service orders redeploys one cashier to pack/present without adding headcount
Governance Standards advisory; compliance optional and invisible Audit-linked enforcement from Q2 2024; exception process via the annual operating plan

Constraints

Constraints. Labour was the hardest constraint: the foyer host is a floating role competing with back-of-house demand, and lower-volume stores could not staff one at all. Cash was a policy constraint: kiosks take no cash by design, cash-capable machines cost 3–4× a standard kiosk with an unproven business case, and team members handling cash at the machine were prohibited as unsafe. Technology added fragility — POS integration, nightly menu refreshes, pin-pad timeouts, all-kiosk outages. Data was siloed: channel sales existed but no customer-level linkage, so lifetime-value claims could not yet be confirmed. Accessibility and menu-board regulations constrained layouts, and franchise heterogeneity meant any standard would need local translation.

Why service design

Why service design. This was not a training-content problem on one channel. The failures spanned the machine, the floor, the rostering system, the learning platform, the audit, and four franchisee operating models — a socio-technical system whose failures live below the line of visibility. Polaine, Løvlie, and Reason's arc — research to insight, ideas, prototyping, implementation — gave the engagement its spine (See: 03-service-design-from-insight-to-implementation/04-from-research-to-insight.md); Lou Downe's good-services principles supplied the audit lens (See: 05-good-services/02-the-fifteen-principles.md); and the Service Profit Chain framed the economics: investment in frontline capability pays back through service value, satisfaction, and retained demand (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md).

03 · Approach

Approach: the AI-first service design process

The engagement followed the Double Diamond, with the channel review as Discover, the insight set as Define, the concept — floor role, training ecosystem, labour mathematics — as Develop, and the standards and governance as Deliver, every phase evidence-first: no concept before the insight set passed its acid test, no pilot before a written hypothesis, no standard before pilot evidence, no enforcement before the compliance mechanism existed. The engagement ran on desk research, service design, and feedback analytics; the agent workflow shown here is the practice pattern (Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, GitHub — Section 9).

04 · The work, phase by phase

Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research & evidence (weeks 1–8)

Goal. Explain the contradiction: a channel that doubles every year, installed in half the network, with no training, no rollout process, and no floor role — and locate where the customer experience actually breaks.

Method & SOP. Four evidence streams (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md): (1) channel performance — sales mix by store format and asset type, penetration and adoption; (2) guest-feedback mining at two scales — a deep read of every self-service-related comment in a three-month pull, and a wide sweep across a much larger store population to measure how often the channel surfaces at all; (3) cross-market benchmarking with four franchisee groups running parallel floor-role programs, plus a global scan of markets with older kiosk footprints; and (4) pilot performance measurement — mirror-store surveys on menu-board removal, store-level adoption tracking, and a monthly KPI cut benchmarking a pilot store pair. A design agency's current-state omnichannel map (online/offline × delivery/pick-up/dine-in/takeaway/drive-thru) supplied the customer spine.

AI execution. Fieldwork and store visits stayed human — franchisee relationships and restaurant floor contact are not delegable. Everything after collection was agent-amplified: Claude Code processed the feedback dumps into theme-coded evidence items, every quoted comment kept verbatim with its store-format tag, and ran the wide sweep across ~6,700 comments; OpenCode aggregated the channel-mix baseline and generated the sample dataset (data/kiosk-channel-sample.csv, linked in Section 5). The evidence register became the single source for every figure in the synthesis.

Human designer judgment. Choosing the two-scale feedback design — the deep dive alone would have over-weighted the channel's problems, the wide sweep alone would have missed them entirely — and insisting the benchmarking treat the four franchisee programs as designs to learn from, not compliance risks to audit.

Artifact produced. Evidence register, channel-mix baseline, feedback theme codes, benchmarking interview notes, journey-map desk research.

4.2 Synthesis (weeks 9–12)

Goal. Convert the evidence register into insights that each change what the chain designs — not observations that merely describe.

Method & SOP. Bottom-up affinity synthesis on a shared wall, as a hybrid session: the working group plus the channel lead worked the clusters while Claude Code maintained the digital twin — cluster names had to be active sentences, not nouns: "franchisees built their own training cards because none existed," not "Training."

AI execution. The synthesis agent proposed the first-pass clustering; humans merged and renamed freely; the agent re-propagated edits. Convergence produced seven insight cards in the standard three-part syntax (observed behavior + root cause + design implication). Codex then ran an adversarial challenge pass, asked to attack rather than agree — the pattern that has caught fabricated quotes on other engagements:

TERMINAL
codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read synthesis/insight-cards.md and
research/evidence-register.jsonl. For each of the 7 insights: (1) verify every
cited quote exists verbatim in the register, (2) argue the strongest
alternative explanation for the observed behavior, (3) flag any insight whose
design implication is not actually implied by the evidence. Be adversarial."

Codex killed one candidate insight ("customers distrust self-service" — contradicted by the doubling channel) and forced evidence upgrades on two others. The headline insight survived on the least intuitive evidence: when the floor role works, the channel becomes invisible in feedback.

Four design principles followed: D1 The human is the channel's load-bearing wall — name the role, roster it, train it. D2 Adoption, not complaints, is the KPI. D3 Design the limitations, don't hide them. D4 Standards without enforcement are suggestions.

Human designer judgment. Naming is framing: "the foyer host effect" redirected attention from the machine to the human and gave the franchisee programs a shared language; and the 3-of-3 failure finding was accepted as the project's most important data point, not an embarrassment to manage.

Artifact produced. Seven insight cards; four design principles; the feedback two-panel (Figure 4).

The foyer host effect

When the floor role works, the channel becomes invisible in feedback: ~45 of ~50 comments in the deep dive mention the kiosk; only 6 of ~6,700 in the wide sweep do. Feedback absence is the success signal — mine for it.

4.3 Mapping (weeks 13–15)

Goal. Build the two alignment artifacts the concept hangs on: the current-state journey map and the kiosk order workflow with the human role embedded.

Method & SOP. Journey mapping from tagged evidence, phases defined by guest goals rather than internal stages, pains strictly separated from opportunities (See: 09-mapping-experiences/04-customer-journey-maps.md). The order workflow was mapped as a swimlane — guest frontstage, restaurant backstage — with greeting scripts embedded where the human appears.

AI execution. Claude Code authored both diagrams as version-controlled mermaid files, diffed in pull requests like software. Every unanswerable question in the mapping rehearsal — "who flips the order to ready when the presenter is packing?" — became a tracked issue. Codex reviewed the workflow adversarially and caught a missing exception path: cash guests had no defined queue position, and the out-of-stock script did not exist.

Human designer judgment. Placing the line of visibility: the kiosk and collection screen are the visible face; the POS, kitchen display, and packer chain are deliberately backstage — but the foyer host's greeting is a frontstage act that must never be backstage. Refusing to split the map per channel would have re-siloed the artifact.

Artifacts produced. A1 journey map and A2 order workflow (Section 5), fail points carried into every training and standards decision.

4.4 Ideation & concept design (weeks 15–18)

Goal. Convert the insight set into one coherent concept — the human half of self-service — rather than a pile of training fixes.

Method & SOP. Structured how-might-we formulation from the insight cards, then a convergence workshop with the working group, franchisee representatives, and the learning team. Divergence and convergence were strictly separated; concept sheets were scored on desirability, feasibility, and viability.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the HMW bank (each prompt traced to an insight ID) and the four concept sheets; the digital synthesis wall kept every franchisee program visible as a live input. OpenCode built the first deployment calculator — the labour rule as a table any restaurant manager could read.

Human designer judgment. The pivotal reframe — from "how do we train people on the machine?" to "how do we design the human half of a self-service channel?" — and the decision to treat the four franchisee role programs as legitimate prototypes, not deviations from a single corporate answer.

Artifact produced. The concept, in three parts:

  • The floor-role standard. A foyer host required during peak hours for the first six months of any kiosk installation, or until adoption reaches 80% of in-store sales — reporting to the restaurant manager, owning kiosk operations and pre-peak checks (menu availability, collection screen, cleaning, stock), greeting every guest with a script, directing cash customers to the counter, with a presenter owning the 60-second hand-off.
  • The training ecosystem. Channel-specific, service-first: a 30-minute shift-huddle model built around practice, competition, and recognition — not passive e-learning — plus job aids, QR-linked video, and an error-code catalogue so the team, not the help desk, is the first line of recovery.
  • The labour mathematics. A deployment rule every franchisee can model: roughly 60 self-service orders per peak hour redeploy one cashier to packing and presenting — no added headcount.

4.5 Prototyping & pilots (weeks 18–22)

Goal. Test the riskiest assumptions at the lowest fidelity that answers the question — hypothesis first, prototype second.

Method & SOP. Four parallel prototypes, each with a written experiment canvas before anything shipped:

  • R1 — Franchisee floor-role programs (live, in network). Four franchisee groups ran four designs — a foyer greeter-host with roleplay training and a training card; a customer-experience champion program with an eight-hour curriculum; a customer specialist role still in design; a service champion behaviour standard — benchmarked, measured, and converged, with the honest failure capture: a post-implementation phone check found 3 of 3 sampled restaurants unaware of the flagship program.
  • R2 — Training huddle model (30 minutes, in store). Practice on the machine, a head-to-head order competition, a knowledge check, recognition cards. Against the chain's passive e-learning, the huddle won on completion, confidence, and recall.
  • R3 — Menu-board removal mirror study (8 restaurants). Four stores with menu boards above kiosks against four mirror stores without; surveys on ease of ordering, menu clarity, and product findability, plus a financial analysis across a wider set. The survey overturned the incumbent design: guests found ordering easier without menu boards.
  • R4 — Pilot store-pair KPI cut (monthly). A redesigned store benchmarked against a conventional twin every month: order-taking time, ticket uplift, card mix, feedback scores, in-store pick-up time.

AI execution. OpenCode turned the deployment calculator into a reusable template and generated the sample dataset that accompanies this article. Claude Code drafted the huddle guide and roleplay scripts from R2 edge cases — first-time guest, cash guest, failed kiosk, out-of-stock — and tagged the mirror-survey results nightly. Codex reviewed the menu-board study design before it ran and flagged a sampling imbalance, corrected before fieldwork.

Human designer judgment. Setting pass/fail thresholds before each pilot and holding them — the 3-of-3 awareness failure was a finding that changed the rollout design, not a franchisee's fault. Also rejecting cash-capable kiosks: 3–4× the cost, unproven throughput, unsafe with team members handling cash at the machine.

Artifacts produced. Four experiment canvases, huddle guide v1, deployment calculator, mirror-survey readout, monthly KPI cut, and the honest-failure log behind the governance design.

4.6 Implementation & governance (weeks 23–26)

Goal. Turn validated pilots into enforceable standards — implementation as design continued, not handoff.

Method & SOP. Standards-setting from the pilot evidence, an interim rollout process and training plan (the gap the brief named first), a franchisee submission and approval flow for installations (floor plan or photos, five-day review, the entire restaurant team trained before go-live), and a troubleshooting FAQ that turns the error catalogue into self-service recovery.

AI execution. The global brand standards were assembled as a version-controlled document set — hardware and placement standards, the kiosk-count formula, the operating model, the governance timeline — every clause traced to an insight or pilot. GitHub issues carried the open questions; PRs were the review gates, franchisee operations required on any clause touching labour, learning on any clause touching training. OpenCode generated the SOP documents and the placement self-assessment template.

Human designer judgment. The governance decision: compliance tied to the audit, not advice — new builds non-compliant after Q2 2024 may not open; ≥90% of stores compliant by end-2025; an exception process through the annual operating plan so enforcement has an escape valve. And the flagship program would be franchisee-led rather than mandated network-wide, given labour constraints.

Artifacts produced. Brand standards v10 (mid-2023), implementation playbook, troubleshooting FAQ with error-code ranges, interim rollout process, placement self-assessment template.

4.7 Measurement (weeks 26–28)

The human is the channel's load-bearing wall — name the role, roster it, train it.

Design principle D1

Goal. Close the loop: triangulated measurement across channel performance, guest feedback, labour, and compliance, feeding the scale decision.

AI execution. OpenCode maintained the KPI aggregation — channel mix, adoption by format, feedback theme counts, compliance readiness — into one readout; Claude Code drafted the evaluation report with every number generated from the same query set. The training model that won the pilot: a 30-minute shift-huddle built around practice, competition, and recognition — the opposite of the chain's passive e-learning, which had never covered the channel. A5 — Deployment cards & usage calculator

The labour mathematics as a reusable table: peak front-counter forecast × kiosk usage % → redeployed cashiers → pack/present staffing, under the golden rules (guest through the system within 5 minutes; order to collect within 60 seconds; one packer/presenter per cashier; till capacity 60). Worked example: peak forecast 150 × 40% usage = 60 kiosk orders → 1 cashier redeployed → 2 cashiers + 4 pack/present, no added labour. Every card carries the recurring note: the foyer host is key to driving customer interaction, use, and adoption of the kiosk during peak.

A6 — Implementation playbook

Placement standards (kiosks between entrance and counter on the dominant path; 3+ kiosks ideal; spacing minimums; power placement never dictated by wiring alone), the kiosk-count formula (peak-hour order rate × growth factor × target adoption ÷ per-kiosk capacity ≈ 6, minimum 3), and a franchisee submission flow: floor plan or three photos → five-day approval → installation → the whole team trained before go-live.

06 · Use cases

Use cases

Use case A — First-time guest, older, carrying cash

Scenario. A guest who has never used a kiosk walks in during lunch peak with cash. Before the redesign this was the store's worst-case interaction: no one greets, the kiosk rejects cash, the counter queue is long, and the review — if one is left — blames the machine and the crew.

What was designed. The foyer host greets within seconds, asks cash or card first, and offers the kiosk only as a faster, cashless option — never the only one. A cash guest is directed to the counter queue; a card guest gets a 60-second guided first order (I-03, I-05). The presenter verifies the order at hand-off and the feedback link lands on the receipt.

AI working session. Claude Code drafted and roleplayed the scripts against edge cases — first-time guest, gave-up-at-kiosk, "can I just pay at the counter?" — and Codex flagged the assist script as reading like a sales pitch; it was rewritten to lead with help, not conversion.

Outcome (pilot stores). The 3-of-3 awareness failure closed: sampled restaurants could describe the greeting script unprompted; staff-recognition rose to the top of positive feedback; cash-guest wait complaints dropped to near zero where the role was staffed.

Use case B — Cash customer at peak

Scenario. Peak hour, every kiosk occupied, a queue forming at the counter — and the store's cash customers have no path but the counter.

What was designed. The exception path is designed, not improvised: cash guests are identified by the host at the door and directed to a dedicated counter queue (the stealth POS — a register hidden from the ordering flow, never a cash-capable kiosk). The cash-machine case was examined and rejected: 3–4× the cost, lower throughput, unsafe with team members transacting cash at the machine (I-05, D3).

AI working session. OpenCode modelled the queue economics — kiosk versus counter throughput at 20–50% adoption bands — quantifying how many cash guests one stealth register absorbs before the queue breaks the 5-minute rule. Codex challenged the queue-independence assumption; the model was re-run with correlated usage and the staffing guidance updated.

Outcome (pilot stores). The 10+ minute cash wait verbatim disappeared from the fail-cluster in pilot stores; counter queue time held inside the 5-minute rule at 40% adoption.

Use case C — Kiosk failure recovery

Scenario. Three kiosks down at peak. Before the redesign, the crew "shrugged and stared" — troubleshooting knowledge lived in help-desk documents, not in restaurant teams (I-07).

What was designed. A first-line recovery ladder in the training standard: team members retry and read the error-code catalogue (ranges 100–699); a manager-only reboot is the second rung; the stand-alone reprint recovers an order whose payment state is unknown; guests are directed and reassured at every step. Only unresolved cases reach the help desk, code range already logged.

AI working session. Claude Code converted the FAQ into a decision-tree job aid; Codex red-teamed the ladder and found a missing step — a paid order that did not print had no recovery — so the auto-refund path was added.

Outcome (pilot stores). The "all kiosks out of order" cluster dropped sharply in trained stores; help-desk tickets fell as the team became the first line of recovery.

Use case D — New crew onboarding & franchisee self-assessment

Scenario. A new team member starts at a kiosk store; a franchisee opens a new build. Both previously received nothing — the onboarding courses had no self-service content and the rollout process did not exist.

What was designed. The 30-minute huddle loop (A4) is the onboarding vehicle: teach, practice, compete, recognise, verify — plus job aids and QR-linked videos. The franchisee path runs through the playbook: placement submission, five-day approval, installation, the whole team trained before go-live, and a placement self-assessment template for audit readiness.

AI working session. OpenCode generated the self-assessment template from the playbook's placement clauses; Claude Code drafted the huddle facilitator guide; GitHub PRs gated training-content changes with the learning team as reviewer.

Outcome (pilot stores). First-week completion of the huddle loop above 90% in pilot stores; audit-readiness self-assessments returned with zero placement gaps in the first franchisee wave.

07 · SOPs for AI

SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Three SOPs were encoded for agent execution, each following the same pattern: method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

    Feedback-mining SOP (research). Output: theme-coded evidence items with verbatim quotes. Acceptance: quotes copy-paste exact from the source; each item carries theme, store-format tag, and sentiment; the two-scale counts computed and reported together — never one without the other. Gate: human spot-check of 10% plus the Codex challenge pass. The prompt template:

NOTE
You are FEEDBACK-MINER. Input: one guest-comment export (CSV).
Extract discrete evidence items about the self-service channel.
For each item output EXACTLY:
- evidence_id: EV-<seq>
- theme: help | cleanliness | cash | tech_failure | speed | staff_recognition | other
- sentiment: -2..+2
- store_format: drive_thru_led | mall | inline | unknown
- text: the verbatim comment (never paraphrase)
- channel_mentioned: true|false
RULES: quote text must be copy-paste exact; if unsure, drop the item.
Run BOTH passes — deep dive (kiosk-related subset) and wide sweep (all
comments) — and report the counts together. Output JSONL. No invented
content; every item traces to a source row you include as source_row.
  1. Floor-role SOP-as-code (operations). The foyer host standard encoded as a configurable procedure so any market can translate it without breaking the core: triggers, presence rules, scripts, exceptions, and KPIs are fields, not prose. The standard below is the reference implementation.
CONFIG
sop: QSR-FHR-001
name: Foyer host floor-role standard
version: 1.0
owner: channel_lead
triggers:
  - kiosk_installed AND weeks_since_install < 26
  - adoption_share < 80%          # % of in-store sales via kiosk
presence:
  required: peak_hours
  rosterable: true                # time & attendance platform supports the role
  reporting: restaurant_manager
scripts:
  greeting: "Hi, I'm [name], your ordering host. Would you like faster,
             cashless ordering at the kiosk?"
  assist: help_navigate_without_judgment
  direct: cash_and_specific_piece_requests_to_counter_queue
pre_peak_checks:
  - menu_availability_synced
  - collection_screen_functional
  - kiosks_cleaned
  - receipt_and_payment_stock_ok
duties:
  primary: greet, assist, direct, drive_adoption
  secondary: foyer_cleanliness, high_touch_surface_cleaning_hourly
exceptions:
  cash_guest: counter_queue_with_smile
  out_of_stock: script_out_of_stock + counter_fallback
  tech_failure: first_line_ladder (retry -> catalogue -> manager_reboot -> reprint)
kpis: [adoption_share, feedback_staff_recognition, feedback_cleanliness,
       cash_wait_complaints, first_line_recovery_rate]
review_cadence: monthly

    Artifact-change SOP (implementation). Every change to a training or standards artifact is a PR containing: diff, linked insight or pilot ID, plain-language check, and a franchisee-operations label where the clause touches labour. Acceptance: schema checks green plus required reviewers; CODEOWNERS route training to the learning team, labour to franchisee operations, standards to the channel lead.

The meta-SOP. The engagement ran research → synthesize → map → ideate → prototype → implement → measure, with a hard gate between stages: no concept before the insight set passed its acid test; no standard before pilot evidence; no enforcement before the compliance mechanism existed. Process enforced by tooling, not memory.

Guardrails.

    No fabricated feedback. A quoted comment not found verbatim in the register fails the build; insights without evidence IDs fail schema validation.

    No invented economics. Every labour and cost claim in a standard traces to the calculator or a pilot readout; the cash-machine rejection rests on the written business case, not opinion.

    Franchisee variance is data. Agents may propose convergence, never uniformity; any clause presuming one operating model carries a translation flag for the franchisee review gate.

  • Human sign-off points. Insight naming; the floor-role standard; scripts; the cash decision; governance dates; the compliance exception process; the scale decision.

Failure modes observed. (1) Over-clustering in the first synthesis pass — noun buckets that would have flattened the franchisee designs into one generic "floor service" theme; fixed by active-sentence names. (2) A confident but wrong read of the wide sweep — the first pass counted channel mentions only in kiosk-related pulls, which would have hidden the absence signal entirely; fixed by the two-scale rule in the SOP. (3) Labour-model drift — an early calculator redeployed cashiers on average usage instead of peak, overstating the saving; fixed by locking the calculator to the peak-hour band the deployment cards use.

08 · Value

Value creation & measurement

Before/after (transformed from the engagement register).

Metric Before After (transformed)
Self-service share of in-store sales Low single digits (Year 1) ~15% network; ~40%+ at top formats; doubling annually for three years
New-install adoption No rollout process; no training; adoption left to chance ≥80% of in-store sales within 6 months when the floor role is staffed
Guest feedback Kiosk-friction cluster: waiting, cleanliness, cash, tech failures Staff recognition the top positive theme; channel near-invisible in the wide sweep
Ticket vs counter ~10% uplift, mostly incremental (test vs control) Protected; the machine "does the upselling for us" without discounting
Foyer cleanliness "Predominantly negative" in feedback Owned by the floor role's secondary duties; hourly high-touch cleaning in the standard
Tech recovery Help-desk dependent; guests abandoned at failed kiosks First-line team ladder: retry, error-code catalogue, manager reboot, reprint
Cash path 10+ minute counter waits; guests excluded from the channel Designed counter path with the stealth register; cash-machine option rejected on evidence
Governance Standards advisory; compliance optional and invisible Audit-linked from Q2 2024; ≥90% of stores compliant by end-2025; exception process defined

Causal logic (Service Profit Chain). The chain ran as Heskett, Sasser, and Schlesinger describe (See: 20-the-service-profit-chain/03-external-service-value.md): internal service quality — a rostered floor role, channel training that actually teaches, first-line recovery tools — raised frontline capability; external service value rose chiefly through the denominator of the value equation: access costs collapsed (no abandoned first-timers, no 15-minute ignored waits, a cash path that does not punish, a machine failure that does not end the visit). Satisfaction and staff recognition followed, retention and higher tickets produced the economics, and the redeployment rule converted labour cost into service capacity without adding headcount. Estimated annualized effect at scale: the uplift and redeployment are self-funding, and the engagement removed the adoption drag that threatened the sub-two-year payback.

KPI tree. North-star: kiosk adoption as % of in-store sales, read at store-format level. Drivers: penetration (stores equipped, new-build compliance), floor-role presence (host rostered at peak), training completion (huddle loop verified), tech reliability (first-line recovery rate). Lagging: ticket uplift vs counter, feedback sentiment (staff recognition, cleanliness, cash-wait complaints), redeployment achieved, standards compliance. Reviewed monthly in pilot; the wide-sweep count re-read quarterly as the absence signal.

09 · AI tools

AI tool playbook

An honest caveat first. The original engagement ran on desk research, service design, and guest-feedback analytics — no AI tooling. What follows is the retrospective practice pattern: how the same engagement would be executed today with named agent surfaces. The adjacent voice-AI ordering initiative is future direction, not part of this project.

Claude Code — evidence processing and artifact authoring. The engagement's synthesis engine: theme-coded evidence items under verbatim-quote rules, the digital synthesis wall, the HMW bank and concept sheets, the journey map and order workflow as version-controlled diagrams, the huddle guide and evaluation report. Excels at structured extraction against a schema; 80%-there first drafts. Failed at judging which insight would land politically — the designer named "the foyer host effect," not the agent. Human took over: live research, insight naming, gates.

OpenCode — data pipelines and calculators. Ran the channel-mix baseline, the two-scale feedback counts, the sample dataset (data/kiosk-channel-sample.csv), and the deployment calculator as a reusable template. Excels at self-directed pipelines and realistic seed data. Failed at method intent — an early pass counted mentions only in kiosk-related pulls, erasing the absence signal; the two-scale rule in the SOP became the leash.

Codex — adversarial review. Second-opinion engine, run with the artifact plus a specific concern, never the whole repo. Delivered the insight challenge pass (one insight killed), the workflow review (missing cash path and out-of-stock script), the script review, and the queue-model challenge. Excels at disagreeing productively — "be adversarial" is a load-bearing instruction. Occasionally flagged stated franchise constraints as defects; every finding was verified before action.

GitHub — the system of record. Issues as work items (every gap and open question traced to an insight or pilot ID), PRs as review gates (CODEOWNERS: learning team for training, franchisee operations for labour, channel lead for standards), the board as phase tracker, milestones as stage gates. 10 · Lessons

Lessons learned & pitfalls

    Design the service role alongside the technology. The strongest lesson of the engagement: kiosks succeed when a named, rostered, trained human drives adoption; pure tech rollout strands customers — "shrugged and stared at us" is the verbatim that should haunt every digital-channel business case.

    Feedback volume is not the KPI — mine for absence. The two-scale design (45-of-50 in the deep dive, 6-of-6,700 in the wide sweep) is the project's most reusable method: when the service works, the channel disappears from feedback — a single-scale read would have produced panic or complacency.

    Training must be channel-specific but service-first. Team members see their job as excellent service regardless of the channel; the 80%-technology threshold rule was a trap that left the fastest-growing channel untrained for years. The recommendation — a cross-channel experience blueprint before building channel training — is the right ordering.

  • Rostering enablers lag role design — fix them early. A brilliant floor-role standard is worthless if the time & attendance platform cannot roster the role. Scheduling must be "intentional about kiosk success" — this gap, not training, was the deepest blocker to scale.

    • Measurement discipline converts opinion into scale. Test-vs-control ticket evidence, mirror-store surveys, store-level adoption tracking, and monthly KPI cuts produced the numbers that justified global rollout; the 3-of-3 awareness check produced the number that justified governance.

  • Labour mathematics converts naysayers. "Every ~60 self-service orders redeploys one cashier" is a sentence a franchisee can act on; abstract appeals are not. The calculator made the labour case concrete and the foyer host affordable.

    • Design the limitations, don't hide them. Cash, express-kiosk exclusions, out-of-stock mismatches, and machine failure are real customer intents with no kiosk path. Every one needs a counter path and a script; the cash-machine option was rejected on evidence, not aesthetics.

  • Franchisee heterogeneity is the norm. Four groups, four role designs, four maturities — and one franchisee built its own training cards because none existed. Global standards need local translation; convergence, never uniformity.
  • Honest failure capture is what makes standards enforceable. The 0-of-3 awareness check was the engagement's most valuable data point: it converted "we have a program" into "we have a rollout problem" — and the governance design followed.
  • Done differently next time: baseline the audit scores in week 1 (the standards were scored after the pilots, losing comparative precision), and involve franchisee operations at insight synthesis, not standards drafting — two labour-clause rework cycles were avoidable.
