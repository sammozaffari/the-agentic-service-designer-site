Orchestrating Multi-Channel Experiences: One Collect-and-Return Service at Juniper Retail

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Juniper Retail is a fictional composite client, assembled from patterns observed across several European omnichannel home-and-lifestyle retailers; no real retailer, customer, or store associate is depicted. All names, figures, datasets, and quotes are illustrative and anonymized. This article shares knowledge and process — SOPs, prompts, artifacts, review gates — never client specifics. The engagement ran AI-first: Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were the primary working tools, while the human designer retained framing, facilitation, and every consequential decision.

1. Executive summary

Juniper Retail, a fictional EU omnichannel retailer with 140 stores, an e-commerce operation, and 3.2 million loyalty members, had built its click & collect and returns service the way most retailers do: one channel at a time. Online ordering belonged to the e-commerce team, the app to digital product, pickup to retail operations, email to CRM, and returns to a third-party logistics contract. Each channel worked tolerably on its own; the journey across them was a mess. 34% of click & collect orders generated a pickup-related customer contact, 11% of orders were never collected, and returns vanished into an eight-day refund blackout. Over 18 weeks, a single service designer amplified by an AI agent team mapped the journey as one system, used the Orchestration Canvas as the coordinating artifact across five channel-owning teams, redesigned the collection choreography end to end, and piloted a unified collect-and-return service in 12 stores plus online. The pilot halved the contact rate from 34% to 17%, cut no-shows from 11% to 6%, made in-store refunds instant and visible, and lifted pickup NPS from +9 to +34 (all figures illustrative).

What you will learn:

  • How to diagnose a channel-siloed service with a seam audit and journey telemetry, and how to read the Orchestration Canvas gap-first to turn silo friction into a roadmap (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/03-the-orchestration-canvas.md).

  • How to run orchestration work AI-first: interview synthesis at scale, telemetry seam-mining pipelines, canvas-as-code in Git, and adversarial review of cross-channel promises before pilot.

2. The brief

Client portrait. Juniper Retail is a fictional mid-market home-and-lifestyle retailer headquartered in Utrecht: 140 stores across the Netherlands, Belgium, and Germany, a €410M e-commerce channel inside €1.1B total revenue, one national distribution centre, and 3.2M loyalty members generating 61% of sales. Digital maturity is typical for the segment: a competent order management system (OMS), a loyalty app with 1.9M monthly active users, outsourced contact centre, and a returns operation split between stores and a 3PL running carrier pickup, parcel locker, and post office routes.

The challenge. Click & collect was Juniper's fastest-growing fulfillment mode — 1.6M orders per year, 28% of online volume — and its largest source of avoidable contact. 34% of click & collect orders produced at least one pickup-related contact: roughly 544,000 contacts a year at a blended €4.60 each, about €2.5M in failure demand. 11% of orders — 176,000 a year — were never collected inside the five-day window, each costing €3.10 in handling and restock plus markdown risk. Returns ran at 820,000 a year across the full e-commerce volume of about 5.7M orders; click & collect accounted for about 212,000 of those returns (13.3% of 1.6M orders) across four routes (in-store 46%, carrier pickup 21%, parcel locker 19%, post office 14%) with a median eight-day refund delay and no status visibility on any route. Five channel-owning teams — e-commerce, app product, CRM/email, retail operations, and third-party logistics — each optimized its own measures, yet no one owned the journey end to end.

Success criteria (agreed with the client).

Metric Baseline (illustrative) Pilot target

No-show pickup rate 11% ≤ 7%

Return Customer Effort Score (1–5, lower better) 3.8 ≤ 2.2

Constraints. Budget €240,000 all-in; 18 weeks to a pilot readout before the autumn peak trading freeze; no OMS replacement in-year (choreography had to sit on existing systems plus a thin event layer); GDPR including explicit consent for any location-triggered push; store labor budgets frozen, so no additional store headcount; the returns 3PL contract could be re-negotiated only at its September renewal.

Why service design. Nothing here was broken inside a channel — every seam between channels was. Risdon and Quattlebaum's diagnosis fit exactly: customers experience Juniper as one company pursuing one errand, while Juniper delivered five departmental services that happened to share a logo (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/01-why-orchestration.md). The work required an outside-in journey spine, a coordinating artifact every silo could read, and governance at the seams — orchestration as an operating discipline, not a UX polish pass (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/02-what-is-orchestration.md, 14-outside-in/05-designing-the-experience.md).

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research (weeks 1–4)

Goal. Understand the collect-and-return journey as customers actually live it — across every channel at once — and locate the seams where context drops.

Method & SOP. Triangulated fieldwork (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/04-research-methods.md): 60 episodic customer interviews (recruited across four archetypes: time-poor parents, serial returners, occasional C&C users, accessibility-needs customers), 90 store associate interviews across 30 stores (deliberately including the associates who staff the pickup desk, not just managers), an artifact audit of 41 journey communications, and the cross-channel seam audit from Risdon and Quattlebaum: at every channel transition, what context should carry forward, what actually transfers, what must the customer repeat (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/01-why-orchestration.md).

AI execution. Interviews were transcribed, pseudonymized (C-01…C-60, SA-01…SA-90), and processed by a Claude Code subagent team using the evidence-extraction SOP (Section 7): extractors produced evidence items, a verifier checked every quote verbatim against the transcript store, a reducer merged themes — 428 evidence items tagged by journey phase and channel seam in research/evidence-register.jsonl. In parallel, OpenCode ran the telemetry seam-mining pipeline in agent mode over 14 months of operational data — 1.9M click & collect order records, 960k return records, 740k tagged contact records:

opencode run --agent build "Read telemetry/orders-14m.csv telemetry/returns-14m.csv
telemetry/contacts-14m.csv. For every C&C order reconstruct the event timeline
(placed, confirmed, prep_started, ready, collected, lapsed, returned, refunded).
Compute: (1) contacts per order by event gap, (2) time-to-contact after each
notification, (3) no-show survival curve by window day, (4) refund-day distribution
by return route. Output seam-stats.json + 4 charts data. Seed fixed."

The pipeline produced the finding that reframed the engagement: 62% of pickup contacts arrived within six hours of an expected-but-missing notification. Customers were not confused about pickup in general; they were calling into silence at specific seams.

Human designer judgment. Recruitment quotas (oversampling serial returners, whom CRM had already mislabeled as fraud), the consent protocol for recording in stores, and the call to interview associates rather than only managers.

Artifact produced. Evidence register (428 items), seam inventory (X1–X6), artifact audit wall (41 artifacts), telemetry baseline.

4.2 Current-state mapping (weeks 4–6)

Goal. Build the artifact that makes the silo structure visible: one journey map with per-channel rows and every break annotated.

Method & SOP. Current-state journey mapping with phases defined by customer goals — get it, collect it, send it back — never by internal stages, with channel ownership rendered as rows so the handoffs are the subject of the map (See: 09-mapping-experiences/04-customer-journey-maps.md, 08-orchestrating-experiences/01-why-orchestration.md).

AI execution. Claude Code authored the map as mermaid code in Git. A mapping agent cross-checked every claim against the evidence register; any pain point without ≥2 evidence items or telemetry corroboration was flagged ungrounded. Six channel breaks survived:

  • X1 Web checkout → confirmation: order number arrives by email only; the app cannot see the order for up to 24 hours (batch loyalty sync).
  • X2 OMS → store: the "ready within 4 hours" promise is computed from batch stock, not floor reality; 31% of promises breached.
  • X3 Store ready → customer: ready notification sent by 6pm email batch; app silent; SMS only on the carrier's contract, not C&C.
  • X4 Customer → store: pickup desk location, queue, and ID rules vary by store; nothing in any channel tells the customer what to expect.
  • X5 Window end: the five-day window lapses silently; the order is restocked and refunded without any warning message; 41% of no-shows later contact support believing the order is still waiting.
  • X6 Return → refund: four routes, four SLAs, zero status visibility; median eight days of silence between drop-off and refund.

Human designer judgment. Refusing the e-commerce director's request to publish five per-channel maps — that would have re-siloed the one artifact meant to create cross-channel accountability.

Artifacts produced. A1 journey map, emotion curve, channel-break annotations X1–X6 carried into A9.

4.3 The Orchestration Canvas (weeks 6–7)

Goal. Give the five channel-owning teams one shared surface where the customer's journey and the organization's machinery meet — and read it gap-first.

Method & SOP. A two-day canvas co-build workshop following Risdon and Quattlebaum's architecture: journey phases on the horizontal spine, customer hemisphere (goals, actions, emotions, moments that matter) above, organizational hemisphere (channels, ecosystem actors, signals, responses, ownership, metrics) below (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/03-the-orchestration-canvas.md). Day one populated from research; day two ran the gap-first diagnostic: unsupported phases, orphaned signals, uncoordinated actions, unowned seams.

AI execution. Claude Code pre-populated the canvas skeleton from the evidence register, structured workshop inputs live, and produced canvas v1 as versioned markdown (canvas/orchestration-canvas-v1.md, PR #31). A checking agent scanned each phase for empty cells and each signal for a missing response:

You are CANVAS-AUDITOR. Input: canvas/orchestration-canvas-v1.md (markdown table).
For each journey-phase column report: (1) ORPHANED SIGNAL - a signal row entry
with no response row entry; (2) UNSUPPORTED MOMENT - a moment-that-matter with
no owner; (3) COLLISION - two channels listed as Primary for the same phase;
(4) UNOWNED SEAM - a channel transition with no named operational owner.
Output: gap register as JSON, one line per gap, citing canvas cell coordinates.
No interpretation beyond these four rules.

The audit found 19 gaps, including orphaned prep_started and window_day4 signals and one ownership collision (CRM and app both claiming the ready notification). Codex then caught a load-bearing promise error: the parcel-locker route could not honestly promise an instant settled refund, only instant initiation with an ETA.

Human designer judgment. Facilitation across five teams with competing KPIs, and the governance call that ended the notification collision: app leads, email supports, and a single Journey Owner arbitrates — channel roles, not channel territories (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/05-channels-and-ecosystems.md).

Artifacts produced. Orchestration canvas v1 (Section 5), gap register (19 items), five testable experience principles:

  • P1 One order, one story. Every channel shows the same status timeline. Negative test: never ship a status in one channel that another channel contradicts.
  • P2 Tell, don't be asked. Proactive notification beats pull. Negative test: never a status the customer must phone to obtain.
  • P3 Grace over penalty. Missed windows are rescued, never lapsed silently.
  • P4 Refund at the speed of return. Money moves when goods move; the customer watches it move.
  • P5 Consent-gated awareness. Location-triggered push only with explicit opt-in, revocable in one tap.

4.4 Choreography, blueprint, and requirements (weeks 7–11)

Goal. Design the future collect-and-return service as a choreographed sequence across channels, then specify it as a blueprint and cross-channel requirement cards engineering can build.

Method & SOP. Channel role definition per phase (Primary, Supporting, Recovery, Silent), handoff protocol design at each seam, then future-state blueprinting rehearsed column-by-column with store and operations leads (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/05-channels-and-ecosystems.md, 14-outside-in/05-designing-the-experience.md). Requirements were written as cross-channel conditions, not channel feature lists — "a customer who misses day 5 receives a rescue prompt on the channel they last used, with extension, re-slot, or refund, and every channel reflects the choice within 60 seconds" (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/06-defining-requirements-and-design.md).

AI execution. Claude Code drafted the choreography diagram and the first-pass requirement cards. OpenCode built the interactive prototype of the unified collection experience in agent mode — timeline, QR pass, grace-window rescue, return initiation, refund tracker — seeded with the mock dataset so every test used realistic orders:

opencode run --agent build "Scaffold prototype/ as static HTML+JS: order timeline,
pickup pass QR, day-4 rescue prompt, return initiation with route picker,
refund tracker. Seed with data/orders_sample.csv. No backend; stub events with
600ms delays. Copy at CEFR B1. Every screen shows the same five-step status
timeline - principle P1 is testable."

Sixteen hallway tests plus four store walkthroughs followed. The return-route picker failed 5 of 16 tests and was rebuilt around a map with opening hours. Codex stress-tested the requirement set against abandonment, channel switching, outage, high-stress use, and partner delay, exposing a Saturday OMS-outage gap; a degradation path was added.

Human designer judgment. Store associates refused to scan returns without a condition-check script that protected them from confrontation. The designer co-wrote it with two senior associates; the agents' drafts were too legalistic to say aloud.

Artifacts produced. Channel choreography diagram, future-state blueprint (A2), 14 cross-channel requirement cards, prototype v2, degradation playbook.

4.5 Pilot (weeks 11–17)

Goal. Prove the choreography live: 12 stores plus the full online funnel, six weeks, against pre-agreed thresholds.

Method & SOP. Pilot as design continued, with a charter naming scale/iterate/kill gates, frontline enablement, and weekly steering (See: 01-this-is-service-design-doing/07-implementation-and-piloting.md, 08-orchestrating-experiences/07-operating-and-evolving.md).

AI execution. The blueprint decomposed into 96 GitHub issues across five workstreams, each carrying its blueprint step ID. PRs were the review gates. The store rollout playbook (A6) ran store-by-store with two half-day training sessions each. Eleven issues were fixed in flight, including a day-4 rescue prompt that fired at 6am (moved to 10am local) and locker-location copy that failed German postal-code edge cases.

Human designer judgment. Gate negotiation with the sponsor, the weekly narrative that turned dashboard noise into decisions, and the floor visits.

Artifacts produced. Pilot charter, 96-ticket backlog, store rollout playbook, live KPI dashboard.

4.6 Measurement and governance (weeks 17–18)

Goal. Close the loop with triangulated measurement and hand orchestration over as a permanent operating discipline, not a project that decays back into silos.

AI execution. OpenCode maintained the KPI pipeline (nightly reconciliation of order events, notification delivery, contact coding, refund timestamps, and post-journey surveys into one scorecard); Claude Code drafted the readout as a reviewed PR with every number generated from the same query set. The governance handover followed Risdon and Quattlebaum's operating model: a named Journey Owner, a monthly Journey Council across the five channel teams, and a journey health dashboard combining lagging sentiment with leading seam telemetry — contacts per order, rescue-prompt response rate, refund-clock hours (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/07-operating-and-evolving.md). Gate decision: scale to all 140 stores over two quarters, with the returns 3PL SLA renegotiated at the September renewal using the cross-channel SLA template (A6).

6. Use cases

Use case A — The missed pickup window rescue

Scenario. Sofie (persona 1) orders storage boxes on Tuesday for Saturday collection. Saturday, her daughter gets sick; the five-day window will lapse Wednesday. Before the redesign: silence, restock, a day-8 trip to an empty desk, and a 14-minute support call.

What was designed. The grace-window engine: day-3 gentle nudge (push, consent-gated), day-4 rescue prompt on her last-used channel offering three one-tap options — extend 48 hours, re-slot to another store, or release with refund; day-6 release happens only after the rescue and carries a plain warning ("we will release your order tomorrow and refund you €84.50"). Every channel reflects her choice within 60 seconds (P1).

flowchart LR D3["Day 3: nudge push"] --> D4["Day 4: rescue prompt - extend, re-slot, release"] D4 --> EXT["Extend 48h: new promise, store notified"] --> OK["Collected day 6"] D4 --> REL["Release: refund initiated, visible in tracker"] D4 --> IGN["No response"] --> D6["Day 6: release with warning, never silent"]

AI working session. Claude Code drafted the rescue copy; a subagent red-teamed it for tone because v1 read like a penalty notice. OpenCode seeded the prototype with no-show records from the mock dataset; hallway tests moved the send time from 6am to 10am local. Codex verified that the refund promise named initiation plus ETA accurately.

Outcome (illustrative). No-show rate 11% → 6% in pilot stores; 58% of day-4 rescue prompts answered within 4 hours; "order disappeared" contacts effectively eliminated.

Use case B — Online order returned in-store, instant refund

Scenario. Daan (persona 2) bought three throws online, keeps one, and walks into his Antwerp store with the other two — no printer, no label, no patience.

What was designed. The till scans his in-app order QR (the order is the credential — no RMA codes). The associate runs the co-written condition script, scans both items, and the refund is triggered at the till: the tracker in Daan's app flips to "refund initiated" before he leaves the desk, with a settlement ETA of 1–2 days from the payments provider. The store's return-to-floor task is created automatically.

AI working session. OpenCode prototyped the till-screen flow in two days; Claude Code iterated the condition script with two senior associates until it was sayable at the desk. Codex caught that "instant refund" was only instant as an initiation event, so the tracker now shows an ETA rather than a bare promise.

Outcome (illustrative). In-store route share rose 46% → 54% of pilot returns; refund visibility for this route is immediate to initiated; return-related contacts in pilot cohort down 49%; return CES for in-store returns 2.0 vs 3.8 baseline.

Use case C — The split-order confusion

Scenario. A customer orders a lamp for click & collect and a rug for home delivery in one basket. Before the redesign: two order numbers, two confirmation emails, the app showing only the rug, and a support call — "where is the rest of my order?" Split baskets generated 2.4× the contact rate of single-mode orders.

What was designed. An order-group view: one basket, one story. The timeline shows both fulfillment tracks as parallel lanes under one order group, each with its own promise and tracker, with a plain-language banner up front: "Your lamp comes to the Antwerp store; your rug comes to your door. Track each below." Emails mirror the same grouping.

AI working session. Claude Code generated the grouped timeline and copy from P1; the notification router's anti-fatigue rules were extended to order-group level after telemetry showed grouped orders doubled message volume. A GitHub Action linted every template against readability and P1 consistency.

Outcome (illustrative). Split-order contact rate −61% in the pilot funnel; order-group comprehension in hallway testing 15/16 on first exposure; zero additional notification volume after anti-fatigue rules.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Four SOPs were encoded for agent execution, each as method → steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Orchestration-canvas build SOP. Journey → channels → dependencies → gaps. Acceptance: every customer-hemisphere cell cites evidence; no phase has two Primaries; no signal without a response or a logged reason. Gate: CANVAS-AUDITOR scan plus the human co-build session.
  2. Channel-choreography prompt. Used to draft and check the choreography diagram:
You are CHOREOGRAPHER. Input: journey spine + channel role matrix + principles.
Produce a mermaid flowchart LR with one subgraph lane per channel actor
(Customer, App, Email-SMS, Store, OMS-Payments). Rules:
- Every notification names its trigger event, its Primary channel, its mirror.
- Every handoff across lanes carries context: order id, state, next promise.
- No channel contradicts another (P1); no silence where a promise exists (P2).
- Every lapse or failure has a designed path (P3); refunds visible (P4).
- Location or push triggers marked CONSENT-GATED (P5).
Self-check: list any moment where the customer would need to phone to know
what is happening. There must be none.
  1. Telemetry seam-mining SOP. Reconstruct per-order event timelines → compute contact proximity to missing events → output seam stats. Acceptance: every claimed correlation reproducible from the same query set; no hand-typed numbers.
  2. Contact-reduction SOP-as-code (JR-CC-001, A6): triggers, responses, guardrails, KPIs as YAML.

The meta-SOP. Research → synthesize → map → canvas → choreograph → blueprint → prototype → pilot → measure → govern, with hard gates: no canvas before the seam inventory is evidence-backed; no prototype before choreography passes the no-phone-needed self-check; no pilot before the blueprint rehearsal closes all blueprint-gap issues; no scale before thresholds are met.

Guardrails.

  • No channel-owner blindness. Agents must flag any requirement that improves one channel's local metric at a seam's expense.
  • Consent for location push. Any location-triggered or push response is marked CONSENT-GATED; opt-in explicit, revocable in one tap, EU data residency.
  • Hallucination control. Quote verification on every synthesis PR; canvas cells require evidence IDs.
  • Human sign-off points. Interview conduct, insight/principle naming, channel role assignment, spoken customer copy, pilot go/no-go, scale decision.

Failure modes observed. (1) Agents defaulted to app primacy; fixed by the channel role matrix and a non-app-path CI check. (2) Over-notification; fixed by max-4-message and cooldown guardrails. (3) Promise inflation around "instant" and "real-time" claims; fixed by a promise audit. (4) Mermaid drift on the choreography diagram, killed by CI linting.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • The problem was never inside a channel. Every silo's dashboard was green while the journey bled at six seams. The seam audit and the 62%-of-contacts telemetry finding did more to align five teams in one workshop than a year of cross-functional meetings had (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/01-why-orchestration.md).
  • The canvas only works as a living artifact. Canvas v1 as a workshop poster would have died in a week; canvas-as-code in Git, re-scored quarterly with an automated gap audit, became the governance surface. Version it or lose it.
  • Agents default to app-primacy. Every agent draft initially assumed the app was the center of gravity, which would have designed out a fifth of customers. The channel role matrix and the non-app-path CI check are now standard guardrails — assign channels roles, never let convenience assign them (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/05-channels-and-ecosystems.md).
  • Promise inflation is the AI-specific failure mode of this domain. "Instant refund," "real-time tracking," "live stock" — agents reach for the strongest adjective. The promise audit (name what is instant, name what has an ETA) caught eleven instances before customers did.
  • Store associates are the seam. Ninety associate interviews produced the map's most quoted line — "I tell them to call the number on the email" — and the condition script that made instant refunds survivable at the till. Frontline research is where orchestration designs are won.
  • Serial returners were a segment, not a fraud ring. Telemetry plus nine interviews showed rational, rules-following behavior; the CRM team's fraud framing dissolved under evidence and the group became the refund tracker's design north star.
  • Grace beats penalty, measurably. The rescue engine recovered 58% of lapsing orders with one prompt; the client's draft policy had been a no-show fee. Punishing life-happens would have taxed Juniper's most loyal archetype (See: 08-orchestrating-experiences/02-what-is-orchestration.md).
  • Done differently next time: instrument the leading seam telemetry before the pilot build (it arrived week 14, costing two weeks of dashboard blindness), and bring the returns 3PL into the canvas workshop in week 6 rather than at SLA renegotiation — their scan-feed latency was discoverable months earlier.
