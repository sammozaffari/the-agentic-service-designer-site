Story Mapping & Narrative Structure: Re-Storying Onboarding at Quill

0. Confidentiality & provenance note

Quill is a fictional composite client, assembled from patterns observed across several European B2B SaaS vendors; no real company, user, or workspace is depicted. All names, figures, datasets, and quotes are illustrative and anonymized. The article shares knowledge and process — SOPs, prompts, artifacts, review gates — never client specifics. The engagement ran AI-first: Claude Code, OpenCode, Codex, and GitHub were the primary working tools; the human designer retained framing, facilitation, and the ethical calls on persuasive design.

1. Executive summary

Quill, a project-management SaaS headquartered in Amsterdam with roughly 41,000 workspaces, had built its onboarding the way most product teams do: as a catalog of features to be toured. A nine-step tooltip carousel, a 14-field workspace setup form, and an invite wall before any value meant that of every 100 new workspaces, 52 finished setup, 26 created a first project within 14 days, and only 18 were still active in week 4. Over 24 weeks, a single lead service designer amplified by an AI agent team restructured the entire setup-to-habit journey as a story: the user — never the product — as hero; a dramatic question ("will this team be genuinely productive in Quill within 14 days?"); a story spine with named beats; a guided-first-project "mentor moment"; and celebratory mechanics that reward real user accomplishment instead of system events. Telemetry for 12,000 workspace funnels, 85 interviews, and a 4-week storymapping workshop series with product, support, and customer success fed the redesign. Headline results after rollout: day-14 activation 26% → 47%, median time-to-first-project 9 days → 2 days, week-4 active workspaces 18% → 33%, invite viral coefficient 0.65 → 0.94.

What you will learn:

  • How to run storymapping as a service design method — story extraction from interviews, the Pixar story spine, beat-maps, and plot-hole diagnosis — with agents doing the extraction and drafting while humans hold the narrative frame.
  • How to restructure a feature-led product experience around a narrative spine: progressive disclosure, an early climax, a mentor moment, and honest celebratory mechanics with explicit anti-manipulation guardrails.
  • How to measure a narrative: activation beats as a KPI tree, beat-transition telemetry, and story-led executive readouts — with the narrative living as versioned docs in GitHub, gated by PR review on every copy change.

2. The brief

Client portrait

Quill is a product-led-growth SaaS vendor for project and work management, headquartered in Amsterdam with about 180 employees. It serves roughly 41,000 workspaces across Europe — from solo freelancers to 2,000-seat enterprises — at an average of about six seats and €86 per workspace per month. Growth is mostly self-serve (around 300 new workspace starts per week), with a small sales-assisted motion for enterprise migrations. Digitally, Quill is mature: full event telemetry, feature flags, experimentation infrastructure, and a strong design system. Culturally, it is feature-led: the roadmap reads like the company's biography — capabilities shipped, not transformations delivered.

The challenge

The funnel leaked at every stage, and the leaks were narrative failures, not feature gaps. Analysis of 12,000 workspace funnels (44 weeks of new-workspace cohorts, ~1.9M events) showed, per 100 starts: 52 completed setup, 34 sent an invite, 26 created a first project within 14 days, and 18 were weekly-active in week 4. The nine-step product tour had 4% completion. The first-project screen — the moment that should have been the climax — was a blank canvas with a blinking cursor; 57% of workspaces that created a project added one task or fewer. Support absorbed 38 tickets per 100 new workspaces, dominated by "how do I set this up for my team?" Onboarding-cohort NPS sat at +6, and 90-day new-workspace retention at 61%. The copy audit told the same story in words: 22 of 61 onboarding artifacts cast Quill as the hero ("Quill is the most powerful way to…"), and the week-2 lifecycle email led with guilt ("You haven't logged in").

Constraints

Budget €140,000 for the engagement plus an estimated €220,000 internal build; 24 weeks to a full-ring rollout with a measurement tail; no dark-pattern mechanics under Quill's published ethical-design policy (no fake scarcity, no streak debt, no guilt loops); GDPR for all telemetry processing (pseudonymized workspace IDs, EU residency); copy had to clear brand voice guidelines and CEFR B1 readability for non-native English speakers (78% of the user base); and every beat of the new narrative had to be shippable behind feature flags without a big-bang release.

Why service design

The failure was not in any screen but in the structure of the experience over time — which is exactly what journey-level design exists for (See: 09-mapping-experiences/04-customer-journey-maps.md). Donna Lichaw's argument in The User's Journey is the sharper diagnostic: organizations think in features while users experience products chronologically, as heroes pursuing a goal against obstacles, and experiences without an inciting incident feel unmotivated, without a climax feel tedious, and without resolution feel unfulfilling (See: 10-the-users-journey/01-why-story.md). Quill had all three defects at once. The remedy was not better tooltips but a re-storied service: hero, stakes, beats, mentor, reward, and return — then a blueprint and backlog that could produce it.

3. Approach: the AI-first service design process

The engagement followed the Double Diamond with Lichaw's narrative methodology as the spine: extract the story from research, map it, re-story it, then translate beats into a backlog and a measurement system. The designer ran every phase with named AI collaborators: Claude Code for story extraction, beat-map and copy drafting; OpenCode for the telemetry-to-beats pipeline and prototype; Codex for adversarial review of narrative coherence and manipulation risk; GitHub as the system of record where the narrative lives as versioned docs and every copy change passes a PR gate.

4. Phase-by-phase narrative

4.1 Research: the story hidden in the telemetry (weeks 1–5)

Goal. Understand why workspaces stall between signup and habit — as a story that breaks, not as a list of usability defects (See: 10-the-users-journey/05-storytelling-for-research-and-communication.md).

Method & SOP. Triangulated fieldwork with a narrative lens: 12,000 new-workspace funnels, 85 episodic interviews (24 new users, 22 admins, 20 lapsed users, 12 CS/support staff, 7 enterprise migration leads), 18 moderated usability sessions, and an audit of 61 onboarding artifacts.

AI execution. Interviews stayed human; everything downstream was agent-amplified. OpenCode mapped every instrumented event to a candidate beat and computed the beat-transition matrix — the quantitative shape of the current arc:

opencode run --agent build "Read telemetry/events-44wk.parquet (pseudonymized
workspace_ids). Map each event_type to one of [exposition, inciting, rising,
climax, falling, resolution] via telemetry/beat-map-rules.yaml. Compute per-100-
starts conversion at each beat boundary, median elapsed time between beats, and
the top 5 events preceding abandonment within each beat. Output
research/beat-transitions-baseline.csv plus a 60-row sample matching
data/activation_funnel_sample.csv schema, seed 42."

The key finding: the steepest drop sat where a climax should be — between "project created" and "project with real work in it" — while the invite beat arrived before any value existed to share. Claude Code processed transcripts into evidence items under the story-extraction SOP; each item carries participant code, beat, valence, and a verbatim quote verified by a second subagent. Result: 414 evidence items in research/evidence-register.jsonl (PR #6).

Human designer judgment. The designer oversampled lapsed users — the people who walked out of the story — and made one early framing call: the product did not need more education; it demanded effort before establishing stakes.

Artifact produced. Research plan, evidence register (414 items), beat-transition baseline, copy audit wall (61 artifacts, 22 vendor-hero, 9 guilt-framed).

4.2 Story extraction & storymapping: naming the dramatic question (weeks 5–9)

Goal. Convert evidence into a shared narrative frame the whole company can build against: hero, stakes, dramatic question, story spine, and a diagnosed current-state storymap (See: 10-the-users-journey/02-the-user-as-hero.md, 10-the-users-journey/04-storymapping-in-practice.md).

Method & SOP. Hero casting per persona, then candidate dramatic questions, then the seven-line Pixar story spine before any screens were discussed. From there the team ran Lichaw's six-step storymapping process: capture frames, group them under beats, annotate action/emotion/friction/product role, and diagnose plot holes. This happened in a 4-week workshop series (3 hours weekly, 8 participants) with the current-state journey map built in parallel from the same evidence.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted three candidate spines per persona from the evidence register, each beat required to cite evidence IDs. During workshops it acted as scribe, maintaining the digital twin in narrative/storymap-current.md. The spoken walkthrough in session 2 — a support lead narrating the journey in first person — surfaced three hidden plot holes within twenty minutes. Codex then challenged the dramatic questions:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read narrative/candidate-questions.md and
research/evidence-register.jsonl. For each candidate dramatic question: (1) verify
every cited evidence ID exists and says what is claimed, (2) argue for the
strongest competing framing of the same evidence, (3) flag any stakes claim that
no participant actually expressed. Be adversarial."

Codex killed one candidate and forced an evidence upgrade on the winner.

Human designer judgment. The designer chose the team-level question — "Will this team be genuinely productive in Quill within 14 days, despite scattered tools, skeptical teammates, and no time for training — so that the person who championed it looks capable, not reckless?" — because social risk appeared in 31 of 85 interviews. The designer also enforced Rule 1 when the PM proposed a vendor-hero spine: the product is the mentor, never the hero.

Artifacts produced. The story spine and dramatic question, the current-state storymap with nine plot holes (PH-01…PH-09), and the A1 journey map (Section 5).

The Quill setup-to-habit story spine (v1, PR #11) Once upon a time there was a team lead whose work lived in seventeen places. Every day, they chased status in chat, rebuilt plans in spreadsheets, and apologized in meetings. One day, they opened a Quill workspace and found a project already taking shape. Because of that, they shaped their first real project in minutes, with a guide beside them. Because of that, they invited the team to see the win — not to do chores. Until finally, the whole team could see its week, and shipped with less noise. And ever since then, Monday planning is a fifteen-minute ritual the team defends.

The nine plot holes, per Lichaw's diagnostic taxonomy (See: 10-the-users-journey/04-storymapping-in-practice.md): PH-01 unmotivated call (invite wall before any value exists to share); PH-02 exposition overload (9-step tour, 14-field setup, 4% completion); PH-03 false climax (confetti on "Workspace created!" — a system event, not a user accomplishment); PH-04 missing climax (first project = blank canvas); PH-05 endless slog (60-template gallery, decision fatigue); PH-06 abrupt exit (no falling action or hook after project creation); PH-07 vendor-as-hero copy (22 of 61 artifacts); PH-08 nag arc (guilt emails, 61% unsubscribe); PH-09 absent mentor at the highest-friction frame.

4.3 Narrative redesign: the re-storied experience (weeks 9–15)

Goal. Re-story the journey so the structure itself carries motivation: early climax, lean rising action, a mentor at the hardest moment, rewards for real wins, and a resolution that seeds the next loop (See: 10-the-users-journey/03-structuring-your-product.md).

Method & SOP. Aha-first backwards design: define the exact payoff (your real week, visible), keep only the strict preconditions, and defer admin work — invites, settings, billing — until after the climax. Ideation used HMW chains from the plot holes plus a 10-plus-10 session and NUF scoring, with concepts ranked on narrative leverage. The mentor moment was prototyped first as a 6-panel storyboard, then as a clickable flow.

AI execution. Claude Code drafted three copy variants per beat under the narrative copywriting SOP — 137 strings total — and authored all diagrams as versioned mermaid. OpenCode built the mentor-moment prototype from a one-paragraph brief, seeded with realistic project names and rosters. Codex ran the red-team pass for coherence, manipulation risk, and what the team called the cringe detector:

codex exec --skip-git-repo-check "Read narrative/copy-pr-set.md and
narrative/tone-guide.md. For every string: (1) does it cast the user as hero and
Quill as mentor (flag vendor-hero verbs), (2) does it state honest stakes only —
flag manufactured urgency, guilt, loss framing, fake social proof, (3) does it
celebrate a user accomplishment rather than a system event, (4) flag anything a
cynical user would screenshot and mock. Cite the string ID per finding."

Codex flagged 23 of 137 strings in round one; all were rewritten before human review. GitHub held the narrative as living docs — spine, beat-map, and copy — with support and CS sign-off plus tone and evidence checks in CI.

Human designer judgment. The designer made the ethics calls: celebrate only user accomplishments with social substance, never streaks or login counts; let users skip the mentor without punishment; and remove the tour despite engineering's instinct to keep it for a minority of users.

Artifacts produced. Future-state narrative journey map (A1b), story map as backlog, mentor-moment storyboard, the guided-first-project blueprint (A2), tone guide, 137-string copy PR set, leverage matrix.

4.4 Ship, pilot, and measure the story (weeks 15–24 + tail)

Goal. Prove the re-storied arc in production, behind flags, with measurement built on the beats themselves — and hand the narrative over as a living organizational artifact.

Method & SOP. Each beat became an epic, each frame a story with narrative acceptance criteria. Rollout ran in rings — 5% at week 15, 25% at week 19, 100% at week 22 — with kill-switch thresholds per ring. Measurement was story-led: the KPI tree instruments beat transitions, not just page events. The executive readout used the same structure as the research readout — protagonist, catalyst, struggle, breakdown, consequence, new resolution — enacted as a narrated walkthrough of the before and after prototype.

AI execution. OpenCode maintained the nightly beat-transition dashboard; Claude Code drafted each ring readout as a narrative PR; GitHub Actions linted every artifact before review. Two in-flight fixes came from the ring pilots: a skeleton render for plan generation above 3 seconds (F1), and a digest subject line rewritten out of the nag register.

Human designer judgment. The designer set the ring gates, rollback thresholds, and the final executive story.

Artifacts produced. 148-ticket backlog with beat IDs, ring readouts, activation-KPI dashboard, and the living-docs handover (narrative/ as the canonical product story).

Template 4 — Narrative copywriting prompt + tone guide

You are NARRATIVE-COPYWRITER for Quill. Input: one beat spec (beat role, hero,
dramatic question, evidence IDs, telemetry context). Draft exactly 3 variants of
every string in the beat. For each string output: string_id, beat_role, variant
text, user_verb (the verb the USER owns), evidence_ids, readability (CEFR).
HARD RULES (tone guide v1.2):
1. The user is the hero: user owns the verbs; Quill speaks as mentor in second
   person. BANNED: "Quill is proud", "we're excited", "revolutionary",
   "supercharge", "unlock your potential", vendor-first sentence subjects.
2. Honest stakes only: never manufacture urgency, scarcity, or loss. No fake
   countdowns, no "only today", no guilt ("you haven't logged in").
3. Celebrate user accomplishments, never system events. NOT "Workspace
   created!" but "Your week is visible." If the event means nothing to the
   user's goal, it gets no celebration.
4. No streaks, no debt mechanics, no loss framing. Milestones are cumulative
   and non-breakable ("4 rituals held").
5. Reading level CEFR B1; sentences under 18 words; no idioms that do not
   survive translation.
6. Every claim traces to an evidence ID; unverifiable claims are deleted, not
   softened.

6. Use cases

Use case A — Solo freelancer: the arc without a team

Scenario. Alba, 31, freelance brand designer in Porto, runs client projects alone. Baseline solo workspaces were the weakest segment: D14 activation 19%, W4 active 14% — the invite-centric arc literally did not apply to her.

What was designed. A solo variant of the spine: the invite beats collapse out, the mentor moment carries the whole arc, and the climax is a client-ready project plan inside 20 minutes. The reward beat becomes "send the client a read-only plan link" — an outward share that fits a freelancer's actual social world — and the return hook is the Monday plan review.

AI working session. Claude Code generated the solo spine variant from the 11 solo-participant evidence items and drafted the share-link copy; Codex red-teamed the variant and caught a subtle vendor-hero drift ("Quill makes you look professional") rewritten to the user's own verb ("Send clients a plan they can actually read"). OpenCode stubbed the read-only client view in the prototype overnight.

Outcome (illustrative). Solo D14 activation 19% → 44%; median time-to-first-project 11 days → 25 minutes; W4 active 14% → 29%; 22% of solo users shared a client plan link in week 1.

Use case B — Enterprise migration: the import as call to adventure

Scenario. Katarina's consultancy: 45 people, years of history in a legacy tool plus spreadsheets. The old arc asked migrants to abandon their history and start blank — the highest-stakes possible entry, treated as admin work.

What was designed. The CSV/tool import is reframed as the call to adventure, and its preview as an early climax: within 90 seconds of uploading, the admin sees their real projects alive in Quill — names, phases, owners — before committing to anything. The mentor moment then runs on their migrated project ("your real week, visible"), and the invite goes out carrying the migrated plan. A CS playbook (blueprint step S4) supports the first-Monday ritual for migration accounts.

AI working session. GitHub issues drove the build (23 of the 148 backlog tickets carry the E2-import label). Codex reviewed the import-preview flow adversarially and flagged a false-climax risk: the preview originally celebrated "Import complete!" (system event) rather than rendering the migrated week (user value) — the celebration moved to the plan render. OpenCode generated synthetic legacy exports of varying messiness so the 90-second claim was tested against realistic data, not demo CSVs.

Outcome (illustrative). Migration cohort: setup completion 58% → 81%; import preview used by 72% of migration starts; W4 active 22% → 39%; support tickets per migration 5.2 → 1.8; zero rollbacks to the legacy tool among ring-cohort migrations.

Use case C — Team of 8: the viral invite loop as nested stories

Scenario. Theo's startup: eight people, async culture, severe tool fatigue. The old flow asked the champion to invite teammates into an empty workspace — an unmotivated call that spent the champion's social capital on a chore.

What was designed. The invite moves to the falling action and becomes its own nested story: the inviter's resolution ("our week is visible") is the invitee's inciting incident. The invite carries the plan itself — invitees land inside a populated project with their name already on two tasks, so their origin story starts at a climax preview, not a blank room. Each invitee's completion of a first task feeds a quiet win back to the inviter (sidekick register, no leaderboard).

AI working session. Claude Code drafted the invite-copy variants; the winning frame ("Theo shared this week's plan with you — two tasks have your name on them") came from a support lead's workshop observation, not the agent. Codex red-teamed the loop for manipulation: it killed a proposed "your teammates are waiting" nudge (guilt framing) and a leaderboard concept (social pressure), both replaced by accomplishment-based updates. OpenCode's prototype seeded invites with realistic roster data so hallway tests felt like a real team, not lorem ipsum.

Outcome (illustrative). Invites per activated workspace 2.1 → 2.6; invite acceptance 31% → 36%; viral coefficient k 0.65 → 0.94; invite-sourced workspaces show W4 active 41% versus 31% for organic — the strongest habit formation in the dataset, because invitees inherit a story already in motion.

7. SOPs for AI — how service design SOPs are best used by agents

Encoded SOPs. Three SOPs were encoded for agent execution, each following the pattern method → numbered steps → output schema → acceptance criteria → review gate.

  1. Story-extraction SOP (research → narrative). Steps: episode segmentation → beat tagging (Exposition/Inciting/Rising/Crisis/Climax/Falling/Resolution) → wants/needs laddering extraction → tension-curve coding → candidate dramatic questions (exactly 3 per persona) → evidence attachment. The prompt template:
You are STORY-EXTRACTOR. Input: one pseudonymized interview transcript.
Work in five steps:
1. Segment the transcript into concrete past episodes (ignore hypotheticals).
2. Tag every utterance that marks a dramatic beat: exposition, inciting
   incident, rising action, crisis, climax, falling action, resolution.
3. Extract wants (surface goals) and needs (laddered emotional drivers) as
   separate fields — never merge them.
4. Code emotional valence per beat on -2..+2.
5. Propose nothing yet: output evidence items only.
For each item output EXACTLY: evidence_id EV-<transcript>-<seq> | participant_code
| role_type | beat | want_or_need | verbatim_quote | valence | source_lines.
RULES: quotes copy-paste exact; if unsure, drop the item; no invented stakes;
no paraphrase inside quote fields. JSONL only.

Acceptance: every quote verbatim-verifiable; every candidate dramatic question later built from these items cites at least 5 items from at least 3 participants. Gate: human framing session plus the Codex challenge pass.

  1. Narrative copywriting SOP (design). Template 4 is enforced in CI: tone lint, readability check, evidence attachment, and a Codex red-team run on any PR touching more than five strings. Gate: support and CS approval on every user-facing string.
  2. Beat-assignment telemetry SOP (measurement). beat-map-rules.yaml maps every event to exactly one beat; orphan events fail the pipeline, forcing the taxonomy to stay coherent. Gate: data review on any new event.

The meta-SOP. The engagement ran research → story extraction → storymap → re-story → translate → measure, with hard gates: no spine before evidence; no future-state before the spine is agreed; no backlog item without a beat ID; no string shipped without the tone gate; no ring expansion before beat KPIs hold for one full cohort.

Guardrails. Honest stakes only; quote verification on every synthesis PR; pseudonymized data and synthetic mocks in prompts; human sign-off on framing, ethics, copy, and ring gates.

Failure modes observed. Arc flattening (everything tagged rising action), cheesy heroics, fabricated drama from thin evidence, and manipulative drift toward streaks, countdowns, and guilt nudges. The mitigations were simple and structural: per-beat quotas, the quiet-mentor tone guide, participant-count thresholds for dramatic questions, and merge-blocking honest-stakes lint.

10. Lessons learned & pitfalls

  • The tour was not disliked — it was unread. Four percent completion is not an education problem; it is exposition overload (See: 10-the-users-journey/01-why-story.md).
  • The highest-leverage change was moving one screen. Deferring the invite wall past the climax cost little and moved three KPIs (See: 10-the-users-journey/04-storymapping-in-practice.md).
  • Agents flatten arcs. Without guardrails they tag everything as rising action, which destroys the diagnosis.
  • Agents drift manipulative by default. Streaks, countdowns, guilt nudges, and hype verbs are the statistical center of growth copy; the honest-stakes lint is not decoration, it is the product.
  • The spoken walkthrough found what dashboards hid. Twenty minutes of first-person narration surfaced three plot holes that 1.9M events had not made legible (See: 10-the-users-journey/06-workshop-and-application.md).
  • Confetti was the tell. Celebrating "Workspace created!" while the user's real goal sat nine days away revealed the whole structural problem in miniature.
  • Done differently next time: instrument beat events from day one, and put support into the research quota explicitly.
