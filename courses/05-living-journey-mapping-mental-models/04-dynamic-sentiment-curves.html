Module 05.4: Deriving Dynamic Sentiment Curves from Verbatim Quotes

Course: Living Journey Mapping & Mental Model Alignment · Level: Intermediate · Duration: 55 min · Format: Theory, Architecture, Code & Hands-on Lab

Learning Objectives

By completing this module, you will master:

  1. Theoretical Foundations: The emotional curve as Kalbach defines it (Mapping Experiences, ch. 4) — affective valence coded on a −5…+5 scale, plotted across phases, with steep drops annotated by trigger events and the deepest valleys treated as priority redesign targets.
  2. Agent Topology & Contracts: Why the toolchain stores valence as node fields (fields.emotion, gated non-empty, drawn from quotes) and provides no sentiment-calculation tool — and how to derive a curve view honestly on top of that substrate.
  3. Architecture & Schemas: A declared, reviewer-visible emotion→valence mapping applied to a real artifact's fields; why the closed kind schema rejects a made-up numeric field.
  4. Case Study Execution: Article 09 — the Albatross finding that sentiment bottomed during uninformed waiting, not at the cancellation, relocating the design intervention from compensation to instant rebooking notification.
  5. Adversarial Verification: The critic gate as the only thing standing between an honest curve and a decorative one.
  6. Hands-on Practice: Derive a sentiment view from a real artifact's fields and render it.

1. Theoretical Grounding & Literature Synthesis

The emotional curve, defined properly

Kalbach (ch. 4) gives the journey map its most scannable element: a continuous plotted trajectory of affective valence — peaks of delight and confidence against valleys of frustration and anxiety. The construction recipe is empirical, not aesthetic:

  1. Code qualitative transcripts and diary logs on a numerical valence scale (e.g., −5 acute distress to +5 peak delight).
  2. Average the scores per journey touchpoint.
  3. Plot the points as a continuous line across phases.
  4. Annotate steep drops with the specific trigger event that caused them.
  5. Prioritize the deepest valleys as redesign targets.

The curve's power is executive legibility — ch. 7's "10-second read" puts the overarching journey shape and its peaks and valleys in the top strip a sponsor scans first. Its danger is symmetric: a curve is the easiest layer of a map to fabricate, because a smooth line looks like data. Kalbach's discipline is that every plotted point must be codable back to source material, and This is Service Design Doing (ch. 2, the Sequential principle) frames why the shape matters: services are time-based rhythms, and emotional arcs — including peak-end dynamics, where the peak moment and the ending disproportionately define remembered experience — are design material, not reporting garnish.

Where curves go dishonest

Three failure modes recur in practice:

  • Inferred emotion. The mapper "knows" the user was frustrated and plots it. The quote layer says otherwise — or says nothing.
  • Smoothing. Averaging across respondents erases the bimodal reality (half delighted, half furious) into a fictitious "mildly satisfied" midpoint.
  • Hidden derivation. A sentiment score appears in a deck with no visible mapping from words to numbers, so nobody can falsify it.

Why there is no sentiment-calculation tool — and why that is the point

The ASD toolchain deliberately stores the raw material of the curve and nothing else: every journey stage carries fields.emotion — a string, required by the journey-map kind schema, gated non-empty by method acceptance, and (per the method instruction) "drawn from the quotes, never inferred." There is no sentiment tool in the 29-tool surface. The curve is a derived view: you read a real artifact's nodes, apply a declared emotion→valence mapping in front of the reviewer, and plot. If the derivation is challenged, the resolution path is not an argument about the algorithm — it is a walk down each node to its evidence[].quote. Stored truth: quote-backed fields. Derived view: the curve. Never confuse the layers.

2. Agentic Topology & Contracts

No new method is needed — the sentiment curve rides the same journey-from-transcripts contract from module 05.3. What changes is the lens each role applies:

Role Sentiment-specific duty Tools
researcher Capture quotes that carry affective language, not just actions; emotion-bearing lines are the curve's raw data sd_evidence_add, sd_evidence_query
mapper Set fields.emotion per stage from a cited quote; order stages so valence can be read over time (edges, relation next) sd_artifact_write, sd_artifact_get
critic (gate) Reject any emotion word not present in — or a fair paraphrase of — the cited quote; reject smoothing claims that contradict per-node fields sd_review

The emotion→valence mapping is not a tool call; it is a documented convention the team writes into the lesson of the review row (e.g. the fix or message text) so the derivation survives audit. Two methods already encode this: journey-from-transcripts requires emotion non-empty per node, and mental-model-alignment (05.2) requires it on both sides of a divergence — enabling two overlaid curves, customer vs frontline, from one artifact.

3. Schemas & Verified Execution

The schema boundary, verified

The journey-map kind schema is closed: stage, emotion, touchpoint, actor, pain, gain, opportunity, description, notes, tags. A write carrying fields.valence: -3 is rejected — artifact.nodes[0].fields.valence is not allowed by the closed kind schema. Valence therefore lives exactly where it should: in the derived view, computed from the gated emotion string, never smuggled into storage as an unevidenced number.

Verified derivation from a real artifact

From the live run (project meridian-night-bus-journey-sprint-d819e68a, artifact 4b5752a381c54de4a84ec53869ba53f2, status final after critic pass):

# nodes in edge order with their stored fields:
#   n1 pre-journey  emotion "anxious"     quote "I check the app three times before I leave work"
#   n2 journey      emotion "confused"    quote "I found out from another passenger, not from Meridian"
#   n3 recovery     emotion "frustrated"  quote "they re-read me the same website page" (+ repeat-call telemetry f91c67f7…)
#   n4 recovery     emotion "relieved"    quote "It just rebooks me."

Applying a declared mapping (anxious −2, confused −1, frustrated −3, relieved +3) yields the curve below — every point traceable to a stored field, every field traceable to a quote:

Reading the curve without lying

  • The valley is a claim about evidence, not aesthetics. n3 is deepest because its emotion word carries the strongest negative charge and it is the only node corroborated by telemetry (f91c67f7…: 41 of 60 evening helpline calls are re-calls). Convergent quote + telemetry is what makes a valley worth prioritizing.
  • The v1→v2 comparison is the honest "dynamic" part. v1 ended at frustrated (−3). v2 adds n4 (relieved, +3) — drawn from post-change quote 03d1bc24…. The curve moved because new evidence entered the store and the artifact was rewritten (module 05.6), not because a smoothing function ran.
  • What you may not claim: statistical significance, population-level sentiment, or precision beyond the mapping's declared granularity. Four quote-backed stages produce a shape to argue with, not a measurement.

Worked example: from a quote-valence table to a derived curve

Fixture — eight coded quotes across four stages (Meridian evening rider sample, sd_evidence_query export). Declared mapping, written down before coding: delighted +4, relieved +3, calm +1, uneasy −1, anxious −2, frustrated −3, distressed −4.

stage evidence id prefix quote (trimmed) asserted fields.emotion coded valence
n1 pre-journey dd7a0a0c… "I check the app three times before I leave work" anxious −2
n1 pre-journey b60e2f19… "If the app says coming, I still leave five minutes early" uneasy −1
n2 journey cf7764fd… "I found out from another passenger, not from Meridian" frustrated −3
n2 journey 2a94d8e6… "The stranger next to me knew more than the sign did" frustrated −3
n3 recovery 433237cb… "they re-read me the same website page" frustrated −3
n3 recovery f91c67f7… (telemetry: 41 of 60 evening calls are re-calls) distressed −4
n4 resolution 03d1bc24… "It just rebooks me." relieved +3
n4 resolution 77c1a3d8… "First time I didn't have to chase anyone" delighted +4

Step-by-step derivation. Per stage, average the coded valences: n1 = (−2−1)/2 = −1.5; n2 = −3.0; n3 = (−3−4)/2 = −3.5; n4 = (+3+4)/2 = +3.5. Plot in edge order and annotate the n2→n3 drop with its trigger (cancellation SMS with no rebooking offer):

Asserted vs derived — the distinction that keeps the curve honest. The emotion string on each node is asserted: it must appear in, or fairly paraphrase, the cited quote, and the critic gate enforces that. The valence number is derived: it is computed by the declared mapping, lives in the review row or the presentation layer, and is rejected by the closed schema if anyone tries to store it. n3 shows why both layers matter: its asserted word (distressed, from telemetry) is stronger than any quote word, and the derived average (−3.5) reflects it — but had the two n3 codings been −4 and +2, the mean of −1 would be a fiction; report the spread, not just the mean.

Interpretation. The curve says sentiment bottoms during the wait, not at the cancellation — and every point can be walked back to a quote id. That walk-back is the entire claim to truth: remove the declared mapping or the evidence column and what remains is decoration.

4. Case Study Lens: Albatross Airways (Article 09)

Synthetic illustration — not a production log. Albatross Airways is a fictional composite client from articles/09/; figures are illustrative.

The Albatross disruption map's decisive finding came from reading the curve honestly. Internal folklore located the emotional low point at the cancellation announcement; the evidence-per-row map showed sentiment bottoming during the 90 minutes of silence afterward — passengers standing in gate lines with no rebooking information. The curve relocated the design intervention from monetary compensation to instant mobile rebooking notification.

Two lessons transfer directly:

  1. The deepest valley was invisible to intuition. Three improvement programmes had aimed at the wrong moment of the journey because nobody had plotted quote-coded valence across stages. The curve's job is precisely to contradict confident folklore.
  2. The annotation did the work. Kalbach's step 4 — annotate steep drops with the specific trigger event — is what turned "passengers feel bad" into "passengers feel worst while uninformed, so fix information latency." An unannotated curve is a mood; an annotated curve is a backlog.

$CLI artifact-write --projectId <pid> --methodId journey-from-transcripts \
  --title "valence smuggle" --nodes '[{"id":"n1","label":"x","evidence":[…],
  "fields":{"emotion":"sad","touchpoint":"app","valence":-3}}]'
# → rejected: "artifact.nodes[0].fields.valence is not allowed by the closed kind schema" (exit 1)

# 4. Critic challenge drill: have a reviewer pick the curve's lowest point and
#    demand the quote chain: node → evidenceId → verbatim line.

Verification checklist

  • Every plotted point names its evidenceId and quote.
  • The emotion→valence mapping is written down before plotting and attached to the review row.
  • The valence smuggle write was rejected by the closed kind schema.
  • The curve's deepest valley matches the node with the strongest negative emotion word — not the one that "feels" worst.

Key Takeaways

  • The emotional curve is coded, averaged, plotted, annotated, prioritized — Kalbach's five steps, each one falsifiable.
  • Valence lives in derived views; the store holds quote-backed emotion strings — the closed schema rejects numeric smuggling by construction.
  • There is no sentiment tool in the 29-tool surface, deliberately: the critic gate plus the quote chain is the entire honesty mechanism.
  • A curve moves when evidence moves — new quotes, a rewritten artifact version, a re-run gate. That is the only "dynamic" a living map needs.
