Module 06.3: Lines of Visibility, Interaction & Internal Penetration

Learning Objectives

1. Theoretical Grounding & Literature Synthesis

The three canonical lines

Shostack's 1982/1984 formulation established the structural divisions that Bitner, Ostrom & Morgan (2008) codified (books/07-designing-for-service-key-issues/02-service-design-and-management.md; books/09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md):

  1. Line of interaction — separates customer actions (above) from the provider's frontstage (below). Every vertical crossing is a direct touchpoint: a moment of truth in Normann's sense, where quality is realized or destroyed (books/07/02). Counting crossings counts touchpoints; a stage of the journey with no crossing is a stage where the organization is absent while the customer still acts — a service gap by construction.
  2. Line of visibility — separates frontstage (what the customer sees) from backstage (what they don't). This is Shostack's masterstroke: it converts "how much should the customer see?" into an explicit, priced design decision. Kalbach's summary is blunt: "Experience failures visible to customers almost always originate below this line" (books/09/05). The line is also bidirectional policy: steps pushed above it (status trackers, open-kitchen repair scheduling) buy trust at the cost of exposing mess; steps pushed below it buy smoothness at the cost of opacity.
  3. Line of internal interaction — separates backstage employees from support processes and systems. Crossings here are system dependencies: every one is an interface contract, an SLA, a failure mode with a vendor's name on it. Older operations literature in the Shostack lineage calls this deepest boundary the line of (order) penetration — the depth to which the customer's specific order drives activity, versus standardized system behaviour that would run identically for anyone. Penetration depth is a strategic choice: deep penetration means customization; shallow means industrialized standardization.

Why lines, not lanes, carry the governance

Lanes tell you who acts; lines tell you what changes hands and under whose rules when action crosses. The three lines have three different governors:

Line What crosses Governor Cost of a bad crossing
Interaction Customer effort ↔ provider response Experience/interaction design A moment of truth fumbled in public
Visibility Information, evidence, assurance Visibility policy / service promise Anxiety (hidden wait) or exposure (shown mess)
Internal interaction (penetration) Data, job state, liability System ownership / vendor SLA Failure modes no frontline apology can fix

The Fernwood blueprint makes this concrete: the tenant's anxious phone call (ca2) is an interaction-line crossing caused by a visibility-line decision (repair status kept backstage) caused by an internal-interaction failure (the ticketing system has no status API). One symptom, three lines, three different owners. A blueprint that draws the lanes but not the lines can locate the pain but never route the fix.

The critical encoding decision: lines live on edges

A line is not a place; it is a property of a crossing. Two nodes sitting in adjacent lanes mean nothing until an edge connects them — and that edge is where the demarcation is real. So the service-blueprint schema encodes demarcation on edges: every edge carries a required relation and free-form fields.label, and the house convention (taught by the presenter step of the method contract) is that a lane-crossing edge's label names the line it crosses. That single convention turns "the line of visibility" from a horizontal rule drawn in marker into a queryable set: every edge whose endpoints straddle frontstage and backstage lanes. Diagnostics in module 06.7 exploit exactly this.

critic (gate) Vetoes crossings that are unlabelled, mislabelled (an edge labelled visibility whose endpoints are both frontstage), or that cross two lines at once with no intermediate node sd_review defect rows

3. Schemas & Verified Execution

The edge schema (verified via schema-get)

"edge": {
  "policy": "optional",
  "endpoints": { "from": "node", "to": "node" },
  "fields": { "weight": {"type":"number","required":false}, "label": {"type":"string","required":false} }
}

And from @asd/core (suite/packages/core/src/types.ts):

export type ArtifactEdge = {
  readonly id: string
  readonly from: string
  readonly to: string
  readonly relation: string     // REQUIRED — e.g. "triggers", "hands-to", "depends-on"
  readonly fields: Readonly<Record<string, unknown>>  // house convention: label names the line crossed
}

Verified live receipts — edges fail closed when malformed

From this course's verification run (real CLI output):

# edge without id:
asd artifact-write: edges[0].id must be a non-empty string

# edge without relation:
asd artifact-write: edges[0].relation must be a non-empty string

# well-formed crossing (accepted in blueprint 4d64fbd3395440efa44c17363e5b8f10):
{"id":"b2","from":"fs1","to":"bs1","relation":"hands-to",
 "fields":{"label":"crosses line of visibility"}}

The malformed-edge rejections matter pedagogically: the toolchain refuses to let a crossing exist anonymously. An edge must say what kind of relationship it is (relation) before the critic will even consider whether its line label is right.

4. Case Study Lens: Article 11 — Visibility as Policy at Fernwood

Synthetic illustration — not a production log.

Article 11's diagnostic pays off at the lines, not the lanes:

  • The three-week status-chasing wait (W on the customer lane) traced to a visibility decision nobody remembered making: repair status lived below the line because the ticketing system couldn't expose it — an internal-interaction constraint silently became a visibility policy.
  • The future-state blueprint's headline change was moving exactly one data element — job status — above the line via SMS push. Note the shape of that fix: no frontstage behaviour changed; a crossing was re-labelled. That is why lines deserve first-class encoding.
  • The article's fail-point register marks each F with the line whose crossing failed, which is what let Phase 3 route fixes to three different owners (contact-centre process, scheduling policy, ticketing vendor) instead of one undifferentiated "ops" bucket.

When you read any blueprint — including ones produced in this toolchain — audit it line by line: for each of the three lines, list the crossings, then ask who decided this boundary sits here, and what evidence supports it?

5. Hands-On Lab

export ASD_WORKSPACE=/tmp/asd-lab06
CLI="node suite/packages/runtime/lib/cli.mjs"

# 1. Take your five-lane draft from module 06.2's lab. Add crossings with line labels:
$CLI artifact-write --projectId <pid> --artifactId <aid> --methodId service-blueprinting \
  --title "Five lanes, minimal" --nodes '<same nodes as before>' --edges '[
   {"id":"x1","from":"c1","to":"f1","relation":"triggers","fields":{"label":"crosses line of interaction"}},
   {"id":"x2","from":"f1","to":"b1","relation":"hands-to","fields":{"label":"crosses line of visibility"}},
   {"id":"x3","from":"b1","to":"s1","relation":"depends-on","fields":{"label":"crosses line of internal interaction"}}
]'

# 2. Deliberate fail-closed demo — an anonymous crossing
$CLI artifact-write --projectId <pid> --methodId service-blueprinting --title "Bad edge demo" \
  --nodes '<4 valid nodes>' --edges '[{"from":"c1","to":"f1"}]'
# EXPECT: edges[0].id must be a non-empty string  (then try id without relation → rejected again)

# 3. Audit your own blueprint line by line: list crossings per line and name each governor.

Verification checklist

  • Every lane-crossing edge in your blueprint names its line in fields.label.
  • The anonymous-edge write was rejected twice (missing id, then missing relation).
  • You can name the governor (interaction design / visibility policy / system ownership) of every crossing.
  • You found at least one stage where the customer acts with zero interaction crossings — and can say whether that is a gap or a deliberate self-service design.

Key Takeaways

  • Interaction, visibility, internal interaction — three lines, three governors, three distinct cost models. The penetration boundary marks how deep a specific customer's order drives otherwise-standard systems.
  • Visible failures originate below the line of visibility — but their cause usually sits at an internal-interaction crossing. Trace symptoms across two lines, not one.
  • Lines are encoded on edges (relation + fields.label), making demarcations queryable data rather than drawing conventions — and malformed crossings fail closed at write time.
  • Moving an element across the line of visibility is a design decision with a price; the blueprint's job is to make that price explicit before launch, not after the complaints.
