1. Theoretical Grounding & Literature Synthesis

What the five lanes are — and why five

The canonical blueprint decomposes a service into five horizontal bands, stacked from the customer's skin outward to the organization's core (Bitner, Ostrom & Morgan 2008, as synthesized in books/09-mapping-experiences/05-service-blueprints.md; Grönroos's servuction-lineage treatment in books/19-service-management-and-marketing/07-designing-and-managing-the-service-process.md):

  1. Physical evidence — the tangible artifacts the customer encounters: the SMS booking reference, the contractor's ID badge, the printed receipt. Booms & Bitner added Physical Evidence to the marketing mix precisely because services are intangible and customers read quality off artifacts (books/19/08). Place it first because it is what the customer can literally hold.
  2. Customer actions — every step the customer performs: reporting the leak, waiting, phoning for status, admitting the plumber. The blueprint is customer-anchored: the chronological spine is the customer's sequence, and everything below exists to serve it.
  3. Frontstage (onstage) contact actions — what contact personnel and interfaces do in view of the customer: the agent triaging on the phone, the plumber at the door. Grönroos calls this the zone where functional quality ("how" it is delivered) is judged (books/07-designing-for-service-key-issues/02-service-design-and-management.md).
  4. Backstage contact actions — what the same people do out of view: re-keying details into the scheduler, preparing the job sheet, ordering parts. Shostack's brokerage case (books/02/01, books/07/02) proved this lane decides satisfaction while remaining invisible.
  5. Support processes — the systems and third parties nobody in the frontline fully controls: the policy engine applying the 24-hour/20-day clocks, the ticketing system, the contractor's own dispatch software. Crossings into this lane are the line of internal interaction (module 06.3).

Why exactly five? Because each additional lane must earn a demarcation line with a distinct governance meaning. Customer↔frontstage is governed by interaction design; frontstage↔backstage by visibility policy; backstage↔support by system ownership. A lane that doesn't change who governs what is a formatting choice, not a lane. (The proposed sixth lane survives this test only when it carries genuinely different orchestration semantics — the subject of module 06.4.)

Lane discipline failure modes

  • Lane collapse: teams merge backstage into frontstage ("it's all staff work") and lose the ability to price visibility decisions. Making a backstage step visible is a design move with a cost — the merged view hides it.
  • Placeholder lanes: a lane row titled "TBD" or "systems stuff" is Shostack's undrawn service returning through the back door. This is why the service-blueprinting contract's critic step reads, verbatim: "Reject any lane left as a placeholder rather than a named process step."
  • Evidence-free lanes: a backstage lane drawn from org-chart assumption rather than observation. The hospital discharge case (books/09/05) only worked because someone watched pharmacy, ward, billing and transport; the toolchain encodes that as min-evidence-per-node: 1.

2. Agentic Topology & Role Contracts

Role Lane responsibility Bound tools Acceptance rule it serves
mapper (owner) Authors nodes in all five lanes; sets fields.lane, actor, stage sd_evidence_query, sd_artifact_write min-nodes: 4, field-non-empty: lane
presenter Annotates demarcations; marks W wait points on customer/frontstage nodes sd_artifact_get, sd_artifact_write Edge labels naming the line each crossing violates
critic (gate) Verbatim: "Reject any lane left as a placeholder rather than a named process step." sd_review with defect rows (fix mandatory) forbid-field-values: lane ∉ {unknown, tbd, n/a}

The lane rules are enforced twice, at different layers: deterministically at artifact-write time by the acceptance engine (accepted:false drafts cannot pass factcheck), and adversarially at review time by the critic (who can veto legal-but-wrong lane assignments, e.g. a step the evidence shows is backstage being drawn frontstage).

Positive case — the 9-node blueprint from the swimlane above:

$CLI artifact-write --projectId fernwood-repairs-blueprint-sprint-7cee33e4 \
  --methodId service-blueprinting --title "Fernwood repairs reporting blueprint (current state)" \
  --nodes '[ pe1, ca1, ca2, fs1, fs2, bs1, bs2, sp1, sp2 — each with fields.lane + one evidenceId ]' \
  --edges '[ b1…b7 with id/from/to/relation, labels naming the line each crossing violates ]'
# → {"accepted":true,"status":"draft","artifact":{"id":"4d64fbd3395440efa44c17363e5b8f10",
#    "kind":"service-blueprint","methodId":"service-blueprinting", …}}

accepted:true means the deterministic lane rules passed. status:"draft" means the critic gate has not. The two verdicts are deliberately separate: schema truth is machine-checkable; lane correctness needs an adversary.

4. Case Study Lens: Article 11 — Fernwood's Five Lanes Under Load

Synthetic illustration — not a production log. Article 11 is a synthetic composite from this repo's articles/ corpus.

Article 11's current-state blueprint (artifact A1b) is a textbook five-lane discipline case:

  • The physical evidence lane is thin by design — one SMS and one paper job sheet — and that thinness is itself the diagnostic: tenants judge a 20-day process by two artifacts.
  • The customer actions lane carries the article's emotional payload (the three-week status-chasing phone calls) — journey symptoms anchored to evidence int-* quotes.
  • The frontstage/backstage split exposes the re-keying step: the same agent the customer sees (frontstage triage) disappears below the line to duplicate data entry (backstage), which is where the triage note dies before reaching the depot.
  • The support lane holds the policy engine and the ticketing system — the two systems whose 24h/20-day clocks and missing status API manufacture the wait the customer feels.

The article's fail-point register works because every F row names its lane; a register of "problems" without lane attribution could not have assigned the fixes to contact-centre process redesign vs. ticketing-system procurement. Lane discipline is accountability routing.

5. Hands-On Lab

# EXPECT: {"accepted":false, check.failures includes field-non-empty on lane}

Verification checklist

  • schema-get shows lane as required under a closed field policy.
  • Your five-lane write returned accepted:true, status:draft.
  • The empty-lane write returned accepted:false with a field-non-empty failure naming the node.
  • You can state which demarcation line each of your edges crosses, and who governs it.

Key Takeaways

  • Five lanes, not four or six, because each boundary has a distinct governor: interaction design (customer↔frontstage), visibility policy (frontstage↔backstage), system ownership (backstage↔support).
  • fields.lane is required and placeholder values are rejected — by the acceptance engine at write time and by the critic at review time. Two layers, two failure classes.
  • Lane assignment is accountability routing. A fail point without a lane cannot be assigned an owner; that is why the placeholder veto exists.
  • W and F marks are data (fields.marker), not highlighter ink — diagnostics in module 06.7 consume them mechanically.
